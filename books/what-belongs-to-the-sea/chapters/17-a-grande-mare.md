At ten past nine, Téo called from the tower.

"Buoy seven's back," he said. "The Department put in a new one at the end of the year. It's giving four
meters ten."

Joana was sitting on the plank floor of the circle platform, in the middle of the Market canal, her back
against one of the bamboo poles of the tarp roof and the device at her ear. The rain was hitting the tarp
sideways. The wind was coming from the southeast, like the other time, and bringing the smell of salt and
seaweed, and the whole platform rocked slowly, one long breath, with every swell that came in at the mouth
of the canal.

"And the others?"

"Four and a half at twelve kilometers." A pause. She could hear the hum of the control room behind his
voice, the air conditioning, the low roar of the building in the wind. She knew the sound by heart. "Same
as last year, Joana. The table gives ten centimeters more. High tide is at three minutes past midnight."

"I know."

"I know you know." His voice changed. "I'm looking at the map. The model's forecast paints all of Lowtown
pale blue. The model thinks you're all going to be under water at midnight."

"The model knows about water," said Joana.

"It doesn't know about rafts," said Téo. She could hear that he was almost laughing. "Keep your device on.
I'll call every fifteen minutes."

---

The circle platform was full. Not of people. Of people and things.

They had decided it at the previous week's circle: on the night of the big tide, whoever lived on the
smaller platforms, the ones at the mouth of the canal and the ones on Quay Street, would go to the bigger
ones. The circle's, the Ropemakers boys', Jairo's, Mrs. Célia's. The small platforms stayed empty, moored,
with slack, rising on their own on the posts. If one broke loose, it would break loose empty.

It was Benedito's idea. In the village, he had said, when a big surge comes, we don't stay in the canoe. We
stay on the sand, watching the canoe.

So the circle platform had sixty people on it, sitting and lying on the plank floor, under the tarp, on
mattresses and mats and in hammocks tied to the bamboo poles. Children sleeping. Old people awake. Mrs.
Célia with Clara in her lap, Clara thirteen now and too big for a lap, and allowing it. Mr. Lauro, wrapped
in a blanket, looking toward the mouth of the canal, where his platform, the one with twenty-two drums and
a porch bigger than the room, was moored on its own. A camp stove with a kettle. A crank radio. Mrs. Ilda in
a beach chair, in the middle, in a shawl, awake.

Raul was sitting on the edge, outside the tarp, in a slicker, legs over the water. He had stayed. Nobody had
asked him to. Nobody had sent him away.

Davi wasn't there. He was in Amaro's boat, tied to the post of Jairo's platform, fifty meters away, with
Jairo and two boys from Ropemakers. It was the rescue boat. That had been decided too.

Nilo wasn't there. He was at the boatyard. In the loft, with a headlamp and the blue notebook, watching the
iron column.

---

At half past ten, the first wave went over the old wall.

Joana didn't see it. She knew from the radio, which crackled in Mrs. Ilda's hand and said, in the voice of
Mrs. Lúcia from Ropemakers, who was on Jairo's platform, the one nearest Quay Street: *First one's over. Wet
the empty porch at the end. Weak.*

"How weak?" asked Joana, taking the radio.

*Weak. Last year the water came off the crest like a waterfall. Now it comes down like rain off a gutter.*

Joana closed her eyes. She did the math. She had done it before, a thousand times, over the last few weeks,
at the table in the village net shed, with Benedito and Taís watching. At high tide, the water over the
oyster cages was deep, two and a half meters, three. A submerged barrier at that depth didn't break a big
wave. It broke a piece of it. A fifth, a quarter, maybe. And the oyster shell on the face of the old wall,
rough, full of cracks and ledges, took another piece when the wave climbed it. The wave reached the crest
tired.

It wasn't enough to hold it back. It had never been for holding it back.

It was enough for it to come down like rain off a gutter.

At a quarter to eleven, Téo called.

"Overtopping on Quay Street," he said. "The channel on the wall is reading eight liters per meter."

"Last year, at this time?"

She heard him move something on the screen.

"Twenty-two."

---

At twenty past eleven, the water in the canal was higher than Joana had ever seen it.

The circle platform was at the top of its posts. She looked at the steel rings Edson had welded. They were
a hand-span and a half from the tops of the eight-meter posts. The math said they would be one hand-span
away. She had done the math with the tide from the table and the previous year's surge. A hand-span and a
half. There was some to spare.

All around, on the canal, the other platforms were at the top as well. The street that breathed had its
chest full. The bridges stretched between one house and another, their ropes taut. The water smooth beneath
them, brown, almost black, with the rain punching holes in it.

And every so often, a swell came in at the mouth of the canal.

Joana watched the mouth of the canal the whole time. Gate Two lying down, invisible under the water, the
oysters covering the orange steel. In front of it, on the sea side, the cages, invisible too. On the bank,
on both sides of the mouth, Benedito's sticks, the first ones, five weeks old, which were now little plants
with four, five leaves and a root beginning to open like a fan in the mud. Under water too. Doing almost
nothing.

Almost nothing.

The swell that came in at the mouth was a hand-span high. She could see it in the light on the platform at
the end. It lifted Mr. Lauro's empty platform a hand-span, and passed, and lifted the next one, and the next,
less, and when it reached the circle platform it was half a hand-span, and when it passed beneath it the
plank floor rose and fell like a sleeping chest.

The year before, a ramp of water had come in there.

The radio crackled in Mrs. Ilda's hand. Nilo's voice, from the boatyard.

*The water's at mark seven on the column. A hand-span above last year. But inside the shed it isn't rising
from waves. It's rising from the tide. Smooth. You can see the mark.*

Mrs. Ilda looked at Joana.

"Is that good?" she asked.

"It's what we wanted," said Joana.

---

At ten to midnight, Mr. Lauro's platform broke loose.

Joana saw it because she was watching. One of the steel rings, the one on the canal-side post, which Edson
had welded in the winter, in the first weeks, before he knew how to weld a ring onto a platform, opened at
the weld. She saw the snap in the light, a quick flash of metal. The platform, held now only by the inner
post, swung in the current, slowly, like a door, and hit the bridge that joined it to the next platform,
and the bridge rope broke.

Mr. Lauro got up from his blanket.

"My house."

"It's empty, Mr. Lauro," someone said.

The platform was swinging around the inner post, the porch bigger than the room turning toward the middle
of the canal, and the current pulling. If the other ring opened, it would go. Down the canal, into the other
platforms. Twenty-two drums and a plank room slamming into houses full of people.

Joana took the radio.

"Davi."

*I see it*, said Davi's voice. *On my way.*

Amaro's boat came out from behind Jairo's platform with a light in the bow. Davi at the oars. Jairo in the
bow, standing, with a coil of rope on his shoulder. Joana watched her son row. He didn't fight the water. He
waited for the swell to pass under the boat, and when it had passed he gave two strokes, short ones, and let
the current do the rest. The boat reached Mr. Lauro's platform side-on, slowly, just as it swung round and
offered up the corner of the porch. Jairo jumped onto the porch with the rope. He passed the rope around a
bamboo pole of the frame, took two turns, and threw the end to Davi. Davi tied it to the thwart of the boat.

Then Davi rowed backward. Against the current. Slowly. The platform stopped swinging.

Jairo took the other end of the rope to the nearest post, the one belonging to the next platform, and took
three turns, and a knot Joana didn't know, and which she learned afterward was her father's knot, the one for
tying a hull on a slipway, which Davi had taught everyone at a circle after the reef.

Mr. Lauro's platform stayed put. Crooked. Whole.

Mr. Lauro sat back down on his blanket.

"My house," he said again, lower.

---

Three minutes past midnight came and went. Joana didn't look at the clock. She knew because Téo called.

"High tide," he said. "Buoy seven's giving four thirty. The channel on Quay Street peaked at eleven liters."

"Last year?"

"Forty-two." He was quiet for a while. "Joana, I'm looking at the camera on Two. The one that's left, on the
post on the bank. You can't see anything. Just water. But the water's going over the gate..." He searched
for the word. "Gently. The water's going over gently."

She didn't answer.

"The map says you're all under water," he said.

"We're on top of it."

The radio crackled in Mrs. Ilda's hand. A voice Joana didn't recognize at first. Hoarse. From far off.
Benedito, from the village, on the net shed's radio.

*In the village the water got to the knee. Same as last year. It didn't come up any more. It's going out
through the engineer's hole. We can see it from here. It's going out like a river.*

The engineer's hole. The embankment across the creek that she had broken with the excavator, on the night of
the cofferdam, and which the village had widened afterward, with shovel and hoe, and lined with stone, and
planted with mangrove along its banks. The creek's new mouth.

"Nobody in the water?" asked Joana.

*Nobody. Everybody's in the net shed. Taís's grandma is complaining about the cold.*

---

The tide turned at twenty to one.

Joana felt it in her stomach before anything else. The circle platform, which had stood still at the top of
its posts, motionless, for almost an hour, gave a thick sigh of wood, and began to come down. A finger. Then
another. The steel rings sliding down the posts, with a low creak, metal on metal, which had been the sound
of sleep in Lowtown for nine months.

All around, the other platforms began to come down as well. All together. Slowly. The street that breathed,
breathing out.

Mrs. Ilda, in her beach chair, closed her eyes.

Nobody said anything. The children went on sleeping. Clara had fallen asleep in her mother's lap, her mouth
open. Mr. Lauro was watching his platform, crooked, held by Jairo's rope, coming down with the others. Raul,
on the edge, outside the tarp, had his head down, and Joana didn't know whether he was asleep.

She took the radio.

"Nilo."

*I'm here.*

"Write it down."

*I already did.* A pause. *Mark seven and a finger. The highest of all. The highest since your father painted
it.*

"And the wave?"

*There wasn't any wave. There was tide.*

She put the radio down on the floor.

---

Davi came back at two in the morning. He brought Amaro's boat up against the post of the circle platform,
and climbed the rope ladder, soaked through, the beaded bracelet on his wrist and his hands raw from the
rope. He sat beside her, against the bamboo pole. He didn't say anything. Neither did she.

They stayed there.

At half past six, with the light coming, the tide low, and Lowtown mud again, shining gray under a clear sky,
Téo called for the last time.

"The Civil Defense bulletin is out," he said. "Upper city, nothing. Lowtown, nothing. The Shallows, nothing.
One platform broke loose, recovered. One man with a cut on his hand. One tarp roof torn off on Quay Street,
on an empty house."

He stopped.

"Nobody, Joana."

She looked at the canal. The thirty-one platforms, resting on the mud, on their wooden skids, level, side by
side, on both banks, with the morning light striking the zinc sheets and the tarps and the bamboo. Mr.
Lauro's, crooked, whole. People coming out of the big platforms, slowly, over the bridges resting on the mud,
going back to their own. A child running. The smell of coffee from someone's camp stove.

Far down, at the mouth of the canal, Gate Two was showing again, with the tide out. Crooked. The curve of
steel covered in oysters, gray and white, shining wet.

A year before, she had spent the whole night watching a map, on a screen, in a tower. Street by street turning
blue.

She didn't need any map this time.

"Nobody," she said.
