At ten past nine, Téo called from the tower.

"Buoy seven's back," he said. "The Department put in a new one at the end of the year. It's reading four
point one meters."

Joana was sitting on the plank floor of the circle platform, in the middle of the Market canal, her back
against one of the bamboo poles of the tarp roof and the device at her ear. The rain was hitting the tarp
sideways. The wind was coming from the southeast, like the other time, and bringing the smell of salt and
seaweed, and the whole platform rocked slowly, in one long breath, with every swell that came in at the
mouth of the canal.

"And the others?"

"Four and a half at twelve kilometers." A pause. She could hear the hum of the control room behind his
voice, the air conditioning, the low roar of the building in the wind. She knew the sound by heart. "Same
as last year, Joana. The tide table puts it ten centimeters higher. High tide is at three minutes past
midnight."

"I know."

"I know you know." His voice changed. "I'm looking at the map. The model's forecast paints all of Lowtown
pale blue. The model thinks you're all going to be under water at midnight."

"The model knows about water," said Joana.

"It doesn't know about rafts," said Téo. She could hear that he was almost laughing. "Keep your device on.
I'll call every fifteen minutes."

---

The circle platform was full. Not of people. Of people and things.

They had decided it at the previous week's circle: on the night of the great tide, whoever lived on the
smaller platforms, the ones at the mouth of the canal and the ones on Quay Street, would go to the bigger
ones. The circle's, the Ropemakers boys', Jairo's, Mrs. Célia's. The small platforms stayed empty, moored,
with slack, rising on their own on the posts. If one broke loose, it would break loose empty.

It was Benedito's rule. In the village, when a big surge came, nobody slept in the canoe: you pulled it
up, tied it, and slept on land. And the rescue boat went out for people, not for houses.

"An empty house that breaks loose, let it go," he had said at the circle. "A house you build again."

So the circle platform, on its eighty drums and the last of the foam panels, had sixty people on it,
sitting and lying on the plank floor, under the tarp, on mattresses and mats and in hammocks tied to the
bamboo poles. Children sleeping. Old people awake. Mrs. Célia with Clara in her lap, Clara thirteen now
and too big for a lap, and allowing it. Mr. Lauro, wrapped in a blanket, looking toward the mouth of the
canal, where his platform, the one with twenty-two drums and a porch bigger than the room, was moored on
its own. A camp stove with a kettle, in its box of sand. A crank radio. Mrs. Ilda in a beach chair, in the
middle, in a shawl, awake.

Raul was sitting on the edge, outside the tarp, in a slicker, legs over the water. He had stayed. Nobody
had asked him to. Nobody had sent him away.

Davi wasn't there. He was on Jairo's platform, the seventh on the canal, with Amaro's boat tied to the
post, with Jairo and two boys from Ropemakers. It was the rescue boat. That had been decided too.

Nilo wasn't there. He was at the boatyard. In the loft, with a headlamp and the blue notebook, watching
the iron column.

---

At half past ten, the first wave went over the old wall.

Joana didn't see it. She knew from the radio, which crackled in Mrs. Ilda's hand and said, in the voice of
Mrs. Lúcia from Ropemakers, who was on Jairo's platform, where you could see Quay Street side-on: *First
one's over. Wet the empty porch at the end. Weak.*

"How weak?" asked Joana, taking the radio.

*Weak. Last year the water came off the crest like a waterfall. Now it comes down like rain off a gutter.*

Joana closed her eyes. She did the math. She had done it before, a thousand times, over the last few
weeks, at the table in the village net shed, with Benedito and Taís watching. At high tide, the water
over the oyster cages was deep, two and a half meters, three. A submerged barrier at that depth didn't
break a big wave. It broke a piece of it. A fifth, a quarter, maybe. And the oyster shell on the face of
the old wall, rough, full of cracks and ledges, took another piece when the wave climbed it.

It wasn't enough to hold it back. It had never been for holding it back.

It was enough for it to come down like rain off a gutter.

At a quarter to eleven, Téo called.

"Overtopping on Quay Street," he said. "The channel on the wall is reading eight liters per meter."

"Last year, at this time?"

She heard him move something on the screen.

"Twenty-two."

---

At twenty past eleven, the water in the canal was higher than Joana had ever seen it.

The circle platform was up high, on the piers of the old footbridge. All around, on the posts, the steel
rings Edson had welded had climbed some three meters, and there was post to spare. It wasn't the height
she was watching. It was the side. The current pulling the houses toward the back of the canal, and the
posts at the mouth, Edson's spliced ones, half of each buried in the mud, leaning a finger inward,
holding.

The bridges stretched between one house and another, their ropes taut. The water smooth beneath them,
brown, almost black, with the rain punching holes in it.

And every so often, a swell came in at the mouth of the canal.

Joana watched the mouth of the canal the whole time. Gate Two lying down, invisible under the water, the
oysters covering the orange steel. In front of it, on the sea side, the cages, invisible too. On the bank,
on both sides of the mouth, Benedito's sticks, the first ones, five weeks old, which were now little
plants with four, five leaves and a root beginning to open like a fan in the mud. Under water too. Doing
almost nothing.

Almost nothing.

The swell that came in at the mouth was a hand-span high. She could see it in the light on the platform
at the end. It lifted Mr. Lauro's empty platform a hand-span, and passed, and lifted the next one, and the
next, less, and when it reached the circle platform it was half a hand-span, and when it passed beneath
it the plank floor rose and fell like a sleeping chest.

The year before, with Two down, a ramp of water had come in there.

The radio crackled in Mrs. Ilda's hand. Nilo's voice, from the boatyard.

*The water's passing mark ten on the column. That's the second surge's already. But inside the shed it
isn't rising from waves. It's rising from the tide. Smooth. You can see the mark.*

Mrs. Ilda looked at Joana.

"Is that good?" she asked.

"It's what we wanted," said Joana.

---

At ten to midnight, Mr. Lauro's platform broke loose.

Joana saw it because she was watching. One of the steel rings, the one on the canal-side post, which
Edson had welded in the winter, in the first weeks, before he knew how to weld a ring onto a platform,
opened at the weld. She saw the snap in the light, a quick flash of metal. The platform, held now only by
the inner post, swung in the current like a door, and hit the bridge that joined it to the next platform,
and the bridge rope broke.

Mr. Lauro got up from his blanket.

"My house."

"It's empty, Mr. Lauro," someone said.

The platform was swinging around the inner post, the porch bigger than the room turning toward the middle
of the canal, and the current pulling. If the other ring opened, it would go. Down the canal, into the
other platforms. Twenty-two drums and a plank room slamming into houses full of people.

Joana took the radio. Before she pressed the button, she saw the light of Amaro's boat come out from
behind Jairo's platform.

"Davi. The rule."

*It'll hit the others*, said Davi's voice. *On my way.*

Jairo was in the bow, standing, with a coil of rope on his shoulder. Nobody had called him. He had jumped
in as the boat was already pulling away, Joana learned afterward, because the boy was going alone. The two
boys from Ropemakers had stayed on the platform.

Joana watched her son row. He didn't fight the water. He waited for the swell to pass under the boat, and
when it had passed he gave two strokes, short ones, and let the current do the rest. The boat reached Mr.
Lauro's platform side-on, just as it swung around and offered up the corner of the porch. Jairo jumped
onto the porch with the rope.

The next swell brought the porch back. Joana saw it in the light: the corner of planking coming down on
the boat, and Jairo's leg still between the edge of the porch and the hull. She heard the shout over the
rain.

Jairo didn't let go of the rope. Down on the porch, he passed it around a bamboo pole of the frame, took
two turns, and threw the end to Davi. Davi tied it to the thwart of the boat and rowed backward, against
the current, until the platform stopped swinging. Then he brought the boat up against the post of the next
platform and took three turns, and a knot Joana didn't know, and which she learned afterward was her
father's knot, the one for tying a hull on a slipway, which Davi had taught everyone at a circle after the
reef.

Mr. Lauro's platform stayed put. Crooked. Whole.

Davi brought Jairo to the circle platform lying in the bottom of the boat, white, biting the sleeve of his
slicker. Raul went down the rope ladder before anyone told him to, got his arms under Jairo's arms and
climbed with him backward, rung by rung. Jairo held on to his arm. Neither of them spoke. When they laid
him on the plank floor, Raul's palm was open in a cut, from the ladder's hook.

The leg was broken below the knee. Joana saw it from the angle and looked away. Mrs. Célia was already
cutting a bamboo splint.

Davi stayed kneeling beside Jairo, dripping.

"It was me," he said, for everyone to hear. "It was Benedito's rule and I went out. He came because I
went."

Jairo opened his eyes.

"Six weeks in a splint," he said. "You row my boat till it comes off."

"I will."

Mr. Lauro sat back down on his blanket.

"My house," he said again, lower.

---

Three minutes past midnight came and went. Joana didn't look at the clock. She knew because Téo called.

"High tide," he said. "Buoy seven's reading four point three. The channel on Quay Street peaked at eleven
liters."

"Last year?"

"Forty-two." He was quiet for a while. "Joana, I'm looking at the camera on Two. The one that's left, on
the post on the bank. You can't see anything. Just water. But the water's going over the gate..." He
searched for the word. "Gently. The water's going over gently."

Joana looked at the water around the platform, smooth, punched with rain.

"The map says you're all under water," he said.

"We're on top of it."

The radio crackled in Mrs. Ilda's hand. Mrs. Lúcia, from Jairo's platform.

*It took the two at the end. The small ones, the empty ones. Tore them off the rings and threw them at the
wall. It's all in pieces.*

Joana did the math before she felt anything. Two houses. The drums, the planks, the two zinc roofs. A
month of Quay Street's work. Nobody inside.

Then the radio crackled again. A voice Joana didn't recognize at first. Hoarse. From far off. Benedito,
from the village, on the net shed's radio.

*In the village the water got to the knee. Same as last year. It didn't come up any more. It's going out
through the engineer's hole. We can see it from here. It's going out like a river.*

The engineer's hole. The embankment across the creek that she had broken with the excavator, on the night
of the cofferdam, and which the village had widened afterward, with shovel and hoe, and lined with stone,
and planted with mangrove along its banks. The creek's new mouth.

"Nobody in the water?" asked Joana.

*Nobody. Everybody's in the net shed. Taís's grandma is complaining about the cold.*

---

The tide turned at twenty to one.

Joana felt it in her stomach before anything else. The circle platform, which had stood still at the top
of its piers, motionless, for almost an hour, gave a thick sigh of wood, and began to come down. A finger.
Then another. The steel rings sliding down, with a low creak, metal on metal, which had been the sound of
sleep in Lowtown for eight months.

All around, the other platforms began to come down as well. All together. The whole canal letting out its
breath.

Mrs. Ilda, in her beach chair, closed her eyes.

Nobody said anything. The children went on sleeping. Clara had fallen asleep in her mother's lap, her
mouth open. Jairo, in his splint, was breathing through his teeth. Mr. Lauro was watching his platform,
crooked, held by Jairo's rope, coming down with the others. Raul, on the edge, outside the tarp, had his
hand wrapped in his sleeve and his head down, and Joana didn't know whether he was asleep.

She took the radio.

"Nilo."

*I'm here.*

"Write it down."

*I already did.* A pause. *Mark eleven and a finger. The highest since I started writing it down.*

"And the wave?"

*None. Just tide.*

She put the radio down on the floor.

---

Davi stayed sitting beside Jairo all night, against a bamboo pole, his hands raw from the rope. Joana sat
down on his other side. At two in the morning he leaned his head on her shoulder, as he hadn't done since
he was small, and she let him.

They stayed there.

At half past six, with the light coming, the tide low, and Lowtown mud again, shining gray under a clear
sky, Téo called for the last time.

"The Civil Defense bulletin is out," he said. "Upper city, nothing. The Shallows, water to the knee.
Lowtown: one man with a broken leg, one with a cut hand, two platforms lost on Quay Street, empty. No
dead."

He stopped.

"Nobody, Joana."

She looked at the canal. The thirty-one platforms, resting on the mud, on their wooden skids, level, side
by side, on both banks, with the morning light striking the zinc sheets and the tarps and the bamboo. Mr.
Lauro's, crooked, whole. People coming out of the big platforms over the bridges resting on the mud,
going back to their own. A child running. The smell of coffee from someone's camp stove.

On the bank at the mouth of the canal, on both sides, half the sticks were gone. The mud was smooth where
they had been.

Benedito came by in his canoe at seven, through the mouth, with the empty cloth bag on his shoulder,
looking at the bank. He stopped below the circle platform. He looked at Jairo lying on the planks, and at
Davi, and back at the bank.

"It took half," he said. "Plant twice."

And he rowed back to the village, to get seed.

"The reef held," said Mrs. Ilda, from her chair.

Joana stood up. She said it out loud, for the whole platform to hear, for the ones waking up and the ones
already climbing down to the mud.

"The reef held a piece of it. This time there was no gate to fail. The surge came a little smaller than
last year's. The houses rose with the water, instead of staying under it. And the oysters left the wall
rough. The reef was a fifth of that. A quarter."

Nobody argued. Mrs. Ilda made a short gesture with her hand, like someone handing back the right change.

Far down, at the mouth of the canal, Gate Two was showing again, with the tide out. Crooked. The curve of
steel covered in oysters, gray and white, shining wet.

A year before, she had spent the whole night watching a map, on a screen, in a tower. Street by street
turning blue.

She didn't need any map this time.

"Nobody," she said.
