The chickens were floating.

Joana saw it from the end of Caulkers Street, with the water up to her knees, her rubber
boots full, a shopping bag in each hand, held up to keep them dry: in the middle of the
street, in front of the boatyard, tied to a lamp post that hadn't lit in a month, a
henhouse. A box of wire mesh and wood the size of a table, with an old zinc roof, sitting
on four blue plastic drums. Inside, six chickens. The water was rising slowly, with the
afternoon tide, and the henhouse was rising with it, a finger at a time, held to the post
by a loop of rope that slid upward as the box rose.

One of the chickens looked at her through the mesh, with one eye, without any interest.

"Davi made it," her father said, from above. "With Mr. Laércio's drums."

He was sitting at the edge of the open door of the boatyard, on the first step of the
wooden stairs he had put up on the inside, his legs swinging half a meter above the water.
In shorts, barefoot, his button-up shirt open. He had lost weight. She saw that before
anything else, and then saw that he was darker, from the sun, and that he was smiling.

"Mr. Laércio let him?"

"Mr. Laércio went to the Plateau on Tuesday. Left the whole bakery." Her father shrugged.
"The drums weren't going with him."

---

The boatyard had changed in five weeks.

Joana climbed the wooden stairs behind her father, with the bags, and stopped in the loft
to look. She remembered that loft as a long shelf of planks, full of junk, coils of rope
and cans of paint and wooden molds for hulls that had never been built, and her hammock in
the corner, when she was eight, ten, sixteen. The junk was gone. Or not gone: moved down.
It was on the ground floor, stacked against the walls, up on sawhorses, above the dark line
the water left on the brick every day.

In the loft, now, there was a house.

A double bed made of two bed frames. A plank table with two chairs. A two-burner stove,
on a gas bottle, and a shelf with pots. A five-hundred-liter water tank, black plastic, in
the back corner, with a pipe that ran up the wall to the roof and went in through a gap in
the zinc. A hammock for two. A clothesline. A crank radio on the table, the Civil Defense
one. And, hanging on nails on the wooden wall, in a row, her father's tools, each in its
place, as they had always been.

"Drinking water's from the rain," said her father, who had seen where she was looking.
"The pipe takes it off the gutter. The first water we throw out, that washes the zinc. The
second we keep."

"And when it doesn't rain?"

"It rains." He took one of the bags from her hand and looked inside. "Coffee. Good. Sugar.
What's all the beans for?"

"So you don't have to go to the upper city."

"I don't go to the upper city." He took the bag of coffee out and put it on the shelf, in
the exact spot where there was a space the size of a bag of coffee. "Davi brings it."

---

Davi was down below, on the ground floor, in the water.

Joana saw him from the rail of the loft. The water in the shed was at his chest and still
rising, and he was standing in the middle of it, in swim shorts, no shirt, arms spread,
holding a long plank horizontally above his head. At the other end of the plank, against
the wall, there was a frame of wood and bamboo, a square two meters by two, tied underneath
to empty plastic bottles, hundreds of them, wired to each other in rows. The frame was
floating. Badly. One end was deeper than the other, and it rocked every time water from the
canal came in through the door.

"Grandpa!" shouted Davi, without looking up. "It keeps tipping toward the wall!"

"Because there's more bottles on the other side," said her father, from the rail, beside
Joana. "Count."

Davi put the plank on top of the frame and dove. Joana saw his body pass under the frame, a
long shadow in the brown water, and come out the other side, and dive again. Twice. Three
times.

She didn't say anything. She had gone five weeks without saying anything about Davi and the
water.

He had started coming down to Lowtown the Saturday after the inquiry. He hadn't asked. He
had told her, at the front door, with his backpack on: *I'm going to Grandpa's.* And she had
let him, because she didn't know how not to, and because her father wasn't going to come
up, and someone had to bring the coffee. Then it had been Saturday and Sunday. Then the
school in the upper city had started sending messages, polite ones, about the Wednesday
absences.

"Eighteen on one side," shouted Davi, his head out of the water, spitting. "Twenty-four on
the other."

"And the frame?"

"The frame what?"

"Where's the weight of the frame?"

Davi stood still in the water, blinking. Then he looked at the frame. Joana looked too, and
saw it before he did: the thickest bamboo, the one that made the edge, was all on one side.
The wall side. The side that was sinking.

She opened her mouth to say it, and her father put his hand on her arm. Not hard. He just
put it there.

Davi looked for a long time. Then he slapped the water.

"The bamboo!"

"Hm," said her father.

---

She stayed the afternoon.

She hadn't planned to. She had come down to bring the groceries, because her father's
relocation place expired in nine days and she wanted to talk to him about it, and the
afternoon tide had started rising while she was crossing Lowtown, and by the time she got
there there was no way back on foot without swimming. So she stayed. She sat on a stool in
the loft, beside her father, and watched her son work.

Davi untied the frame. He moved the thick bamboo to the middle. He took six bottles off the
side that floated too high and tied them on the other. He did it all in the water, diving,
with the wire between his teeth, and her father called down from above, without hurrying,
one thing at a time, and never the answer.

"Put your hand on top. In the middle. Push."

Davi pushed. The frame sank a hand-span and came back.

"Now at the end."

Davi pushed at the end. The frame heeled, the other end came up, and when he let go it
rocked three, four times, and settled level.

"It came back," said Davi.

"It came back." Her father leaned forward on the rail. "Now get on it."

"Standing up?"

"Standing up."

Davi climbed on. Joana held her breath without meaning to. He put one knee on the edge,
then the other, then crouched in the middle, and then, slowly, stood, arms spread, a boy a
meter eighty tall on top of a square of bamboo and old bottles in the middle of a flooded
shed. The frame sank to the edge of the top bottles. It rocked. And it stayed.

Davi laughed. A loud laugh, a child's, that hit the zinc of the roof and came back.

"Stay still," said her father. "Now raise one arm. Straight up."

Davi raised it. The frame tipped a little to that side.

"Now crouch."

Davi crouched. The frame went level.

"Why?" asked her father.

Davi thought about it, crouching, with the water slapping at the bottles.

"Because the higher the weight, the more it tips?"

"Weight low," said her father. "Wide in the beam. That's all it is. The rest is fuss."

Joana was doing the math in her head and couldn't stop. Center of gravity, center of
buoyancy. Metacenter. The distance between one and the other, and the width of the base
squared, and the moment that brought the hull back upright. She had studied it in a
five-hundred-page book, in her second year of university, with integrals. Her father had
learned it from his father, in a shed, with a boat.

*Weight low. Wide in the beam.* It was the same thing. It was exactly the same thing.

---

The tide stopped rising at ten past five. Davi came out of the water, dripping, and climbed
the wooden stairs, and she threw a towel at him, and he dried himself standing at the rail,
looking at the frame down below, floating level in the still water.

"What's it for?" asked Joana. "The frame."

"For Mrs. Ilda's house," said Davi.

She looked at her father. Her father looked at the water.

"Mrs. Ilda came back?" she asked.

"She never left. She stayed three days in the shelter and came back to her roof." Her
father pulled his shirt closed and it didn't close. "Her and Nilo and a few more. The
Ferreiras came back too. There's people on almost every roof on the lower street now. About
fifty, just in these four blocks. Further over I don't know."

"Dad, in nine days they cut everything off. The tap water, the power..."

"The tap stopped the day before yesterday. Power I haven't had since the night."

"Civil Defense is coming to take everyone out on the fifteenth. With the police."

"I know."

"And what are you going to do?"

Her father didn't answer. He got up from the stool, slowly, leaning on the rail, and Joana
saw that he needed two tries, and that on the second he stood still for a second, his hand
on his chest, breathing short. Then it passed. He went over to the wall where the tools
were.

---

She knew every one.

The adze, short-handled and curved in the blade, which had been her grandfather's and
before him someone nobody remembered anymore. The big chisel and the three small ones. The
marking gauge, dark wood, with the steel point that scored the cutting line. The iron plane.
The fine-toothed saw, wrapped in a cloth. The caulking mallet, with its wooden head. And the
plumb bob: a brass cone the size of a finger, hanging on a cord, which her father used for
everything, to see whether a wall was straight, whether a mast was upright, whether a thing
was where it should be.

He took them off the nails one by one. Slowly. And laid them on the plank table, in a row,
the way they had been on the wall.

Davi watched, the towel on his shoulders, not understanding.

"Grandpa?"

"Get the box from under the bed."

Davi got it. A long wooden box with a hinged lid, which Joana knew too, where the tools went
when her father traveled to some boatyard somewhere else, once a year, to help a friend. Her
father packed them in. The adze first, at the bottom, because it was the heaviest. Weight
low. Then the chisels, the plane, the marking gauge, the saw in its cloth. The caulking
mallet. Last, the plumb bob, wound in its own cord, in a corner.

He closed the lid. He ran his hand over it.

"They're yours," he said to Davi.

Davi stood still with the box in front of him.

"Grandpa, no. You need them. We're making Mrs. Ilda's house..."

"That's exactly why." Her father sat down on the bed, hands on his knees. "Whoever does the
work has the tools. I say. You do. It's been like that for a month already. Now it's
proper."

"Dad," said Joana.

He looked at her.

She didn't know what she was going to say. There was something in her throat, very big, and
she didn't know if it was anger or the opposite. She had asked for those tools once, at
twelve. She had asked to learn. Her father had said it wasn't for a girl who was going to be
a doctor of something, and had bought her an old slide rule in a junk shop, as a present,
and she had been so happy with the slide rule that she had never asked again.

She still had the slide rule. In a drawer.

"Nothing," she said.

---

At night the tide went out and they ate rice and beans in the loft, the three of them, by
the light of a flashlight hung from the clothesline. Davi went to sleep in the hammock, with
the toolbox under it, on the floor, where he could put his hand on it. Joana wouldn't be
able to get back to the upper city before the morning tide, and her father had spread a
foam mat in the other corner.

She didn't sleep. She sat at the rail, her legs hanging over, looking at the dark shed down
below. The water had gone and left the mud of the floor shining a little, with the light
that came through the gaps in the zinc. Severino's hull was resting on its cradle again. Her
father had managed to lift it with a block and tackle. The bottle frame was lying on the
mud, waiting for the water to come back so it could be something.

Her father came to sit beside her. She heard his breathing before he sat. Short.

"Your wall," he said, after a while. It wasn't the start of a fight. It was the tone of
someone picking up a conversation that had stopped halfway, five weeks before, on a roof.
"It was meant to hold."

"It was."

"A boat doesn't hold anything back." He looked at Severino's hull, resting on the mud. "A
boat rises. The water comes, it rises. The water goes, it comes down. It doesn't fight.
That's why it lasts."

"Dad, a boat isn't a city."

"Why not?"

She opened her mouth. And she had nothing. She had a hundred technical answers, and none of
them was an answer to that. Foundations. Sewage. Scale. People who can't swim. But under all
of them was a question she had never asked, because she had spent her whole life, since she
was twelve, since the slide rule, learning to make things that held.

"I don't know," she said.

Her father nodded, as if that were the right answer.

"Your mother used to say you'd be the only Amaral who built things that stood still," he
said. "She was proud of it. So was I."

"Was."

"Am." He put his hand on her knee. The hand was warm, rough, and shook a little. "Your wall
held for fourteen years, Joana. It held the tide of seventy-nine, of eighty-four, of
eighty-seven. I was here. I saw. Nobody died, not once."

She couldn't answer.

"Now it's done," he said. "It's not blame. It's age. Everything that holds stops holding one
day. Then it has to be something else."

Down below, on the mud, the bottle frame was waiting.

"What something?" she asked.

Her father looked at the hammock where Davi was sleeping, his hand hanging out, over the
wooden box.

"He'll know," he said. "So will you. You just don't know yet that you know."
