The glass field was finished on a Tuesday, and by Thursday there had already been a fight.

Joana was on the roof of the Market when she heard it. It was where she spent most of the day
now: on top of the arch of iron and glass, on a platform of boards the young men had nailed
over the beams, in the middle of a hundred and sixty shallow tanks, each the size of a door
laid flat, lined up like garden beds. Each tank was a wooden box lined with black tarp, with
two fingers of seawater in the bottom, and over it a pane of glass, propped higher on one
side than the other. The sun hit the glass, heated the black tarp, the water evaporated, and
the vapor rose, touched the glass, which was cooler because of the wind, and turned into
drops. The drops ran down the sloping glass to the low edge, where an aluminum channel cut
from an old window frame caught it all and carried it, through a hose, to a blue drum.

It was the simplest thing in the world. Joana had made one just like it at seventeen, for a
science fair, and had won second place. First place had gone to a robot that served coffee.

A hundred and sixty tanks. Three hundred square meters of glass. It had taken two and a half
weeks to gather the glass, and it was the whole of Lowtown that gathered it. Smashed shop
windows. Windows from houses nobody was going to live in again. The glass doors of the bank
branch on Market Hill, which the sea had left whole by some miracle and which three young
men from Ropemakers took apart with a screwdriver one night at low tide. Jairo carried glass.
He didn't speak to her once in two weeks, but he carried glass, two panes at a time, on his
back, up the Market's iron ladder, and didn't break a single one.

On a good sunny day, the field made nine hundred liters. She had measured it. On Tuesday,
eight hundred and seventy. On Wednesday, cloudy, four hundred and ten.

On Thursday, at eight in the morning, at low tide, the line for water reached the canal.

---

The fight was a small one. Joana came down the iron ladder from the roof and reached the
Market's ground floor in time to see the end of it. Two men, one from Quay Street and one from
Ropemakers Street, holding the same twenty-liter jug by the handle, one on each side, both
talking at once. Nilo in the middle, with Joana's tablet in his hand, the list open, trying
to read something out loud that nobody heard. Behind them, the line, mud to the ankle,
turned all the way around to watch.

"...already took yesterday, it says so right here..."

"...I took it for my mother-in-law, she can't do stairs, you want her carried down?..."

"...it doesn't say any mother-in-law..."

Joana got into the middle. She put her hand on the jug.

"Let go," she said.

The two men looked at her. They let go.

She looked at the list on Nilo's tablet. It was her list. She had made it on Monday night, by
flashlight, in the boatyard loft: every occupied house in Lowtown, which Nilo and Davi had
counted by boat, with the number of people in each, and the amount of water per day, three
liters a head, and a column to mark who had already collected. It was a good list. It was
the kind of list she knew how to make. Clean, fair, with the sums right.

The Ropemakers man's mother-in-law wasn't on it. Nor were the two grandchildren of a woman
from Quay Street who had come back from the Plateau on Sunday. Nor an old man who lived
alone in a house at the end of the Hill and never opened his door to any boat.

"Give him the water," she said to Nilo. "Put the mother-in-law on the list."

"And the others?" It was someone in the line. "The ones who aren't on the list?"

"Put everyone on."

"Then it won't stretch."

Joana looked back at the line. Sixty people, seventy. With jugs, buckets, bottles, pots. She
did the math. The math didn't come out.

---

The problem wasn't the water. She understood that on Thursday afternoon, sitting on the board
platform on the Market roof, her notebook on her knee, watching the hundred and sixty tanks
sweat under the sun.

Nine hundred liters on a good day was enough for three hundred people. But the cloudy day
made four hundred, and then someone went without. And the line was at low tide, from seven
to ten, and whoever lived at the end of the Hill took forty minutes to reach the Market
through the mud, and when they got there the water was gone. And whoever got there first
took it for the whole family, for the mother-in-law, for the neighbor, and there was no way
to know whether the mother-in-law existed. And Nilo, with the list, became the man who said
no, and Nilo was twenty and knew everyone, and couldn't bear to say no to anyone.

And there was the other thing, which she hadn't foreseen, and which an old man from
Ropemakers had said in the line on Thursday morning, to nobody in particular, while he
waited:

"A list again. Like Civil Defense. Like the Council. You put your name on a list and then
somebody comes along and crosses it out."

She had made a fair list. Nobody wanted a fair list. They wanted not to be on a list.

She sat looking at the tanks. The sun was low now, and the drops on the glass had turned
gold. Beautiful. She could make more tanks. She could make a better list, with photos, with
times by street, with a boat to take the water to whoever couldn't come down. She could make
a system. It was what she knew how to do. It was what she had done her whole life.

Down below, on the canal, Davi went by in Amaro's boat, with the tide coming in, carrying two
children and a sack of flour somewhere. He raised a hand to her.

---

At night, Mrs. Ilda sent for her.

It wasn't a message. It was Nilo, in the boat, on the nine o'clock high tide, bumping up
against the boatyard stairs and saying, without coming up:

"Grandma asked if you'd come over. Now, if you can."

Joana went. The green house was four houses down, on the other side. She knew the way over
the roofs by now, Mr. Lauro's plank, the rope ladder from the Ferreiras' terrace. But she
went by boat, with Nilo.

Mrs. Ilda was on the terrace. Not in her room. On the terrace, in the dark, sitting in a beach
chair, with a small flashlight on the floor beside her, pointed up.

Around her, on the terrace, there were four tanks.

Joana stopped at the top of the iron stair.

They were smaller than hers. Much smaller, each the size of a window, because they were made
of windows. Whole windows, frame and all, laid on top of wooden market crates lined with black
garbage bags. Tilted up on a brick at one side. A channel of bamboo split down the middle at
the low edge of each, carrying the water into a pot. Into four pots. One each.

"The Ferreiras made them," said Mrs. Ilda. "The girl saw yours on the Market roof and copied
them. They put them here because my terrace gets the most sun on the street." She pointed
with her chin at the flashlight, and Joana looked at the pots: they were half full. "Makes
about twelve liters, on a good day. Enough for the Ferreiras, for me, for Nilo, for Mr.
Lauro, and some left to make coffee when someone comes by."

"It's leaking," said Joana. It was the first thing she had seen. "The glass on top. It isn't
sealed at the edge. It's losing vapor. And the slope's too shallow, the drop runs back into
the tank instead of down."

"I know," said Mrs. Ilda. "That's why I called you."

---

Joana sat on the floor of the terrace. There was no other chair.

"You want me to fix yours."

"I want you to fix everybody's," said Mrs. Ilda. "Not you. I want you to teach. Davi and Nilo
go from roof to roof, the ones that get sun, and teach people how to make them. We've got
windows without end. Every house makes its own. Or one for every three houses. The Market is
for whoever doesn't have a good roof, and for cloudy days."

Joana didn't answer right away.

She did the math. A big tank of tarp and glass, well sealed, like hers, yielded more per
meter than an old window on top of a crate. Thirty percent more. Maybe forty. A hundred and
sixty tanks in one place were easier to maintain, to clean, to check. With a central field
you could know exactly how much was produced, how much was used, where it fell short. With a
hundred windows scattered over a hundred roofs, nobody would know anything. The water could
come out dirty, salty, from a badly cleaned pane, with salt splashing into the channel. There
would be roofs that made too much and roofs that made nothing.

She opened her mouth to say all of that.

"It's less efficient," she said.

"Efficient for who?" said Mrs. Ilda.

Joana closed her mouth.

"There was a fight at the Market today," said Mrs. Ilda. "I heard. Over a jug. Over a
mother-in-law." She leaned forward in the chair, so the flashlight caught her face from
below. "Water you make at home, you don't fight over. Nobody fights with their own roof. And
nobody needs a list to know the pot is full."

"And when there's no sun?"

"Then the Market. Then the list, if you need one. But only then."

"And how do I know the water's good? That nobody's drinking salt?"

Mrs. Ilda looked at her as if she were a child who had asked a silly question, but kindly.

"You go and taste it," she said. "Or you teach the boy to taste it."

---

Joana sat on the floor of the terrace for a long time.

She thought about the list. About the column to mark who had already collected. About the old
man from Ropemakers: *You put your name on a list and then somebody comes along and crosses it
out.* She thought about the control room, the wall screen, the flood map that recalculated
Lowtown every thirty seconds and didn't know how high the water was inside the houses. She
thought about the wall. Three kilometers and two hundred meters of a single thing, in a single
place, that held everyone or held no one.

She thought about Jairo. *We stayed because there was your wall.*

She had spent her whole life making big, central, efficient things, that held a lot of people
at once. And that, when they broke, broke for everyone at once.

"A hundred windows," she said, slowly. "If one breaks, one's broken."

Mrs. Ilda didn't say anything. She picked the flashlight up off the floor and switched it
off, and the two of them sat in the dark, and with the dark the stars came out over Lowtown,
a lot of them, because there was no light left anywhere in the neighborhood, and the sound of
the water rising in the streets, and from somewhere, far off, a crank radio playing an old
song.

"The salt's easy to fix," said Joana, in the dark. "Put the channel a finger above the edge
of the tank and the splash can't reach it. And you seal glass with caulking compound. My
father had a bucket. It's at the boatyard."

"I know," said Mrs. Ilda. "I watched him make it."

---

They started on Saturday.

Davi and Nilo took Amaro's boat on the morning high tide and went to Ropemakers Street, with
the bucket of caulking compound, a roll of black garbage bags, a saw, and a sheet of paper on
which Joana had drawn, by hand, the simplest still she could think of. A box. A black bag. A
window. A brick to tilt it. A bamboo channel, a finger above the edge.

Joana didn't go with them. She stayed at the boatyard, sitting at the table in the loft, with
the Department's old conductivity meter, a device the size of a fat pen that she had
forgotten to give back along with her badge, and that measured how much salt there was in
water by how easily electricity passed through it. On the table, in a row, there were twelve
glasses. The water from the first twelve windows. Davi had brought them by boat the day
before, each glass with a slip of paper under it, the name of the house written on it.

She dipped the tip of the meter into the first glass. She read the number. Good. Very good.
Purer than the tap water in the upper city.

In the second, good.

In the fifth, salty. Not very. Enough. She looked at the slip: *Jairo, Ropemakers 18.*

She took the boat herself on the afternoon tide.

Jairo was on his roof, in front of the window laid on its crate, and when he saw the boat
come up against the wall, he stood, arms crossed. She climbed the iron ladder. She didn't say
anything. She looked at the still. The bamboo channel was resting against the edge. The
seawater splash, when the wind hit, jumped straight in.

She took off the channel. She picked up two pieces of brick from the floor. She put the
channel back on top of them, a finger higher.

Jairo watched.

"That's it?" he said.

"That's it."

He was quiet for a while. Then he crouched beside the still, looked at the channel up close,
touched it with his finger, measured the gap from the edge with his finger.

"A finger," he said.

"A finger."

He nodded. He didn't thank her. She didn't expect him to.

When she was going down the iron ladder, he said, from above, without looking:

"On Tuesday I'll have two more windows. From my daughter's room, she went to the Plateau. If
the boy comes by, I'll make another one."

Joana stopped halfway down the ladder.

"He'll come by," she said.
