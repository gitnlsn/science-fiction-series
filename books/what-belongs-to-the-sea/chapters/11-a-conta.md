The math took a week, and she knew the answer on the first day.

That was something she never told the interns at the Department, back when there were still
interns and a Department: that most big calculations began with an answer, and the rest of the
work was either proving the answer right or finding a way for it to be wrong. Good engineers
did the first. The others, the second. And almost everyone, at some point in their lives, did
the second believing they were doing the first.

She knew that. She did the second anyway, for seven nights, at the table in the room on the
platform, by the light of a flashlight hung from the ceiling, with the device, her father's
slide rule and a notebook.

---

The first number was the water that had nowhere to go.

It was the simplest. She had done that calculation a thousand times, in other forms, for the
Barrier. A big tide with a surge came onto the coast with a volume of water that went
somewhere. Today, with Gate Two lying down, part of that volume came in through the canal and
spread out over Lowtown, over two and a half square kilometers of streets and houses and mud.
All of Lowtown was a sponge. It filled. And while it filled, it held the water that would
otherwise rise higher somewhere else.

With the six-meter ring, the sponge ceased to exist. Lowtown would be dry inside, a meter
below the sea. And the water that went into it would have to go somewhere else.

She opened the map of the coast on the device. The old one, the Department's, which she had
copied onto a memory card years before, and which nobody had asked for back. The Barrier, along
the coast. The Quay, in the north. And in the south, where the Barrier ended and the coast
curved inward, a strip of low sand, between the tip of the wall and the mouth of a creek that
came down from the hill.

The Shallows.

She knew it by name. Everyone did. A fishing village of two hundred houses, maybe fewer now,
built on the sand, at beach level, since before the city had a name. It was outside the wall.
It always had been. The Barrier ended three hundred meters short of it, at a point Joana had
marked on the design, sixteen years before, with a note: *southern stretch unprotected — area
of low occupancy, outside scope*. Nobody had questioned the note.

On the night of the big tide, the Shallows had flooded. She hadn't known that at the time. She
hadn't looked. The Civil Defense bulletin talked about Lowtown. She went looking now, in the
recordings Nilo made from the crank radio, and found it: *The Shallows, water to the knee, no
casualties*. To the knee. Because the village was at the end of the curve, and the sea that
came along the coast hit the wall, and ran along it to the south, and when the wall ended, it
opened out, and spread. And one part went into Lowtown through the canal, and another part went
to the Shallows.

With the ring, the Lowtown part wouldn't go in anymore.

And the new dike, in Raul's drawing, didn't end three hundred meters short of the Shallows. It
curved inland, along the southern edge of Lowtown, to close the ring, and climbed to the hill.
Along the edge of the village. Right up against the village. A six-meter rampart, dark green,
with trees on top, and on the other side of it, ten meters away, the first fishermen's houses.

The sea that ran along the coast would hit the rampart and turn. Toward where there was no
rampart.

She did the math with the data from the night of the big tide. She did it with three different
propagation models, because the first gave a number she didn't want, and the second gave the
same, and the third gave a little more.

In the Shallows, with the ring, a tide like that night's would rise between forty and seventy
centimeters higher than it had.

To the knee, plus seventy centimeters, was to a man's waist. To a child's chest. Inside houses
built on sand.

And there was the creek.

---

The creek was the second number, and she almost didn't see it.

It came down from the hill along the southern edge of Lowtown, between the neighborhood and the
village, and it was through the creek that the Shallows drained. When it rained on the hill,
the water came down the creek to the sea. When the big tide filled the village, it was through
the creek that the village emptied, at low tide, in six hours, seven, carrying the water back
out. She could see it drawn on the old map: a thin blue line, crooked, nameless, which the
Department had never looked after because it was outside scope.

Raul's dike went right over it.

It didn't destroy it. She went to look at Terra Firma's design, which Raul had sent the day after
the meeting on the catamaran, whole, with every drawing, in a four-hundred-page file. The creek
didn't disappear. It was diverted. It went through a concrete culvert, under the toe of the dike,
with a tide gate at the outlet. Well drawn. Correct.

But during construction, to build the toe of the dike, the design called for a cofferdam. A
temporary wall, of steel piles driven into the sand, around the site, to hold back the water
while the foundation was dug. She went to the cofferdam drawing for the southern stretch. She
found it. The cofferdam closed the mouth of the creek. For eleven months. With a temporary pipe
of six hundred millimeters for the water to get through.

Six hundred millimeters was enough for the rain off the hill. It wasn't enough for the big tide
draining out of the village.

If a surge came during construction, the Shallows would fill and have no way to empty.

---

The third number was the pumps, and it was the one she knew best, because it was the one
everyone who worked on dry neighborhoods knew and pretended not to know.

The ground of Lowtown was old fill on top of mangrove. Mud. Sand. Centuries of organic matter.
With the ring closed and the pumps keeping everything dry, the water would leave the soil. And
mangrove soil, when it dries, sinks. The organic matter oxidizes. The mud consolidates. She had
the numbers from other dry neighborhoods, on other coasts, which she had read with envy in Terra
Firma's own reports: one, two centimeters a year. Sometimes more.

In fifty years, the length of the concession, the ground of Lowtown would be half a meter, a
meter lower than it was. And the sea, in the same fifty years, higher. The difference between
the inside and the outside of the dike would grow from both sides at once. The pumps would have
to work harder every year, and forever, and the day they stopped (through a failure, a
bankruptcy, a Council that didn't fix gates) Lowtown wouldn't flood the way it had flooded on
the night of the big tide. It would fill like a bucket. To sea level, all at once, with the
ground a meter deeper.

She closed the notebook on the seventh night and sat looking at the cover.

---

Davi was on the porch. He slept there now, in a hammock, because the room was small and she
stayed up with the flashlight on. The tide was rising, and she could hear the platform's rings
sliding on the posts, a low creak, metal on metal, which had become the sound of sleep.

"Mom," he said, from the hammock. "Are you still awake?"

"I am."

"The people from the Shallows asked something today."

She went still.

Davi had started going to the Shallows in the middle of winter, when a fisherman from there,
Benedito, showed up at the boatyard with a canoe hull split from bow to stern and asked if this
was where Amaro's grandson fixed boats. Davi fixed it. Then he fixed another. Now he went two
afternoons a week, by boat, along the coast, around the tip of the wall. He came back with fish.
He came back with his hair stiff with salt and burnt by the sun, and once with a beaded bracelet
on his wrist that she didn't ask about, and found out later belonged to Benedito's daughter,
Taís, who was sixteen and rowed better than he did.

"What did they ask?"

"If the dike goes past there." The hammock creaked. "They saw the white boat. Somebody said Terra
Firma's going to build a wall that closes off Lowtown, and they wanted to know if it closes off
the village too. Or if the village gets left out again, like it got left out of the Barrier."

Joana looked at the closed notebook on the table.

"It gets left out," she said.

"Oh." Davi was quiet for a while. "Benedito said that when it's done, we can go look at it from
up top. From the village. He said it must be pretty, from below."

She didn't answer.

---

On the eighth night, she opened the other file.

She hadn't gone looking for it. She had found it. It was on the same memory card as the
Department's old map, in a folder of Council documents she had copied whole at the time of the
design, sixteen years before, to have everything to hand, and had never opened again. The folder
had the minutes. It had her spreadsheet. And it had a document she didn't remember seeing: the
technical advisory staff's report. The original. The digital file, not the printed copy with the
paper-clip mark and the coffee stain that Councilor Viegas had put up on the wall.

She knew the text. *Favorable report on reducing the crest elevation.* She didn't open it to
read.

She opened the file's properties. Out of habit. It was what you did at the Department when a
document arrived unsigned: you looked at who had created it. Nobody ever remembered to delete it.

*Author: rmendes.*

*Created: a Monday, sixteen years before, at seven forty in the evening.*

She sat looking at that for a very long time.

Raul Mendes. Who sixteen years before had worked at the desk next to hers, in the windowless
basement. Who three days a week, that year, she remembered now, left after lunch with his coat
over his arm, and said he was *on loan to the Council, boring advisory stuff*. Who wore
headphones and did something else. Whom she had never asked what it was.

*I was there. I remember.*

She had sent her spreadsheet with the math for five meters. To the director. The director had
forwarded it to the Council. To the Council, where the person who read the technical math was
the advisory staff. He was the one who had read her spreadsheet. And written, on a Monday night,
that the residual risk was acceptable. And on Tuesday the Council had voted. And on Friday the
director had come down to the basement with the four-twenty drawing, and he was at the next desk,
headphones on, while she chose.

And afterward, for eleven years, every day, *Morning, Joana.*

And in the Council corridor, arms open: *It wasn't you. It was a room of nine people on a
Tuesday. You did the math right.*

He knew she had done the math right. He had read it.

---

She closed the file.

She sat. The flashlight swung a little from the ceiling, because the platform was rising, and
the light moved over the bamboo walls, slowly, from one side to the other.

She could go down now, take Amaro's boat, go along the coast to the white catamaran, which had
been anchored since the week before at the tip of the Quay, and knock on the dark glass of the
cabin, and put the device on the table screen in front of him. *Rmendes.* And see his face.

She didn't go.

She sat thinking, and what she thought, she would remember later with a clarity that hurt, was
this: that it had been sixteen years ago. That he had been thirty-four and had a boss. That
everybody signs. That he had come to her with the right wall in his hands, six meters, and maybe
that was it, exactly that: he knew. He had spent sixteen years knowing. And now he had come to
make it right.

And the Shallows. The cofferdam. The creek. The pumps forever.

She could fix it. That was it. As chief engineer, with twelve engineers and four hydraulic, she
could change the design from the inside. Put a spillway in the southern stretch, so the water of
the big tide would have a way out without going to the village. Open the cofferdam across the
creek with a six-meter channel, not a six-hundred-millimeter pipe. Give the pumps three times
the redundancy. Take out what was wrong, from the inside, where it could be taken out, instead
of throwing away the only right wall anyone had ever offered that neighborhood. If she said no,
they would hire another engineer. One who wouldn't do any math at all. And the village would be
left as it was in their design.

If she said yes, the village would be as it was in hers.

That was it. It was the only math that came out.

She opened the notebook. She tore out the week's pages, carefully, along the stitching. The ones
on the Shallows. The ones on the creek. The ones on the pumps. She folded them in four. She put
them inside the old plastic folder, with the yellowed five-meter spreadsheet. She put it away at
the bottom of her backpack.

She wasn't going to hide it. She was going to solve it. It wasn't the same thing.

Outside, in the hammock, Davi was asleep. The beaded bracelet on his wrist, hanging over the edge
of the hammock, swung a little with the platform rising.
