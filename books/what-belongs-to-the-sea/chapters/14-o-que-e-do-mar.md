The circle gathered at eight in the morning, at low tide, and the platform sat far down, low
between the mud walls of the canal, tied to the stumps of the old footbridge.

Nobody had called it. Mrs. Ilda had sent Nilo from platform to platform at six, and by eight they
were all there. People sitting on the plank floor, people standing, people on the bridges, which
at low tide came down to the mud and sat crooked. Joana had come on foot from the site, along the
crest of the wall and then across the mud, without sleeping, her backpack on her back. She stood
in the middle. It was the place for whoever was speaking, and she had never stood there.

She put the backpack on the floor. She took out the plastic folder. She took out the pages.

"I knew," she said.

She told them all of it. The Shallows, forty to seventy centimeters more in a tide like the one in
March. The creek closed off by a six-hundred-millimeter pipe for eleven months. The pumps that would
have to work forever on ground that kept sinking. That she had done the math before the vote, seven
nights, and that she had torn the pages out along the stitching and kept them at the bottom of her
backpack. That her son had asked, in front of everyone, and she had said *it can be solved*. That
she had said *trust me*. That the village had filled the night before, as the math said it would,
and that the one who had opened the embankment with the excavator was her.

"The excavator was mine," she said. "Terra Firma will come after me for that, not you. The rest
is what I came to say. I knew."

Nobody said anything while she spoke. When she finished, nobody did either. The platform creaked
against the stumps. A child cried on one of the bridges and someone hushed it.

It was Jairo who spoke. He was sitting in the second row, as on the night of the vote, his arms
crossed.

"I put my stone on the left because of you," he said. Not loudly. "You said six meters. I thought
about the water mark on the wall at my house. How high it is on the stairs."

"I know."

"You don't." He uncrossed his arms. "Last time it was the water that told me. I came down the stairs
and it was already down there." He looked at the pages on the floor, beside the backpack. "This
time it was you. Before."

Mrs. Ilda looked at him, then at Joana, and sent Nilo to fetch the flour sack and the buckets.

The line went around the platform. Jairo was one of the first. He put his stone in the right-hand
bucket, without stopping in front of them. Edson stood at the back a while, the stone in his hand.

"I already signed it over," he said, to nobody. "I don't unsign."

He put it in the left-hand one.

They counted right there, on the plank floor, in little piles of ten. Four hundred and twelve stones
in the right-hand bucket. Thirty-one in the left.

After the count, Jairo passed her the thermos of coffee. Joana drank from the cap and handed it
back.

On Tuesday, the family from number twenty-six on Ropemakers, the couple and their two boys, who had
put their stones on the left, climbed the hill with their things in a wheelbarrow and went to the
Plateau. They left their platform tied to its posts, the door wired shut. Mrs. Ilda didn't cross
their names off the list. She wrote beside them, in pencil: *Plateau.*

Joana saw the list. She worked out how many of the stones on the left had been put there because of
her, on the night of the vote, and how many had come back now, and the math had no number.

---

The proof was painted on an iron column, and the one who had painted it was her father.

Joana only noticed on a Sunday night, a week after the cofferdam, when she went to the boatyard to
fetch a tarp and found Nilo sitting at the rail of the loft with a headlamp and a blue-covered school
notebook on his knee.

He was looking at the column. The iron one, in the middle of the shed, the thickest. The one her father
had marked with white paint, a hand-span at a time, years before, to measure the tides of other days.
The water was standing at the top of the night tide, smooth, and covered the column to the sixth
mark.

Nilo wrote something in the notebook. Then he looked at the clock on his device and wrote again.

"What's that?" asked Joana.

He jumped. He almost dropped the notebook.

"Nothing." He closed the notebook. Then he opened it again and held it out to her. "It's for
Grandma. For the radio. Since the night your father died, Grandma's had me write down the tide. Every
high tide. The mark the water reaches on the column, and the time. So we know if it's rising or falling
from one month to the next. She says the Council isn't going to tell us, so we'll count it ourselves."

Joana took the notebook.

It was six months of tides. Line by line, in the round, careful handwriting of someone who had
done all his schooling in Lowtown. Date. Time. Mark. Sometimes a note. *Full moon.* *Windy.* *Heavy
rain, Grandma says it doesn't count.* Twice a day, nearly every day, since the morning after the night
she had sat in a hull in front of the green house. A few days were missing. The days of the
funeral.

About three hundred and fifty high tides.

She sat on the floor of the loft with the notebook in her lap and read all of it. She took the
average in her head, page by page, the way she did with the buoys. Mean high water sat between the
fifth and sixth marks, closer to the sixth. One point three something meters above mean sea level.
Inside Lowtown. Nilo watched her without understanding. When she finished, he asked if it was wrong.

"No," she said. "It's right. It's all right."

---

Councilor Marta Viegas presided again.

The room was the same as at the inquiry, on the top floor of the tallest building in the upper city,
with the horseshoe table and the glass wall facing east. The sea outside was smooth and blue, as on the
day of the inquiry, and far below, between it and the room, there were now two things that hadn't
existed eight months before: in the middle of the brown stain of Lowtown, a thin, pale line, the
platforms on the Market canal; and to the south, where the Barrier ended, a half-moon of brown water
with an excavator rusting in the middle.

The session was on the ratification of the concession. The Lowtown residents' association had asked for
its revocation three days after the cofferdam. So had the Shallows. Terra Firma had sent three lawyers,
in dark suits, sitting in the front row on the left, with identical tablets. Raul wasn't with them. He
was sitting at the back, alone, in his dark coat, against the wall. Jairo and Edson had come too,
each in his own corner.

Terra Firma's lawyers were right, and Raul was right, and Joana had spent the whole week knowing
they were right. The association had signed on. A hundred and forty titles had been signed over, with
signature and stamp. The Council had ratified. A circle on a canal with stones in a bucket unsigned
nothing.

The first lawyer spoke for forty minutes. He spoke well. He spoke of legal certainty, of a binding
contract, of good faith, of investment already made, of five weeks of foundation destroyed *by the
deliberate act of a former employee*. He didn't say her name. He didn't need to.

Then it was the association's turn. Mrs. Ilda got up from her plastic chair in the third row, went to
the small table with the microphone, and said:

"The engineer will speak."

---

Joana didn't bring Terra Firma's design. She didn't bring the math on the Shallows or the pumps. She
brought a blue-covered school notebook and a sheet of paper with a table on it.

"Councilor, I'd like to begin with a law that's older than this Council," she said.

Councilor Viegas took off her glasses.

"The Shore Law of Thalassa," said Joana. "It dates from the founding of the city. It has only one
article that still stands, and nobody uses it because nobody has needed to. It says that the strip of
land covered by mean high water is for common use. Everyone's. That it can't be sold, or given, or
signed over, or have a private owner, because it belongs to the sea and to those who live from it.
That's why the beach at the Shallows never had a deed. That's why the Quay is public. The mean high-water
line is the boundary. On this side of it, there can be an owner. On the other side, there can't."

"We are familiar with the Shore Law, Engineer."

"Are you familiar with the line?"

Councilor Viegas was silent for a moment.

"The mean high-water line on the Thalassa shore," said Joana, "was last measured forty-two years ago. By
the Department. It's in the land registry. It runs along the beach, outside the wall. All of Lowtown is
on this side of it. All of Lowtown can have owners. The hundred and forty titles signed over to Terra
Firma are for land on this side."

She held up the blue notebook.

"But the law doesn't say *the line measured forty-two years ago*. It says *mean high water*. Where it
is. And since the night of the thirty-first, with Gate Two lying on the bottom of the canal, high water
doesn't stop at the wall anymore. It comes in. Twice a day. I know where it stops now, because a
twenty-year-old named Nilo wrote it down, every high tide, for almost seven months, on an iron column my
father painted. Three hundred and eighty measurements. I checked them against the tide gauge in the
tower on the Quay, which the Department still runs. They agree to within three centimeters."

She put the sheet of paper in front of Councilor Viegas. And another in front of the lawyers.

"Mean high water, today, inside Lowtown, reaches the 1.32-meter elevation. Everything below
that is under mean high water. Under the Shore Law, it's part of the common-use strip. It belongs to the sea." She
breathed. "I asked the Department's land registry, through Téo, an operator in the tower who still talks to
me, to cross the 1.32-meter elevation with the hundred and forty lots signed over to Terra Firma. It's on
the second sheet."

The three lawyers looked at the second sheet at the same time.

"A hundred and twenty-six lots are entirely below the line," said Joana. "Eleven are cut by it. Three
are above it." She looked at the horseshoe. "The residents of Lowtown couldn't sign over to Terra Firma
what was no longer theirs. It wasn't anyone's anymore. It stopped being on the night of the
thirty-first. Nobody noticed, because nobody measured. The sea measured."

---

The room went silent in a way Joana knew. The silence of the inquiry, of waiting. But it wasn't her they
were waiting for this time.

The first lawyer stood up.

"Councilor, this is..." He stopped. He began again. "The high-water line is a registry line. It isn't
changed by a school notebook. It requires an official survey, procedure, publication..."

"It does," said Councilor Viegas. She had the second sheet in her hand, looking at the numbers. "And the
Council is going to order one. Now. With the tide gauge in the tower on the Quay and the drone survey
that this Council, incidentally, already has, thanks to an arrangement with the company you represent."
She put the sheet down on the table. "If the official survey comes out where the boy's notebook does, sir,
your client holds a hundred and forty titles to land that couldn't be sold, and a contract exchanging
nothing at all for apartments."

The lawyer sat down.

Councilor Viegas looked to the back of the room. At Raul.

"Engineer Mendes," she said. "You were the concession's director of projects. I saw your name in the
file. Would you like to say anything?"

Raul stood up, his hands in his pockets. He was silent for a while.

"The southern stretch of the dike pushed the big tide into the Shallows," he said. "Between forty and
seventy centimeters more. Engineer Amaral did the math before the vote. I saw her math on the night of
the tenth." He took his hand out of his pocket. There was a memory card between his fingers. "I'd
seen the same thing before she did, in our own model. I had it rerun with a different scenario, one
that came out lower, and that was the one that went into the file. This is the first run. The one
with the Shallows. With the date on it."

He went up to the horseshoe and put the card on the table, in front of Councilor Viegas. She didn't
pick it up. She looked at it.

"And the technical advisory report that cut the old wall, sixteen years ago, I wrote it," said Raul.
"You have the file, if you want it."

Nobody in the room moved.

"I resigned from Terra Firma yesterday." And he went back to the rear of the room, and sat down.

---

The ratification was suspended that afternoon, pending the official survey. The survey came out
seventeen days later, on the last working day of the year, and came out at one point two nine
meters. Three
centimeters' difference from Nilo's notebook. Terra Firma withdrew its concession application a week
later, by written notice, without a hearing. The white catamaran left one morning at high tide, without
noise, without a wake, and Joana was in the clock tower and saw it go.

The suit against her, Terra Firma didn't withdraw. It reached her device the same week as the
notice, with a read receipt that told Terra Firma the second Joana opened it: damages, against Joana
Amaral, as a private person, for five weeks of foundation destroyed by the deliberate act of a former
employee. She read the amount at the bottom of the page and did the math on her savings. She stopped
halfway.

But that was later. On the afternoon of the session, after Councilor Viegas had suspended everything and
the room began to empty, something else happened, and that was what Joana kept.

Jairo came to talk to her.

He had been standing at the back, near Raul, and he came down the aisle between the plastic chairs
to the small table, his arms hanging, and stopped in front of her.

"I have title," he said. "Ropemakers eighteen. It was my wife's, from her father. My house is at
one point one meters. It's on your sheet. Below the line."

"It is."

"So it's not mine anymore."

Joana didn't look away.

"It isn't anybody's, Jairo. Not yours, not Terra Firma's, not the Council's. It's common use. Whoever
lives there uses it. Nobody takes it away."

He stood looking at her. She waited for the anger. He had a right to it. He had lost his wife to her
wall, and now his house to the law she had found.

"Nobody takes it away," he repeated.

"Nobody can sell it. Nobody can buy it. Nobody can take it away."

Jairo thought. Joana watched him think, his mouth shut, his thumb rubbing a knuckle.

"My wife would've liked that," he said at last. "She used to say a house wasn't meant to belong to
anybody. It was meant to belong to whoever swept it."

"Your wife didn't pay for my shop."

Edson was behind him, in the aisle. He had a copy of the second sheet in his hand, the one with the
lots, folded in four.

"Quay forty," said Edson. "The metal shop. It's here, below the line. Thirty years of payments. I
signed the title over to Terra Firma for an apartment that's never going to exist now, and the
ground I signed over isn't anybody's anymore." He looked at Joana, not at Jairo. "I swept that floor
thirty years, Engineer. I want to know who pays me."

"I don't know," said Joana.

It was true, and it wasn't much. Edson put the sheet in his shirt pocket, the same pocket as the
receipt, and went off down the aisle without looking back. Jairo watched him go. Then he turned
and went too. At the door, he stopped, and turned his head.

"The basil took," he said. "On the platform. It's this big."

He showed her with his hand. A hand-span.

---

Mrs. Ilda waited for her in the corridor, beside the elevator, where eight months before Raul had held
her. She was sitting on a bench, with Nilo beside her, and the blue notebook in his lap.

"The green house is at one point four five meters," said Mrs. Ilda. "Above your line. Thirteen centimeters.
It's still mine."

"It is."

"I've never been so sad not to lose a house." She laughed, a short laugh, from inside. "It would've been
nice, everybody together."

Joana sat down beside her on the bench.

"The sea rises another hand-span and you're in," she said. "About twenty years."

"I don't have twenty years." Mrs. Ilda put her hand on Nilo's knee. "He does."

Nilo was looking at the blue notebook in his lap as if it were something else. Something he hadn't known
he had made.

"Keep writing it down," Joana told him. "Every tide. Don't stop."

"What for, now?"

She thought of her father. Of the iron column with the marks a hand-span apart. *To measure the tides of
other days.*

"Because it's going to keep rising," said Joana. "And someone has to know where it is."
