The list had three hundred and four names, and Mrs. Ilda let it stand.

It arrived on a Monday morning, with the Civil Defense boy, who now came down to Lowtown in uniform, in a
gray boat, twice a week, bringing papers from the Council and taking sick people to the hill. He climbed onto
the circle platform with a folder under his arm and the face of someone carrying something he didn't know
was good or bad.

"It's from the upper city," he said, handing the folder to Mrs. Ilda. "People who've asked the Council to
come down. To live here. Councilor Viegas sent me to ask what the circle thinks."

Mrs. Ilda opened the folder. It was four printed sheets, with names, ages, addresses in the upper city, and a
column at the end, blank, headed *Status*.

She read the whole first sheet with her finger running down the names. Joana was beside her, and saw the
finger stop at some of them. At an old couple from the hill. At a woman with three children. At a name Joana
knew too, a teacher from the school in the upper city, Davi's.

"Three hundred and four," said Mrs. Ilda.

She closed the folder. She kept it in her lap.

"It's a list," said Joana.

"It is." Mrs. Ilda looked at her. "This one I'll let stand. This one isn't for crossing anybody out. It's
for putting them in."

---

It had been three months since the great tide, and Lowtown had a name on paper.

Councilor Viegas had come down in person. On a Wednesday, two weeks after the great tide, at night, in a Civil
Defense boat, with the boy in the orange vest at the oars and two Council staff who seemed afraid of the
water. She had climbed the rope ladder of the circle platform without taking anyone's hand, in pants and a
long-sleeved shirt, with her gray bun and her glasses, and had sat down on the plank floor, in the circle,
between Mr. Lauro and a child, and had listened to the whole circle for two hours without saying anything.
Then she had asked to speak, and read out a paper.

*The settlement on the water situated in the common-use strip of the shore, between the Barrier and the
one-meter-twenty-nine elevation, is hereby recognized as a neighborhood of the city of Thalassa, with the
management of the strip assigned to the residents' association and the community of the Shallows, jointly,
under the terms of the collective-use grant.*

Six votes to three. Councilor Viegas had said it would always be six to three, for everything, and that she
had learned to like the number.

On her way out, at the rope ladder, she stopped beside Joana.

"And Terra Firma's suit?"

"Still running," said Joana. "The lawyer says until next year. That math is mine, Councilor. Not the
neighborhood's."

The Council wasn't going to send piped water. It had no way to, and nobody asked. It would send a teacher in a
boat, three mornings a week, for the children who didn't go up the hill. It would send the Civil Defense boy.
It would send old solar panels the upper city was replacing. It would send the drone, on Saturdays, at noon,
but now the images came to the circle too, and Nilo looked at them on his device, at night, and noted things
in the blue notebook, beside the tides.

The Council's paper said *settlement on the water*. Nobody called it that.

The children called it *the reef*. It had started with Clara and the village boys, who dove at the mouth of
the canal, at high tide, to look at the oysters and the fish, and said *I'm going to the reef*, and then *I
live on the reef*.

She didn't correct them. The old people said Lowtown. The village said the village. The Council said
settlement. The circle would decide one day, with stones, if it had to. Maybe it wouldn't have to.

---

She had given up the apartment in the upper city soon after. She had climbed the hill one last time, alone,
one morning at low tide, and emptied the drawers into two bags, and handed the key to the landlady, and come
down.

In the bags, almost nothing. Clothes. The five-hundred-page books from her second year of college, which
she hadn't managed to leave behind. The photos of her mother. And the old plastic folder, with the yellowed
five-meter spreadsheet and the pages on the Shallows, which she had taken out of her backpack after the
cofferdam and put away in a drawer in the apartment, not knowing what to do with them.

She took it down with her. She put it on the table on platform nineteen, the table from her father's loft.

And the slide rule, which wasn't in the bags.

It had been on the platform table since Mrs. Célia's first house, wrapped in a cloth. The slide rule
her father had bought in a junk shop when she was twelve, in place of the tools. Wood and yellowed plastic,
with the middle slide that ran between the two outer rules, and a glass cursor with a fine red line. She
hadn't used it for anything in thirty years. Nobody did. There was a calculator inside every device, and
there was the model, and the table screen, and the curved wall of the control room.

But she had used it in the boatyard loft, on Mrs. Célia's first house. The one that keeled over. And at
that table, with the flashlight swinging from the ceiling, on the math for the Shallows.

She wrapped it in the cloth again and put it at the bottom of her backpack.

---

That Monday, the morning tide would be one of the big ones of the month. A new moon two days before. It
wouldn't be big like the equinox. It would be beautiful.

Davi brought Amaro's boat up against the ladder of platform nineteen at nine, with the tide rising.

He was sixteen now. He had turned sixteen at the start of the year, on a Monday, in the village, with Taís
and Benedito and a fish grilled on the sand, and Joana had gone, along the coast, by kayak, and come back at
night with her hair stiff with salt, like his. He was taller. Broader. He lived partly on the platform,
partly in the village, and partly at the boatyard, which was now the workshop of four people and the
toolbox, and nobody asked anymore where he slept.

"Ready?" he said, from the boat.

She went down.

He rowed along the canal, between the platforms. Joana sat in the bow, as on the morning after the big
tide, looking. The street that breathed. People on their porches. Mrs. Célia hanging out laundry. Jairo
watering his basil, which was a bush now, in a bucket, and still favoring his leg a little. Mr. Lauro's platform, which Edson had rewelded and straightened,
with a new ring, a thick one, which Mr. Lauro had painted red. A boy fishing off a bridge with a nylon line.
The teacher from the Council boat, in a hat, holding class on the porch of the circle platform for twelve
children sitting on the floor, and the children watching Amaro's boat go by instead of looking at her.

He didn't head for the mouth of the canal. He turned into Caulkers Street.

She already knew where.

---

The boatyard was standing. The brick walls of the ground floor, the iron columns with their white paint
marks, the blue tarp on top. Davi had rebuilt the front wall with new boards, pine, and painted it white,
with a blue stripe along the bottom. The sliding door was open.

He didn't go in through the door. He went around the side of the shed, along the narrow alley between the
boatyard and the house next door, which at high tide was a canal two meters wide, and came out at the back.

At the back of the boatyard, the slipway.

Joana hadn't seen the slipway since she was a child. It was the launching ramp. It began at the floor of the
shed, at the back door, and ran down, wide, stone, at a gentle slope, for forty meters, to the bottom of the
old canal that passed behind the street, where the boats her father and grandfather and great-grandfather
made went into the water for the first time. Two wooden rails down the middle, for the cradle to slide on.
In her day, the slipway only reached the water at high tide. The lower part sat in the mud.

Now the slipway was under water all the way. Even at low tide, the lower part didn't come out anymore. And
at a new-moon high tide, like that morning's, the water was two and a half meters deep over the bottom
end.

Davi tied the boat to an old iron ring, set into the back wall of the shed. The ring for tying up new boats,
before their first launching. It was covered in oysters.

He held the mask out to her.

---

She went down the wall of the shed, holding on to the stones. The cold came first. Then the green,
with the morning light coming in at a slant, in shafts.

The slipway was down below.

She recognized the shape before she recognized anything else. The wide ramp, going down into the dark. The
wooden rails in the middle, which weren't wood now, they were two lines of oysters, thick, gray, following
the shape of the wood underneath like a skin. The stones of the ramp, around them, covered in short brown
seaweed, which swayed all together with the swell that came from the canal. Fish. A school of little silver
fish turning at the same time. A bigger fish, striped, hanging still in the shadow of the left-hand rail. A
crab walking sideways over a stone.

And at the bottom end of the slipway, where the ramp ended and the bottom of the old canal began, where her
father had launched every boat of his life, where her grandfather had launched, where she had seen, at seven,
from above, a white hull slide down on its cradle and hit the water and float, and her father shout *It came
back!*, and everyone laugh, there, on a loose stone at the end, there were three corals.

Small. One the size of a grain of rice, two the size of a fingernail. Golden brown, honeycombed. Almost
touching, in a crooked row.

She stayed looking until her air ran out. She went up. She breathed. She went down again.

Davi was down there, beside her. He had something in his hand. He opened it.

His grandfather's plumb bob. The brass cone, at the end of its cord.

He held the cord up, above his head, and let the cone down, slowly, in the green water, until it almost
touched the stone at the end of the slipway, beside the three corals. The cone swung with the swell. It
swung. And it stopped.

True.

Davi looked at her through the cracked glass of the mask. She could see he was smiling.

---

They came up into the boat with the tide at the top. They sat, he on the middle thwart, she in the stern, on
the rower's thwart. Her father's thwart. Dripping.

She took the cloth out of her backpack.

Davi looked at the cloth. Then he raised his eyebrows at her.

She unwrapped the slide rule. The wood, the yellowed plastic, the glass cursor with the fine red line. She
put it in his lap.

"Grandpa gave it to me," she said. "Instead of the tools. When I was twelve. I asked for the tools and he gave
me this."

Davi took the slide rule in both hands. He ran the middle slide. He ran the cursor.

"What's it for?"

"For doing math."

"I've got math on my device."

"I know." She looked at the green water over the slipway. "This one's for doing math slowly. So you can see
the whole calculation, from one end to the other, and know where every number comes from. So that when
someone says it's acceptable, you know how to ask acceptable for who."

He kept the slide rule in his lap until the water stopped dripping from his hair. Then he wrapped it back up in the cloth, carefully, and put it
in the toolbox, which was in the bottom of the boat. At the bottom of the box. Under everything, even the
adze.

"Weight low," he said.

She laughed. She laughed out loud, the way she hadn't laughed in a year and three months, and the laugh hit
the wall of the boatyard and came back, and from inside, from the shed, someone working in the workshop
shouted *What happened?*, and Davi shouted *Nothing!*, and laughed too.

---

The tide stood at the top at ten past eleven. Joana knew without looking at the clock. She felt it in the
boat.

She stayed there, sitting on her father's thwart, hands on her knees, looking up.

From there, from the back of the boatyard, you could see very little. The brick wall. The blue tarp roof. And
above it, cut out against the morning sky, a piece of Lowtown. The ridges of the houses. The Market's clock
tower, stopped at ten past four. And farther off, to the east, the sea, which you couldn't see but could
hear, a thick, continuous noise, breaking on the old wall covered in oysters, and stumbling, and arriving
tired.

Thalassa. Her mother used to say it meant *sea* in an old language. She had grown up thinking her
mother had made that up. She had checked, one night, in the winter, at the table on the platform, with the
flashlight swinging from the ceiling. It was true. The city had always been called sea. It had just spent
three hundred years pretending it wasn't.

The tide began to fall. Amaro's boat went down with it, on the oyster-covered ring.

And on the canal, on the other side of the boatyard, she didn't need to see it to know, the thirty-one
platforms, and the eleven on Quay Street, two of them rebuilt, and the nine new ones on the hill, were going
down too, all together, on their rings, sliding on the posts, with a low creak of metal on metal, which was the
sound of sleep in Lowtown. In the city.

Near midnight the water would come back. And the city would rise with it.
