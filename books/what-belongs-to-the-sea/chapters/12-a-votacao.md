They put two buckets in the middle of the circle platform, and Mrs. Ilda brought the stones.

They were pebbles from the bottom of the canal, gray, the size of a fingernail, which Nilo and the
boys had picked up at the midday low tide, in a flour sack. Each person took one from the sack.
Each person put it in a bucket. The one on the left was yes. The one on the right was no. There
was no name anywhere. There was no list.

"A stone doesn't lie and doesn't carry a pen," said Mrs. Ilda, when someone from Terra Firma asked
whether it wouldn't be better to do it on a device, with a record. "And nobody comes along after
and crosses it out."

The young woman from Terra Firma, a young engineer in a pale shirt who had come with Raul on the
catamaran and who held a tablet as if it were a shield, looked at Raul. Raul nodded.

Joana was sitting beside him. At the front of the circle. With her back to the canal.

It was the first time she had sat there.

---

She had said yes on a Friday, nine days before, on the deck of the catamaran, at sunset. She
hadn't rehearsed. She had walked to the tip of the Quay along the crest of the old wall, with her
backpack on and the plastic folder inside it, and climbed the catamaran's ladder, and Raul was
on deck alone, looking at the sea, with a glass in his hand.

"I'm in," she had said. "On three conditions."

He had turned.

"The southern stretch is mine. I redesign it. Whatever I change stays changed."

"Fine."

"The cofferdam across the creek too."

He had hesitated. One second. She had seen it.

"Fine."

"And the pump redundancy. Three times."

"That costs..."

"Three times, Raul."

He had looked at her for a while. Then he had smiled, the smile from eleven years in the
basement, and held out his hand.

"Nobody deserves it more than you," he had said.

She had shaken his hand. His hand was warm and dry. She had thought of *rmendes* and hadn't let
go of his hand any faster than she would have let go of anyone's. She had been proud of that, on
the way back, along the crest of the wall, in the dark. Then she had been ashamed of having been
proud. Then she hadn't thought about it anymore.

---

The circle platform was fuller than Joana had ever seen it. People sitting on the plank floor
right to the edge, feet in the water. People standing behind. People on the platforms around it,
on the porches, on the bridges, in boats tied to one another around the big platform, making a
ring of hulls that rocked all together when anyone moved. The tide was in, standing at the top,
and the flashlights hanging from the bamboo poles of the tarp roof made yellow circles on the
water.

Five hundred, maybe. Mrs. Ilda would know.

Raul had spoken first. Well. Joana had heard him speak at Council meetings, on building sites, at
wakes, and he always spoke well, but that night he spoke better than ever, because he spoke
little. He didn't show the model. He had brought a screen, tied to a bamboo pole, and didn't turn
it on. He said what the dike was, and what the pumps were, and what the buildings were. He laid out
the exchange: title for apartment. He gave them the forty percent, without hiding it, and the
priority to buy and the financing and the place on the Plateau, without dressing it up. And he
told them about the next equinox tide, and what could happen to the platforms at the mouth of
the canal, and made no drama of it at all. He said it like someone passing on information. Last,
he said that Terra Firma only signed with someone who had a name at the registry office: if it
was yes, the circle would have to register as a residents' association, with bylaws and a
president.

"I've seen what you've made here," he said, at the end. "I've been an engineer for twenty-six
years. I've never seen anything so beautiful. And I can't let you sleep in these houses on the
next big tide without telling you what I know."

Then he had said that the project's chief engineer would be Joana Amaral.

The platform had gone silent.

---

Joana spoke next.

She didn't remember afterward, exactly, what she said. She remembered pieces, as she did the night
her father died. She remembered saying that she knew the old wall better than anyone, because she had
made it, and that she knew why it had failed. She remembered saying *six meters* and seeing people
nod. She remembered saying that she would be in there, every day, on the site, as she had been at
Gate Two, and that this time nobody was going to cut anything on a Tuesday.

She remembered saying:

"You don't need to trust Terra Firma. Trust me."

And, the moment she said it, feeling the sentence drop onto the platform like a stone into a
bucket.

She remembered Jairo. Sitting in the second row, arms crossed, with his usual face. Looking at her.
When she said *six meters*, he uncrossed his arms.

And she remembered Mrs. Ilda, sitting on her market crate, on the other side of the circle, who
didn't look at her once while she spoke. She looked at the canal.

---

Mrs. Ilda spoke last. She didn't get up. She didn't need to.

"I have title," she said. "The green house is mine. My father bought it, with a deed. If it's yes,
I get a new apartment, on the sixth floor, with an elevator. I've never ridden an elevator in my
own home."

A few people laughed. Quietly.

"Nilo doesn't have title," said Mrs. Ilda. "Nilo lives with me, but the house isn't his. When I
die, it will be. If it's yes before then, the house won't be anybody's anymore to be his." She
looked around. "Mr. Lauro doesn't have it. Mr. Lauro rented his whole life. Célia doesn't have it.
Célia had a one-room house that was her mother-in-law's, who died without passing it on. The boys
from Ropemakers don't have it, they moved into an empty house six years ago. Edson has it. Jairo
has it."

Jairo shifted.

"Forty percent," said Mrs. Ilda. "He said so. Forty percent of us are going to have to
buy what today we're making with our hands, or go to the Plateau." She stopped. "And who made the
houses that float? Who made the glass field? Who carried the foam boards out of the cold rooms? I
know who. I counted. It was half people with title and half people without."

She turned to Raul.

"You're not offering us homes. You're offering homes to half of us. And the other half gets to look
at them from below."

Raul didn't answer. He knew, Joana thought, when not to speak.

---

It was Davi who asked the question.

He was standing at the back, leaning against one of the bamboo poles of the roof, arms crossed, the
beaded bracelet on his wrist. Joana hadn't seen him arrive. She hadn't known he was coming. He had
spent the afternoon in the Shallows, again, as he had spent two school afternoons that week; the
absence notice had come to her device on Thursday, and she had put the device away without
answering.

"And the Shallows?" he said.

Not loudly. But the whole platform turned.

"The dike runs right up against it," said Davi. "In the drawing. I saw. On my mother's table. The
dike closes off Lowtown and turns inland along the edge of the village, and the creek goes
underneath. Benedito asked if the village is inside. She said it's left out." He looked at Raul.
"What happens to the village when the big tide comes, with the dike next to it?"

Raul opened his mouth. Joana spoke first.

"The southern stretch is going to be redesigned," she said. "By me. There'll be a spillway so the
village water has a way out. It's one of the conditions."

"Did you do the math?" asked Davi.

He said it to her. Only to her. Over five hundred people, over the water, in the voice from the
boatyard loft, the one he used when he asked his grandfather *why*.

She felt the plastic folder at the bottom of the backpack, on her back, against her spine. The
pages torn out along the stitching. Forty to seventy centimeters. The cofferdam. Six hundred
millimeters.

"I did," she said. "It can be solved."

It was true. It was true the way *almost every one* had been true, sixteen years before, on a
drawing with the crest at four-twenty.

Davi stood looking at her. She didn't look away. She couldn't look away with five hundred people
watching. He looked away first.

---

They voted at half past ten.

The line went around the platform, one person at a time, to the flour sack in Mrs. Ilda's
hands, and then to the buckets. Nobody looked at which bucket anyone else used. Joana looked
anyway, because she counted everything, and because she couldn't not look.
Mr. Lauro, the right-hand bucket. Mrs. Célia, the same, with Clara by the hand. The
three brothers from Ropemakers, all three in the left, and the cousin in the right, and the three
of them looking at the cousin. Edson, in the left. Mrs. Lúcia, who had asked for the houses on
Quay Street, in the left.

Jairo stood for a while in front of the two buckets, the stone in his closed hand. Then he looked
at Joana, across the platform. She didn't know what was on her face.

He put it in the left.

Mrs. Ilda voted last. In the right. She didn't hide it.

They counted right there, on the plank floor, under the flashlights, two people on each side,
stone by stone, in little piles of ten. Joana didn't go near. She stayed sitting where she was,
beside Raul, her hands on her knees.

Two hundred and forty-one in the left.

Two hundred and eighteen in the right.

Nobody clapped. Some people left right away, by boat, without speaking. Others stayed, in groups,
talking low. The young woman from Terra Firma typed something on her tablet. Raul put his hand on
Joana's shoulder, for a second, and took it away.

Mrs. Ilda passed in front of her, on Nilo's arm. On the day of the inquiry she had gone by alone,
without looking. This time she stopped.

"Twenty-three stones," she said. "Do you know how many people heard you say *trust me* and changed
buckets?"

Joana opened her mouth and closed it.

"I don't know either," said Mrs. Ilda. "But it was more than twenty-three."

---

Davi wasn't on her platform when she got there.

The hammock was on the porch, empty. His backpack wasn't on its hook. In the room, on the table,
her notebook was open. Where the pages torn out along the stitching were missing, you could see,
on the page underneath, the mark of the pencil that had come through the paper. The numbers
pressed into the paper. *40–70 cm. The Shallows.*

Her father's toolbox wasn't there.

She ran out onto the porch. Amaro's boat wasn't tied to the post.

She looked at the canal. The lights of the platforms, the bridges, the water already going out.
And far down, at the mouth of the canal, near the fallen Gate Two, a small light, low, swinging,
moving away. Toward the tip of the wall. Toward the south.

The device buzzed in her pocket.

*I'm staying in the Shallows. Benedito said I can. Don't come after me.*

And then, a minute later, when she already had her thumb over the screen to answer:

*Grandpa used to say a boat doesn't hold anything back. You're building a wall again.*

She stood on the porch until the light disappeared behind the tip of the wall.

Then she sat down in her son's hammock. It was still warm.
