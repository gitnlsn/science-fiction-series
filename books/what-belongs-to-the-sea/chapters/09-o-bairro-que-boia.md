By the end of winter, the Market canal had a street.

Joana could see all of it from the clock tower, which was where she climbed now when she
wanted to see Lowtown from above. The Market tower had four sides of dirty glass around a
clock face stopped for fifty years at ten past four, and an iron spiral staircase that
creaked on every step. At the top there was room for two people standing. You could see the
canal from end to end, from Gate Two, lying on the bottom, to the hill, where the canal
curved and disappeared between the houses.

And in the middle of it, on both banks, the houses.

Thirty-one. She counted every time she went up. Thirty-one platforms moored to iron posts,
fifteen on each side, and one in the middle, bigger, which was the circle's. No two alike.
Mrs. Célia's, bamboo and zinc, the first, on the east bank, in front of the green house. Mr.
Lauro's, which was just a plank room on twenty-two blue drums, with a porch bigger than the
room. The one belonging to the young men from Ropemakers, three brothers and a cousin, who
had built a long, low house like a railroad car, out of door wood, with a different door for
every window. Jairo's, the seventh, which he made alone, without asking for help, and which
was the truest of them all, and which had out front, in a tin can, a basil plant.

Between the houses, the bridges. They floated too. Long planks on top of bottles, tied from
one platform to the next with loose rope, that rose and fell with the tide along with the
houses, so that the way was always there, at low tide, resting on the mud, and at high tide,
a meter and a half above it. A street that breathed.

Twice a day, the whole neighborhood rose and fell. Joana had measured the tide on screens for
twenty years. Now she felt it in her stomach, at night, lying down, when her platform began to
rise.

---

Her platform was the nineteenth. On the west side, at the end of the row, near the bend of
the hill. Small. A room and a porch, a gas stove on a box of sand, the bed of two frames, the
table from the loft, which Davi had taken apart and put back together. She had resisted
leaving the boatyard. The boatyard was her father's. Davi had said that was exactly why.

The boatyard was empty now, and it wasn't. Davi had made a workshop of it. The blue tarp had
become a roof again, stretched over the columns, and the ground floor, at low tide, was where
the platforms were built. There was always one up on the sawhorses. Sometimes two. Sometimes
people Joana didn't know, from other streets in Lowtown, standing around a grid of beams,
arguing about a joint with Davi, who was fifteen and spoke to fifty-year-old men the way his
grandfather had, without raising his voice, one thing at a time, never the answer.

The toolbox stayed on the table at the back, open. Nobody touched it without asking.

That was the table where Benedito found Davi, one morning in the middle of winter. Joana was
in the loft and saw the man come in through the door at low tide, thin, with a round belly
and a straw hat with holes in it, dragging a dugout canoe through the mud by the bow. The
canoe was split from end to end, a dark crack that opened and closed as he pulled.

"You fix these here?" he said.

Davi put down the adze. He went to the canoe and ran his hand along the crack, from bow to
stern, and dug his nail into it in three places.

"I can fix it. It'll take a while."

"I'm from the Shallows. Benedito." The man took off his hat and put it back on. "I'll pay in
fish."

"Then pay in fish."

Benedito turned his back and walked off through the mud, without asking how long.

---

People had started coming back in the middle of winter.

Not many, at first. One family in the first week, from a housing development on the Plateau,
who had lived on Quay Street and came down the hill with two suitcases and a fan without
knowing what they would find, and found their house with mud up to the window and the street
out front with two platforms floating on it. The man's name was Gilson. They slept on the
second floor, above the mud, and the fan stayed in its box. Then another family. Then a man
on his own. Then Edson.

Edson from the metal shop, the Civil Defense boy's father. He appeared one Saturday at low
tide, at the door of the boatyard, with a welding machine on his back, tied on with rope, and
a bag of rods, and looked at Davi, and looked at the platform on the sawhorses, and looked at
the steel rings Davi was trying to fix to the beams with bolts and that wouldn't stay firm.

"That needs welding," he said.

"We don't have a welder," said Davi.

Edson took the machine off his back and set it on the floor.

"Now you do."

They didn't. The machine needed an outlet, and in Lowtown the only power was the two old
panels on the Market roof, which barely kept up with the charging line. The next Friday, the
circle bought a diesel generator from a man in the upper city. It cost what there was in the
circle's cookie tin and what Mrs. Ilda took out of a sock, in her room, without letting
anyone see how much. Joana did the math on the rest. A twenty-liter jug for every afternoon
of welding, carried down the hill on the boys' backs, at low tide. Nearly a liter per ring.
The generator sat at the back of the boatyard, on two tires, beating like a heart that
couldn't find its rhythm, and left the shed smelling of burnt oil until the next day.

Edson didn't explain why he had come back. Joana asked once, weeks later, and he shrugged, and
said that on the Plateau the apartment was three rooms on the fourth floor and there was
nothing to weld. Then he said, lower, looking at the weld: *And Amaro died in a boat. And
Amaro's grandson makes boats.* As if one thing explained the other. He was sixty-six.

His son, the Civil Defense boy, came down on Sundays, in plain clothes, and helped weld.

The first post Edson replaced was the one at the mouth of the canal. One windy night, the
platform moored there, the first for anyone coming in past Gate Two, had banged against its
post for hours, and in the morning the post was bent, a hand-span out of plumb, with the top
ring pried open like a fishhook. Edson took two old posts that had fallen on Quay Street, cut
the foot off one and the head off the other, sleeved one into the other with a length of pipe
and welded the splice all around, three passes. It made a post eight meters long. To drive
it, they rigged a tripod of thick bamboo on two canoes lashed together, with the boatyard's
block and tackle at the top and a drum full of concrete hanging from it. Six men hauled on
the rope and let go. The drum came down on the head of the post with a thud Joana could feel
in the planks of her own platform, across the canal.

It took them an afternoon. Half the post went into the mud.

"Why so deep?" Joana asked, from the bridge. "Height isn't the problem. The old one had
plenty to spare."

"It's not for up." Edson slapped the iron with his open hand, and the iron didn't shiver.
"It's for sideways. An old post bends. This one won't."

"And how far does it have to go in?"

"Till it stops sinking."

Joana thought about doing the math. The wave hitting the platform side-on, the lever arm down
to the mud, the moment at the foot of the post, the iron of a fifty-year-old street lamp,
rusted from the inside. She thought about it on the bridge, and at night, lying down. She
didn't do it. Edson started replacing one post a week, beginning at the mouth, and nobody
asked whether it was enough.

---

Mr. Lauro fell sick the week of the post.

Boots in the mud had been a rule of the circle from the start, and Mr. Lauro had never kept
it. He carried the lidded buckets from his street to the pit on the hill, his own and three
other houses', because the others were old, and he carried them barefoot, the way he had
walked all his life, his pants rolled to the knee.

Joana found him one afternoon on the porch of his platform, wrapped in a blanket, with the sun
beating straight down on him. He was shivering. Both his eyes were red, like someone who had
been crying, and he hadn't been.

"It's the flu," he said. "It's my calves that hurt. Like I've been running."

She put her hand on his forehead. It was burning.

On Sunday morning, the Civil Defense boy came down, looked at Mr. Lauro's eyes, squeezed his
calf, and saw the face he made.

"Does he go barefoot?"

Nilo looked at Mr. Lauro's feet.

"That's rats," the boy said. "Rats and mud. He's going up the hill. Today."

They took Mr. Lauro in Amaro's boat to the foot of the hill, and the boy and Nilo carried him
up sitting in a chair, one on each side, to the hospital in the upper city. He was there
twelve days. He came back thinner, yellow, with a cane, and the first thing he did was sit
on his porch and ask for coffee.

On Friday, boots stopped being advice. Whoever didn't have them got them: the Civil Defense
boy came down with eighteen pairs from the depot, nearly all the wrong size. At the same
circle they settled fire, because an ember had burned through the Ropemakers' deck and melted
a hand-span of the foam underneath, with a sweet plastic smell that lasted two days: a stove
only on a box of sand, the box on a sheet of zinc, never on the bare deck. Mr. Lauro pulled
his boots on over two pairs of socks and went back to carrying the buckets.

By the end of winter they were four hundred and sixty. Mrs. Ilda kept count. Not on a list.
In her head.

---

The circle met now on the big platform in the middle of the canal.

They had built it together, in ten days, the biggest of them all: twelve meters by ten, with
the last of the cold-room foam boards and eighty drums, and a high tarp roof on bamboo poles,
open at the sides. In the middle of the canal there were no posts. They moored it to the
stumps of the old footbridge, two concrete piers the sea had left standing when it took the
span, with chain and gate rings, like the others. On Friday nights, on whatever tide there
was, Lowtown went out to it by boat, by bridge, by kayak, swimming, and sat in a circle on
the plank floor, and decided things.

Joana went. She sat wherever there was room. Sometimes beside Mrs. Ilda. Sometimes beside
Jairo, who didn't speak to her, and who passed the thermos of coffee over her, to the person
on her other side.

That Friday, the last of the winter, the circle decided where to go with the next houses.

Joana had an opinion. She had spent the week doing the math: the Market canal was full, both
banks taken up to the bend, and the next natural street was the hill, which flooded less and
had taller buildings to moor to. She had drawn, in her notebook, a second row of platforms
climbing the hill, tied to the façades.

Before she could open her mouth, a woman from the Ropemakers side, whom Joana knew only by
sight and whom everyone called Mrs. Lúcia from Ropemakers, said that the platforms should go
on Quay Street.

"Quay Street's right up against the wall," someone said. "That's where it hits hardest."

"That's exactly why," said Mrs. Lúcia. "We put the houses there, we see the sea. The sea
comes, we know first. In here on the canal we only know when the water reaches the door."

"And when a surge comes?"

"The wall's still there, isn't it?" said Mrs. Lúcia. "It doesn't hold the big tide. But it
breaks the small waves. I've lived behind it forty years. It's a good breakwater. It just
isn't a wall."

There was a silence. A few people looked at Joana.

Joana thought about the Barrier. Three kilometers and two hundred meters of concrete. Useless,
the Department's report had said, against the sea of the next thirty years. She had never
thought of it as anything else.

*A good breakwater. It just isn't a wall.*

"She's right," said Joana.

Mrs. Lúcia looked at her, surprised. She hadn't expected support from there.

The circle decided on Quay Street. Joana put the notebook with the drawing of the hill away in
her backpack. She wasn't sad. She was surprised not to be sad.

Then Mrs. Ilda said who would go there.

She didn't read it. She had no paper. She said it from memory, name by name, and Nilo, beside
her, typed it into the device. First the ones still sleeping on roofs. Then the ones sleeping
on a second floor with water on the first. Then the ones sharing a room: Mr. Lauro's sister,
who slept on his porch; the two families squeezed into the Ropemakers' railroad car.

Joana did the math while she talked. Counting the posts still standing in the canal, on Quay
Street and on the hill, there was room for fifty-one platforms, if there was foam and drums
enough for all of them. Four hundred and sixty people. Nine to a platform. They didn't fit.
People would be sharing rooms until the end of the following year.

"And last," said Mrs. Ilda, "the ones who came back."

Gilson was sitting near the edge, with his wife and their three children. Joana saw him
understand before he heard his own name, the last one.

"I was born on Quay Street," he said. "My house is on Quay Street."

"Was," said Mrs. Ilda. "Whoever stayed, stayed first."

"We went because Civil Defense told us to. We had kids. You know that."

"I know. We stayed because nobody told us anything." She didn't raise her voice. "The ones
who held Lowtown these months were the ones who were here. It's fair."

Joana knew that word. She had made a fair list, with a column for who had already collected.
She thought of the old man from Ropemakers in the water line. *You put your name on a list
and then somebody comes along and crosses it out.* She thought about saying it. She didn't.
The circle wasn't hers to correct; she had learned that sitting on the floor of a terrace,
and she had learned it too well.

Gilson stood up. His wife pulled at his arm, and he let her pull, and the five of them left
by the bridge, one behind the other, the bridge sinking a little under each of them.

At the end, when the circle was breaking up, Mrs. Ilda went past her, leaning on Nilo's arm.

"You had another drawing," she said. It wasn't a question.

"I did."

"Was it good?"

"It was." Joana thought. "Hers is better. She lives there."

Mrs. Ilda patted her hand, the way someone closes a deal at the market.

---

Gilson left on Saturday, at half past six, at low tide.

Joana was on the terrace of the green house, having coffee with Mrs. Ilda, when they went by.
The five of them, on foot through the mud of Caulkers Street, the two suitcases, the fan in
its box on the oldest boy's back. They were heading for the hill. They didn't look at the
platforms. The mud sucked at their boots, and they pulled, and walked.

Mrs. Ilda followed them with her eyes to the bend. She set her cup down on the floor.

"I crossed them out," she said. "Same as they did."

Joana waited.

"Nilo!"

Nilo appeared at the window below, his face creased with sleep.

"The list. Whoever comes, goes on. They sleep wherever there's room until there's a house.
There's no back of the line."

"And the Quay Street ones, Grandma?"

"They go to Quay Street. And whoever comes back goes with them." She picked up her cup again.
"A list is for putting people on now. Not for crossing them out."

Joana looked at the bend in the hill, empty. She had done the math at the circle and kept
quiet. She had thought keeping quiet was listening.

"I saw it last night," she said. "And I didn't speak."

"Next time, speak," said Mrs. Ilda, and drank her coffee.

Gilson didn't come back.

---

At half past eleven, Joana climbed the clock tower, with the tide coming in.

She liked to watch the street rise. From up there, the rising was almost invisible, but if she
looked at one post, one ring, and waited, she could see it. The thirty-one houses, the bridges,
the boats tied up, all of it rising together, unhurried, a finger at a time. People on the
porches. Clara, on Mrs. Célia's platform, hanging laundry on a line that ran from one post to
the other. Jairo watering the basil. Smoke from a wood stove on one of the platforms in the
middle, on its box of sand. Davi and two boys, down at the end, pushing with poles at a new
platform that was coming out of the boatyard through the open door, carried by the water.

Over all of it, flying low, the Council's drone.

It came over at noon, every Saturday, and had for two months. A gray machine the size of a
tray, with four rotors that hummed like a hive, and a camera underneath, which made one full
circuit of Lowtown, thirty meters up, and went back to the upper city. Nobody knew what for.
The boys threw stones at it. They never hit it. Mrs. Ilda said to leave it be, it was a good
thing for the Council to see.

Joana watched the drone pass over the street of houses and thought of the control room, the
wall screen, the flood map. Of someone, in a room in the upper city, watching this on a screen.
Lowtown seen from above, street by street. The streets blue. And in the middle of the blue,
things the model didn't know what to make of, that rose and fell with the water.

The drone left. She stayed.

And at half past twelve, with the tide almost at the top, she saw the boat.

---

It came in at the mouth of the canal, over the fallen Gate Two, slowly, and Joana knew it wasn't
from Lowtown before she saw anything else, because it made no noise at all and no wake. It was
white. A catamaran with two long, slender hulls, about fifteen meters, with a dark-glass cabin
in the middle and a smooth deck around it. Solar panels on the cabin roof, black, matte, fitted
so well they looked like the paint itself. Not a rope out of place. Not a speck of rust. It
slid along the canal as if the canal had been made for it.

The people on the platforms stopped to look. Clara with the laundry in her hand. Jairo with his
watering can. The boys at the end, with their poles.

On the side of the nearer hull, in big dark-blue letters, there was a logo. A square, with a
straight horizontal line across the middle, and below the line the square was filled in, the
color of earth, and above the line it was empty. Beside it, two words.

TERRA FIRMA.

Joana knew the name. Everyone who worked with water knew it. It was the company that had built
the dry neighborhoods of other coasts, farther south, over the last ten years. High dikes around
a piece of low city, pumps, everything dry inside, new buildings, people in pale shirts walking
on sidewalks where, on the other side of the dike, the sea broke three meters above their heads.
She had seen the photos. She had read the technical reports. They were well made. Very well
made. Reading them, she had felt something she didn't like to admit, and it was envy.

The catamaran slowed in front of the big circle platform, in the middle of the canal. It
stopped. A man came out of the dark-glass cabin onto the deck.

White shirt. Gray hair. His hands in his pants pockets, as if the pockets were too deep.

He looked around. The thirty-one houses. The bridges. The rings on the posts, and the new post
at the mouth, thicker than the others. The wood smoke. He looked at one thing at a time, like
someone measuring.

Then he raised his head toward the clock tower, as if he knew exactly where she would be, and
took his hand out of his pocket, and waved.

Raul.
