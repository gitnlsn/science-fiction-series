The Civil Defense boats arrived at eight in the morning, with the tide coming in, and this
time they had police.

Not many. Two men in black vests in each boat, sitting in the stern with their arms crossed,
with the faces of men who would rather have been anywhere else. The boats were the same gray
rubber ones, and the boy in the orange vest who had taken Joana up the Market canal the
morning after the big tide was in one of them, standing in the bow, the laminated tablet in
his hand. He saw her on the roof of the boatyard and raised his hand, and then lowered it,
as if he didn't know whether he was allowed.

Joana had come down at six, at low tide, walking through the mud, with Davi. She hadn't told
him not to come. She didn't anymore.

"Mr. Amaro Amaral," the boy read, from the boat, below the roof. His voice came up half
broken, as if he had rehearsed the sentence and it had come out different. "Caulkers Street,
seventy-two. You're on the relocation list, with a place in Development Four on the Plateau,
Block C. The deadline ended today. I have to ask you to come with us."

Her father was sitting on the ridge, where he liked to sit in the mornings, with a mug of
coffee. He looked at the boy down below for a while.

"Whose son are you?"

The boy blinked.

"Edson's. From the metal shop on Quay Street."

"Edson's on the Plateau?"

"He is. Since the inquiry."

"Does he like it?"

The boy didn't answer. Her father nodded, as if he had.

"Tell him Amaro says hello," he said. And drank his coffee.

---

The police didn't come up. Joana had been afraid they would, and they didn't, and later she
would understand why: they had orders not to take anyone off a roof by force, because an old
man slipping off a roof in front of a camera was a story the Council didn't want. So the
boats went from house to house, and the boy read the name, and heard the answer, and marked
it on the tablet. Some went. Most didn't.

Nilo, Mrs. Ilda's grandson, showed up at ten in a yellow kayak, paddling with a single
paddle, to say that the Civil Defense radio was calling for a surge tonight.

"Not as big as the other one," he said, holding the kayak against the wall of the boatyard
with his hand. "But with Saturday's full moon, the tide's just as high. And now there's no
gate."

Joana did the math. She didn't want to and she did. That night's full-moon tide would come
close to the height of the big tide, some ten centimeters under it. The surge was smaller,
the wall would be overtopped less. But it no longer made any difference how much the wall
was overtopped. The mouth of the Market canal was open. The sea didn't need to come over
anything. It would come in through the door.

On the night of the big tide, the water had reached two and a half meters inside the
boatyard. That night the still water would stand a hand-span below that, at two point three
meters. But it would come with waves, up the canal, and the waves came on top.

The loft floor stood two point nine meters above the floor of the shed, and it would hold.
So would the brick walls of the ground floor. What she didn't know about was the timber above, the old boards that closed in
the shed from halfway up the wall to the roof, and that had never taken a wave.

"Dad," she said. "You're going to Mrs. Ilda's house tonight. It's concrete, it's three
stories."

"I'm staying in mine."

"Dad."

"Ilda can come here, if she wants," he said. "It's higher here."

"It's wood here."

Her father looked at her. For the first time, she saw that he wasn't arguing. He was tired.
That was a different thing.

"Joana," he said. "I was born in this shed. On the floor. Your grandmother didn't make it to
the hospital, because the tide was in and the road was closed, and I was born right
there, where Severino's cradle is. Let me stay."

She went to help Davi with the ropes.

---

The wind started at four in the afternoon.

At first like an ordinary wind, an end-of-afternoon wind, that came off the sea and brought
the smell of salt and seaweed. Then stronger. By five, the clouds were low and dark and
running west, over the upper city, and the rain came sideways, as on the night of the big
tide, hitting the zinc with a noise like gravel. By six, the water, which had gone down in
the afternoon and left the street dry, was rising up the canal again, and it was rising
faster than the tide, pushed.

Davi had spent the afternoon tying things down. The henhouse drums, which he had brought
inside the shed. The bottle frame, which now had planks on top and was almost a floor.
Severino's hull, which he tied to two columns, with two ropes on each side, and slack to
rise, the way his grandfather had shown him.

"Slack," her father had said, from the rail. "A short rope sinks the boat when the water
rises. A long rope lets it bang. It has to be the right amount."

"How much?"

"The height the water rises, and a hand-span."

Davi had looked at his mother. She had said the number. He had measured the rope with his
arm, shoulder to fingertip, and tied it.

At seven, Mrs. Ilda arrived in Nilo's kayak, sitting in the middle with a bag on her lap,
and climbed the wooden stairs taking no one's hand.

"He said it's higher here," she said to Joana, in the loft, taking off her raincoat. "I said
it's wood here. He said I could stay in mine." She looked around, at the table, the bed, the
pots. "So I came."

---

The water came into the shed at eight, through the open door, and rose.

Joana stood at the rail of the loft, with a headlamp, watching the waterline against the
iron columns, which her father had marked with white paint, a hand-span at a time, years
before, to measure the tides of other days. At half past eight, the water was at the
fourth mark. At nine, at the sixth, where an ordinary tide reached. At twenty past nine, at
the eighth. Severino's hull rose with it, quiet, on its
ropes, and the frame of planks and bottles rose too, and the blue henhouse drums, and the
chickens inside, which had stopped complaining.

At nine forty, the first wave came in through the door.

It wasn't an open-sea wave. It was a swell, a body of water that came up the canal, turned
the corner of Caulkers Street and went into the shed as if the shed were a throat. It lifted
everything at once, half a meter, and hit the back wall, and came back. Severino's hull
pulled at its ropes and they held. The plank frame hit a column.

And the whole shed groaned.

Joana felt it in her feet. A tremor that came from the boards of the loft floor, ran up her
legs and was gone. She looked up. The timber of the walls, the wide boards that closed in
the shed from halfway up, was moving. Not much. But moving, all of it, out and in, like a
chest breathing.

The second wave came at nine forty-three. Bigger.

On the canal side, in the wall that faced the street, a board came loose from top to bottom
and fell outward, and through the hole came the wind, and the rain, and the noise of the
sea, which until then had been muffled. Joana's headlamp caught the street outside: there
was no street anymore. There was a wide brown river, running, with things in it.

"Dad."

Her father was sitting on the bed, with Mrs. Ilda beside him. Davi was standing, the toolbox
in his arms, not knowing where to put it.

"Dad, the front wall's going to go. And when it goes, the roof loses its support."

Her father looked at the hole in the wall. Then he looked at Severino's hull, down below,
pulling at its ropes.

"Ilda's house," he said. And got up.

---

What happened next Joana would remember forever in pieces, and never in the right order.

She would remember Davi going down the wooden stairs with the water already at the
next-to-last step, and jumping into Severino's hull, and the hull rocking and coming back.
She would remember putting Mrs. Ilda into the hull, and that
she weighed much less than she looked, and Mrs. Ilda saying *I know how to get into a boat,
girl.* She would remember Nilo untying the yellow kayak and letting it go, because it didn't
fit. She would remember her father coming down the stairs last, facing them, one
step at a time, the way you go down a ladder on a boat, with the water at his chest and the
rain in his face, and sitting in the stern, on the rower's thwart, and taking the oars.

"Dad, let Davi..."

"Davi casts off," her father said. "Stern line first. Then the bow. When I say."

Davi cast off the stern line. The hull swung a little in the current coming through the
door.

"Now."

Davi cast off the bow line.

Her father rowed.

He didn't row hard. Joana would think about that later, many times. He didn't fight the
water. He waited for the next wave to come in through the door, lift the hull, hit the back
of the shed and start back, and when it went back, he gave two strokes, short ones, and the
hull went out the door along with the water going out. As if the wave itself were carrying
it outside. In the street, the current was pushing toward the hill, away from the canal,
and he let it push, and rowed only to correct. One stroke on one side. One on the other. The
rain in his face. Joana's headlamp in the bow catching the windows of the houses, the bars,
the posts. Mrs. Ilda's house was four houses down, on the other side. A three-story concrete
building, painted green, on high fill eight steps above the street, with an iron stair
outside up to the roof terrace.

He reached the iron stair in three strokes. He brought the hull up against it the way you
pull a car into a garage.

Behind them, when Joana looked, the front wall of the boatyard was gone. Not all of it. The
wooden part. The brick of the ground floor was still there, under the water, and the iron
columns, and on top of them the zinc roof, crooked, one corner down, and the wind getting in
underneath it and lifting the sheets one by one, and throwing them into the dark.

---

They climbed to the terrace of the green house, and then to the third-floor room, where
Mrs. Ilda had put a mattress and a camp stove and a picture of a saint on a shelf. Nilo tied
Severino's hull to the iron stair, with slack. Davi put the toolbox in a corner, under the
window, away from the water.

Her father didn't come up.

Joana only noticed once she was in the room. She looked around, counted, and someone was
missing. She ran down the iron stair, with the headlamp, and he was still in the hull,
sitting on the rower's thwart, the oars shipped across his knees, and the rain beating on
his back.

"Dad. Come up."

"In a bit," he said. "Let me sit."

She climbed down into the hull. It rocked a little and came back. She sat on the forward
thwart, facing her father, her knees almost touching his.

He was breathing short. She could see his chest rise and fall too fast, and his mouth open,
and in the light of the headlamp the color of his face was wrong. Gray.

"Dad, are you feeling..."

"I'm feeling the boat," he said. "It's good. It's dry inside."

She put her hand on his wrist. She counted. She lost count. She counted again.

"Davi!" she shouted, upward. "Davi, the radio! Call Civil Defense!"

Up above, someone moved. She heard Nilo's voice. She heard the crank of the radio.

Her father put his hand over hers, the one on his wrist.

"Leave it," he said.

"No."

"Joana." His voice was almost nothing. She had to lean in to hear, and the rain beat on both
of them. "The rope. It had to be the height the water rises, and a hand-span."

"I know, Dad."

"The boy measured it right."

Davi had come down. She didn't see him arrive. He was on the iron stair, holding on to a
step, the water at his waist, looking into the hull.

"Grandpa."

Her father turned his head toward him. It took time. He smiled. The sideways smile, all the
lines in one place.

"I told you," he said. "Every boat floats. The hard part is making one that comes back."

He looked at Mrs. Ilda's house, at the terrace, at the street of brown water running. At the
boatyard they had left, four houses away, without its front wall, its roof crooked, still
standing.

"It came back," he said.

---

The radio got through to Civil Defense at twenty past ten. They said no boat would go into
Lowtown before the tide went down. They said to keep the patient warm and lying down, and to
do chest compressions if he stopped breathing, and explained how, and Joana did them, in the
bottom of the hull, on her knees in the rainwater, with Davi holding the flashlight, and Nilo
counting out loud to thirty, and then again, for forty minutes. Then Mrs. Ilda came down the
iron stair with the water at her chest, and put her hand on Joana's shoulder, and said her
name, and Joana stopped.

The tide turned at ten to midnight.

She stayed in the hull with her father. She wouldn't go up. She covered him with Mrs. Ilda's
raincoat and sat on the forward thwart, with her hand on his knee, while the water went down
the street, and the hull went down with it, slowly, on its ropes, until it settled in the
mud, tilted, in front of the iron stair of the green house.

Davi came down at two in the morning. He sat on the middle thwart, between them. He didn't
say anything. Neither did she. They stayed there.

---

The Civil Defense boat came at seven, with the tide coming in again. It was the same boy in
the orange vest, Edson's son from the metal shop. He saw the hull, and what was in the bottom
of the hull, covered with the raincoat, and took off his cap.

He helped carry her father to the boat. Carefully. Joana would remember that too.

Then he stood in the bow, the tablet in his hand, not turning it on.

"Engineer," he said. "You live in the upper city. I know. But I have to ask, because you're
here. If you want, I'll take you and the boy now, in the boat. And if you want a place on the
Plateau, I..."

"No."

He waited.

Joana looked at Caulkers Street. The water was rising again, a finger at a time. The
boatyard, four houses away, without its front wall, with half its roof, the iron columns
standing with their marks of white paint and the night's mud line past the tenth mark.
Inside, resting on the floor of the shed, the frame of planks and bottles Davi had made for Mrs. Ilda. It had held the whole night, tied to a column. It
was still there. Waiting for the water so it could be something.

"I'm not going to the Plateau," she said. "And I'm not going to the upper city."

The boy looked at her without understanding.

"You can't stay here. It's a retreat zone. There's no water, there's no power..."

"I can," said Joana. "There's just no water or power."

Behind her, on the iron stair, Davi had come down again, with the toolbox in his arms. She
didn't need to look to know.
