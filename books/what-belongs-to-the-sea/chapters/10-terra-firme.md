Inside the catamaran it was cold.

That was the first thing. Joana went in through the dark-glass door of the cabin, from the deck
where the afternoon sun beat on the smooth boards, and the air inside was dry and cold, and
smelled of nothing. Not of salt, not of mud, not of wood smoke, not of fish. Of nothing. She
hadn't smelled that in five months and had forgotten it existed.

The cabin was a long room with a low ceiling, pale leather benches on both sides and a table
in the middle. The table was a screen. It was off, black, reflecting the ceiling. Through the
dark-glass windows, the Market canal looked like somewhere else, bluer, cleaner, like a photo
of itself, and the platforms outside looked like toys.

"Sit," said Raul. "Water? Coffee?"

She wanted water. He took from a cabinet built into the wall a chilled glass bottle, and a
glass, and poured, and she drank, and the water was so cold and tasted so much of nothing that
it hurt her teeth. She thought of the window stills, the bamboo channel a finger above the
edge, the conductivity meter. She drank it all.

Raul sat down on the other side of the table. He had come ten days before, on the Saturday,
and waved from the bow, and she had come down from the clock tower and gone out to the circle
platform, and they had talked for half an hour on the deck, with half of Lowtown watching from
their porches. About nothing. About her father. Had he gone to the funeral? He hadn't been able
to, he was in the south, on a job. About Davi, whom he hadn't seen since the boy was ten.
Then he had left a card, real paper, thick, with the logo of the square cut in half, and said:
*Tuesday the fifth, at three. I'd like to show you something. Just you.*

"You look well," he said.

"I'm living on a raft."

"I know. I saw." He smiled. "You look well."

---

He pressed a corner of the table, and the table lit up.

It was Lowtown. Seen from above, in color, sharp, from now. She recognized it at once: the
Market canal with the thirty-one platforms, the clock tower, the boatyard without its front
wall, the blue tarp, Mrs. Ilda's green house. A photo. From the Saturday before, by the light.

"The drone," she said.

"The drone is the Council's. The images are ours." Raul didn't apologize. "They didn't have
the money for a survey. We did. An arrangement."

He touched the table. The photo tilted, gained height, became a model. Lowtown in three
dimensions, every house at its height, every street at its elevation, and around it the sea, in
a smooth drawing-board blue. And the wall. The Barrier. Three kilometers and two hundred meters
of gray, with Gate Two lying on the bottom of the canal.

Joana looked at the wall in the model and felt the thing she always felt.

"Now," said Raul.

He touched it again.

The wall disappeared. In its place, farther out into the sea, some thirty meters, another one
rose. Not gray. Dark green, with a stone slope on the sea side, in steps, and a wide crest on
top, flat, with trees. It was high. Much higher than the Barrier. Joana saw the number in the
corner of the screen before she saw anything else.

*Crest: +6.00.*

The new wall ran along the coast, from the Quay to past the mouth of the canal, and then turned
inland, at an angle, and climbed toward the upper city along the southern edge of Lowtown, until
it met the hill, at the eight-meter contour. It closed all of Lowtown inside a ring. A bucket.
At the mouth of the canal, where Gate Two had been, a pump station. Not a gate. A station. A big
one. Six pumps.

"We don't close the canal," said Raul. "We close the sea. The canal becomes a lake. The rain
that falls inside the ring, the pumps throw back over the dike. The level inside stays a meter
below mean sea level, constant. Always dry."

He touched it. The blue inside the ring went down. The streets appeared. The mud disappeared.
The platforms disappeared. In their place, on the blocks of Lowtown, buildings rose. White, six
stories, on pilotis, with balconies and green roofs, lined up around the canal that was now a
long lake, with a promenade on each side and trees. Small people walking on the promenade. A
boy on a bicycle.

At the top of the screen, a name lit up. Thin letters, elegant.

*THALASSA FIRMA*

Joana looked at the name. It was the name of the city. The old name, the one nobody used
anymore for anything but stamps on documents and the sign at the bus station, because since
she was a child the city had been the upper city and Lowtown and nothing more. Thalassa. Her
mother used to say it meant sea in an old language. She had never checked.

"Firma," she said.

"It's the brand. All the projects get it." Raul shrugged. "I think it's ugly too."

---

She looked at the model for a long time. Raul didn't speak. He had always known when not to
speak. It was one of the things she remembered from the basement: he could tell when she was
doing a calculation and waited for her to finish.

She was doing several.

The stepped stone slope, on the sea side, broke the wave before it could climb. That was good.
It was much better than a smooth concrete face. The wide crest, twenty meters, with trees,
spread the load and could take overtopping without eroding the landward slope. Good. Six meters
above mean sea level left three point four meters of freeboard on the equinox tide, with a surge
like the one on the night of the big tide. Almost no wave would reach it. Good. The pump station at
the mouth of the canal, with six pumps, and — she touched the table without asking, and the
table obeyed, and opened the section of the station — its own generator, solar and diesel, and a
redundancy of two pumps on standby. Good.

It was good. It was very good. It was what she would have drawn, if they had given her money
and sixteen more years of data.

It was what she had drawn. Or the beginning of it. The five-meter elevation the Council had
erased on a Tuesday. Plus one more meter.

"Who did it?" she asked.

"Terra Firma's design team. Twelve engineers. Four hydraulic." Raul leaned forward, elbows on
the table, over the model, and the light of the screen caught his face from below. "We're
missing a chief engineer."

She raised her eyes.

"Terra Firma has the concession," he said. "The Council approved it last week, in closed
session. Six to three. The retreat zone becomes a concession zone. We build everything, the
dike, the pumps, the buildings. The Council pays nothing. We run the pumps for fifty years and
sell whatever apartments are left over."

"Left over from what?"

"From the exchange." He touched the table, and the model shrank into a corner, and in its place
a text appeared, clean, in columns. "Every resident of the retreat zone who holds title to the
land signs the title over to Terra Firma. In exchange, they get a new apartment, in the same
place, free. The same floor area they had, or more. A new deed. In a building that doesn't
flood."

She read it. She read it again.

"This is the retreat zone, Raul. Does the title hold?"

"It holds. The lawyers went over it three times." He touched a line of the text, which lit up.
"The Shore Law only takes what lies under high water, and the high-water line was measured
forty-two years ago and runs along the beach, outside the wall. On paper, all of Lowtown is dry
land."

"And whoever doesn't have title?"

Raul didn't look away.

"By our count, about forty percent. Tenants. People who moved into empty houses. Inheritances
that were never transferred." He opened his hands. "They get priority to buy, at a discount,
with thirty-year financing. And a place on the Plateau, which they already have today."

"Forty percent."

"Joana." His voice changed. It got lower, closer. The voice from the Council corridor, on the day
of the inquiry. "Today it's a hundred percent. Today, a hundred percent of the people on those
rafts have nothing. No running water. No power. No school. They're living in a place the Council
has declared doesn't exist. I'm offering a real home to sixty percent of them. Free."

"And the pumps?"

"What?"

"The pumps." She pointed at the corner of the table, where the model had shrunk. "The level
inside a meter below the sea. Forever. If the pumps stop, it fills. Who pays for the pumps?"

"Condo fees." He didn't hesitate. "It's in the contract. It's low. Less than the Department
used to charge for water."

"And if Terra Firma goes bust? If it leaves? In twenty years, in fifty?"

"Then the Council takes over. That's in the contract too."

"The Council that didn't fix Gate Two."

Raul ran his hand over the tabletop, where there was nothing to wipe.

"You're doing the right math," he said. "I knew you would. That's why I want you. I don't want
an engineer who says yes. I want you, who's going to ask all those questions, and then build the
dike in a way that keeps them from being a problem."

---

He touched the table one last time, and the model came back, big, in the middle. But it wasn't
the model of the dike. It was Lowtown as it was now. The platforms, the bridges, the blue tarp.
And over it, in the corner, the date of a tide.

"The next equinox," said Raul. "New moon again. The tide table puts it ten centimeters above this
year's big tide. No way to know if a surge comes with it. But if it comes like the last one..."

The blue rose in the model. Slowly, as on the flood map in the control room. It filled the
canal. The platforms rose on their posts, and there was post to spare. It wasn't the height. And
Joana saw, before the model showed it, what was going to happen, because she already knew: the
wave that came in at the mouth of the canal, the one that had torn the front wall off the
boatyard, came in sideways, with the current behind it, and struck the first platforms like a
hull lying broadside. The posts bent. The rings opened.

In the model, the platforms at the mouth of the canal tore their rings off the posts. One, two.
They went into the others.

Raul switched off the table before the rest.

"I'm not saying it'll happen," he said. "I'm saying it can. And that you know it. And that what
you've all made is beautiful, Joana. It's the most beautiful thing I've seen in ten years of
coastline. I stood watching from the bow that Saturday, the houses rising, and I almost cried.
But it won't survive the next big tide. And you know that too."

She knew. She had known it somewhere under her thinking since the night she saw Mrs. Célia's
house keel over in the canal. The posts were lamp posts, old, six meters, planted in the street
to hold up a bulb, not a house shoved sideways. Nobody had done the math on the posts.

"I need to think," she said.

"Of course." He got up. "You have until the end of the month. Terra Firma is going to present the
proposal to the residents at an open meeting, on the first. They vote. If they accept, work
starts a week later." He stopped at the dark-glass door. "I'd like you to be there, beside
me. But if you're not, I understand."

She stayed sitting.

"Raul."

"Hm."

"Why me?"

He stood with his hand on the door.

"Because it's your wall," he said. "The one they didn't let you build. I'm giving you the chance
to build it. Right. With everything. With the eighty centimeters and another meter."

He opened the door. The heat of the deck came in, and the smell of salt, and of mud, and of wood
smoke.

"Nobody deserves it more than you," he said.

---

She went back to her platform over the bridges, with the tide coming in. The bridges swayed a little
underfoot, and she had learned to walk on them without thinking, her knees loose. She passed
Jairo's platform. He was sitting on his porch, mending a net, and raised his eyes when she went
by, and looked toward the catamaran, white, sitting in the middle of the canal, and pulled the net needle through harder.

On her platform, Davi was sitting on the porch floor, legs over the edge, feet in the water,
sanding a new oar.

"So?" he asked.

"Nothing. He wanted to show me a project."

"What project?"

"Later."

She went into the room, and opened her backpack, and took from the bottom,
from inside an old plastic folder, a printed sheet, folded in four, yellow at the folds. A
spreadsheet. Sixteen years old. With the math for the five-meter elevation, and for the
four-twenty, and for the city with no wall. She had kept that sheet in every drawer of every place
she had lived.

She opened the sheet on the bed. She looked at the numbers. Hers. Right.

Outside, through the mesh window, the white catamaran was starting out along the canal, without
noise, without a wake, with the low sun striking the dark glass of the cabin.

Her hands shook on the sheet.
