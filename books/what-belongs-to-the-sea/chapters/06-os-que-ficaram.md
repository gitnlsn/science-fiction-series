Lowtown had two maps, and it took Joana three weeks to learn the second.

The first she knew by heart. It was on the wall of the control room, in the drawings in the
drawer, in her head: streets, blocks, elevations, the Market canal down the middle like a
spine. It was the low-tide map. It worked from seven in the morning to ten, and from seven
at night to ten, more or less, when the mud dried enough to walk on in boots, sinking to the
ankle, and the streets were streets.

The second map was the high-tide map, and it wasn't anywhere. It was in the people.

It was made of roofs. Of wooden planks stretched from one ledge to another, over the
streets, three, four meters up, with a hand rope along the side. Of iron stairs and rope
ladders. Of second-floor windows that had turned into doors, with a brick step outside for
whoever arrived by boat. Of dinghies, canoes, kayaks, an old iron bathtub with an oar in
it, and Severino's hull, which everyone now called *Amaro's boat*, and which went up and
down the Market canal with Davi at the oars, carrying people and things from one side to
the other, twice a day, at high water.

By that second map, Caulkers Street was ten minutes from the Market, over the roofs, if you
knew the way. Joana didn't. She went by boat, with her son.

"That's Mr. Lauro's," said Davi, pointing with his chin at a long green plank that joined two
terraces over the hill. "He put it up. It was his kitchen door."

She looked. It was. It still had the handle.

---

Her father had been dead nineteen days.

They had buried him in the upper city, in the cemetery on the hill, on a sunny Wednesday,
with few people. Joana had thought there would be few people, and had been surprised to
arrive and see the cemetery gate crowded, and then had understood: it was Lowtown. People
she didn't know, their pants muddy to the knee, because they had come down at low tide and
walked up the hill. Mrs. Ilda. Nilo. Mr. Lauro, out of his splint now. The Ferreiras. The
Civil Defense boy, in plain clothes, at the back. An old man she didn't recognize who said
afterward that he was Severino.

Nobody made a speech. Before it went down, Mrs. Ilda put on the coffin a piece of mooring
rope, coiled, the length of an arm. Joana didn't ask why.

Afterward they had gone back to Lowtown, she and Davi, and hadn't gone up to live again.

The apartment in the upper city was shut. She was still paying the rent, out of fear, or out
of not wanting to decide. They went once a week to take a shower and wash clothes and
charge the devices, and Davi went to school three days a week, and she didn't have a job
anymore, and their savings would last a year, a year and a half, if nobody got sick.

They lived in the boatyard loft. What was left of it. Davi had stretched a big blue tarp over
the iron columns, where the roof used to be, and tied it at the corners with caulker's
cord, and closed up the front wall that had gone with boards he found floating in the
street, uneven, some from doors, one from a table. Inside, the bed of two frames, the table,
the gas stove. On the wooden back wall, the empty nails where the tools had been.

Joana hadn't taken out the nails. Neither had Davi.

---

The circle met at the Market.

The Market building stood in the middle of Lowtown, at the edge of the canal, and it was the
oldest thing in the neighborhood: a two-story hall of iron and glass, with an arched roof
and a clock tower at the front that hadn't run since before Joana was born. The ground floor
had been fish and meat. Now it took the tide, like everything, and smelled of it. But the
second floor was a wide gallery with an iron floor that ran around the central well, and
there, over the last two months, without anyone having decided it, the people of Lowtown had
started to meet.

Davi brought Amaro's boat up against a column on the canal side, where an iron ladder was
fixed to the outside. The tide was in, and the water was a meter and a bit below the gallery
floor. Up above, Joana could hear voices. A lot of them.

"Do they know I'm coming?" she asked.

Davi tied up the boat. With slack.

"Mrs. Ilda knows."

---

There were more than a hundred. Joana counted without meaning to, the way she counted
everything, as she climbed the ladder and put her head level with the iron floor: people
sitting on the floor, on crates, on plastic chairs, on market benches, around the central
well, on all four sides of the gallery, their backs against the railing. People standing
behind. Children running in the back corridor. A dog. Old people, a lot of them.
Middle-aged women. Young men. Few people her age. Almost nobody who had anything to do in the
upper city, she would understand later: whoever had a job had gone. Whoever had stayed was
whoever had only the house.

Mrs. Ilda was sitting on a market crate, beside the well, with Nilo behind her. She looked at
Joana when she appeared on the ladder, and made no gesture.

The voices stopped. Not all at once. Like a wave losing strength on the sand: the nearest
first, then the ones across the well, then the ones at the back. Until the only sound was
the children in the corridor, and the water slapping the columns below, and a pigeon on
some beam.

Joana climbed the last rung and stood on the iron floor.

"It's the one with the wall," someone said, on the other side. Not loudly. But the gallery
echoed.

---

She stood still. She didn't know whether she should sit, and there was no room near her.
Davi had climbed up behind her and was beside her, a little back.

A man got up from the middle of a group on the left. He was about fifty, short and very
strong, thick-armed and round-shouldered, with the burnt skin of someone who had worked
outdoors his whole life. Joana didn't know him. Or knew him by sight, from some corner, from
some year.

"I'm Jairo," he said. "From Ropemakers, number eighteen."

She nodded.

"My wife is in the cemetery on the hill. Two rows from your father." He spoke slowly, without
shouting, and his hands were closed at his sides. "She was on the ground floor when the
water came from the canal. She'd gone down to get her blood-pressure pills. I was upstairs.
I heard the bang. When I got down the stairs, the water was at my chest and there was nobody
on the ground floor anymore."

Nobody moved.

"I went to the inquiry," said Jairo. "I heard you talk. You talked nice. The numbers. You
said you wouldn't say how many would have died without your wall, because it wasn't fair to
the thirty-one." He stopped. "My wife is one of the thirty-one. And I wanted to tell you
that I don't care how many would have died. It doesn't interest me. I wish you'd refused. I
wish there'd been no wall at all, and that everybody here had known there wasn't, and that
we'd left sixteen years ago, each of us wherever we could. Instead there was your wall. And
we stayed. Because there was your wall."

Joana didn't answer. She had no answer.

It was the thing she had never thought of. In sixteen years, not once. She had done the math
for the city with no wall, and for the city with the low wall, and for the city with the high
wall. She had never done the math for the people who stayed *because of* the wall. Who
bought the house, who opened the bakery, who didn't leave, because there was a gray line of
concrete between them and the sea, and someone in a shirt with a broken wave on the chest
had said it would hold.

Her father had stayed. *Your wall's still there, isn't it?*

"You're right," she said.

Jairo blinked. He hadn't expected it. His hand opened, slowly, at his side.

"I'm not going to apologize," said Joana, "because it's no use to anyone, and I don't know
which thing I'd be apologizing for. But you're right. The wall made you stay. I'd never
thought of that. I'm thinking of it now."

Jairo stood looking at her for a long time. Then he sat down, slowly, in the middle of his
group, and said nothing more. One of the men beside him put a hand on his shoulder.

---

Mrs. Ilda got up from the crate. She was short enough that, standing, she was almost the
height of the people sitting in chairs. She didn't need to ask for quiet.

"She's here because I asked her," said Mrs. Ilda. "Nobody has to like it. I don't like it
either. But I saw her do forty minutes of compressions on her father's chest inside a boat,
on her knees in the water, until I told her to stop. Somebody who does that doesn't leave in
the middle."

A few people shifted. Nobody spoke.

"We've got a problem," said Mrs. Ilda, turning to Joana. "Just one. The rest we can manage.
Food we buy in the upper city, or catch, or grow in cans. Power we don't need, we've got
flashlights and we've got sun. The garbage we burn, God forgive me. The doctor, Nilo takes
whoever needs it by boat to the hill, and from there they walk up." She counted on her
fingers, slowly. "What we don't have is water."

"The rain tanks..."

"Went salty." It was a woman at the back who said it. "On the night of the big tide, and
again in the surge. The seawater got into every tank below the roof. Whoever had a tank on
the roof is drinking. Whoever had a tank on the ground is buying."

"Buying from whom?"

"From the upper city," said Mrs. Ilda. "Twenty-liter jugs. Nilo and the boys carry them down
the hill on their backs, at low tide, and bring them over by boat. It's costing more than
food. And when the rain stops, the roof tanks dry up too." She paused. "When the rain stops,
we'll have to choose between drinking and eating. And then the upper city will win, and we'll
go to the Plateau, one at a time, and the retreat zone will retreat on its own. Which is what
the Council wants."

The gallery was silent. Down below, the tide had stopped rising. Joana knew the sound of
still water against iron columns: a small, irregular tick, from the metal cooling where the
water touched it.

"You're a water engineer," said Mrs. Ilda. "That's what your mother used to tell everyone
who went by the school gate. *My daughter's going to be a water engineer.* I sold tapioca
next to her and heard it every day."

Joana hadn't known that. She hadn't known her mother said it to anyone.

"There's water all around us," said Mrs. Ilda. "Twice a day. None of it fit to drink."

She looked at Joana. The whole gallery looked at Joana.

"We need fresh water," said Mrs. Ilda. "For three hundred people, which is how many we are,
counting the roofs that didn't come. It isn't forever. It's until somebody invents something
better. Do you know how to make it?"

---

Joana thought before she answered. She always thought first. Her father used to say she
answered a question ten seconds after everyone else and that it was worth the wait.

Three hundred people. Three liters a day each, just for drinking and cooking, no bathing, no
washing anything. Nine hundred liters a day. The sea was right there, free, twice a day. So
was the sun. A solar still, the simplest there was, a shallow tank of salt water painted
black inside with a sloping pane of glass on top, made three, four liters a day per square
meter, on a good sunny day. On a cloudy day, one. On a rainy day, nothing, but on a rainy day
there was the rain.

Nine hundred liters, at three per meter, was three hundred square meters of glass.

Three hundred square meters of glass in Lowtown. She thought of the smashed shop windows. Of
Mr. Laércio's bakery. Of the house windows the sea had burst. Of the panes in the Market's
own arched roof, over her head, half broken, half whole. Of the boatyard, of the cans of
black paint her father kept in the corner of the ground floor, up on sawhorses, above the
waterline.

"I do," she said.

Mrs. Ilda nodded, once, like someone closing a deal at the market.

"Then start tomorrow," she said. And she sat down on her crate again.

The noise came back to the gallery. Not all at once. The nearest first, then the ones across
the well. The children had never stopped. The pigeon left the beam and flew out through the
broken glass of the roof.

Joana stayed standing where she was. Davi leaned his shoulder against hers.

"Do you really know how?" he asked, quietly.

"I know how to make one," she said. "I don't know how to make three hundred meters."

"So?"

She looked at the gallery. The hundred faces. Jairo, sitting in the middle of his group,
looking at the floor. Mrs. Ilda on her crate, already talking to a woman beside her about
something else, as if the water were settled.

"So I'm not going to make it alone," she said.
