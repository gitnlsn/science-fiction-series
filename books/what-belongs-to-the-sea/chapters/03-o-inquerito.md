The Council chamber was on the top floor of the tallest building in the upper city, and it
had been built, Joana knew, so that you could see the sea from it.

It was a long room with a low ceiling, a horseshoe table of pale wood and one whole wall of
glass facing east. Behind the horseshoe, in high-backed chairs, sat the nine councilors.
In front of it, a small table with a microphone and a chair, for whoever was speaking.
Behind the small table, rows of plastic chairs, all of them taken, and people standing at
the back, leaning against the wall, and people outside in the corridor, looking over
shoulders. From the window, that morning, the sea was smooth and blue and innocent, and
between it and the room, far below, the gray wall ran along the coast like a pencil line,
and behind it Lowtown was a brown stain.

The tide was high. Joana didn't need to look at the clock to know.

She was sitting in the front row, in the long-sleeved Department shirt, buttoned to the
top. She had spent the night before choosing that shirt and then giving up on it and then
choosing it again, because taking the logo off her chest felt like running, and wearing it
felt like having no shame at all. In the end, she had worn it. The wave broken by a
straight line.

The inquiry was in its third day. It was her turn.

---

Councilor Marta Viegas presided. She was a woman of sixty, thin, with gray hair pinned in a
bun, who had been a math teacher before politics and still spoke like someone grading a
test: slowly, pointing out each mistake with the pencil.

"Engineer Joana. You were the lead designer of the Barrier."

"I was."

"Of the wall, the three gates and the pump station."

"Of the wall and the gates. The pump station was another team, with my numbers."

"Could you tell the Council, in your own words, what happened on the night of Friday the
thirty-first."

She had rehearsed. In front of the bathroom mirror, at two in the morning, with the tap
dripping. And when she opened her mouth, she used none of what she had rehearsed.

She said that the tide that night was the biggest of the year, a new-moon tide, close to
the equinox, and that the surge was coming from the southeast with waves of more than four
meters. She said that the wall had been overtopped from ten forty on, and that the
overtopping had gone past the capacity of the pumps at five past eleven. She said that the
water coming over ran down the streets and gathered at the lowest point, which was the
Market canal. She said that the canal had filled from the inside, with Gate Two closed,
and that the gate had been left with water on both sides, and with waves hitting one of
them. She said that the gate had held, and that the axle bearing, on the east side, had
not.

She spoke slowly. She used the numbers. She didn't look at the audience. The whole room
stayed silent while she spoke, a silence that wasn't respect, she realized partway
through, but waiting, as if everyone knew the important part hadn't come yet.

"Was the gate designed for that condition?" asked Councilor Viegas. "Water on both sides?"

"No."

"Why not?"

Joana looked at the window. At the pencil line.

"Because, in the design, the wall didn't let enough water over to fill the canal from the
inside."

"In the design."

"In my design."

Councilor Viegas didn't say anything. She moved a paper in front of her, slowly, and Joana
knew that she already knew, and that she had waited three days to get there.

---

The document appeared on the wall of the chamber, enlarged, beside the window. It was the
minutes of a Council meeting from sixteen years before. A Tuesday. The paper had been
scanned from a printed copy, and you could see the mark of a paper clip in the corner, and
a round coffee stain.

*Item 4. Shore Barrier. Cost review.*

Joana knew the text. She had read it once, at the time, in a corridor, standing up, and
never again. She didn't need to read it again. But she read it, because it was there on
the wall, the size of a door, and the whole room was reading it with her.

*The technical advisory staff of this Council submits a favorable report on reducing the
crest elevation from 5.00 m to 4.20 m above mean level, with an estimated saving of 22% in
the volume of concrete. The report considers the residual risk of overtopping acceptable
under the tide and sea-level-rise scenarios adopted for the design horizon. Approved by six
votes to three.*

Below, the nine signatures of the councilors of the time. None of them was in the room
anymore. Two had died. One, Joana knew, now lived inland, in a house with a pool.

"You were aware of this report," said Councilor Viegas.

"I was."

"Who wrote it?"

"I don't know. The technical advisory staff didn't sign reports individually. They signed
as the staff."

"Did you agree with it?"

"No."

"Did you record your disagreement?"

"I did. In writing. To my director. I sent the spreadsheet with the math." She breathed.
"My director forwarded it to the Council. The spreadsheet is in the file. You must have
read it."

"I have." Councilor Viegas pressed a button, and the minutes left the wall, and another
paper took their place. A technical drawing. A section of the wall, with the crest
elevation written by hand at the top, in a handwriting Joana knew better than her own
face. *Crest: +4.20.* And in the lower right corner, in the title block, beside *Designer
of record*, her signature. "And after sending the spreadsheet, you signed this."

The room made a noise. Not a loud one. The noise of two hundred people shifting in their
chairs at the same time.

---

Joana didn't answer right away.

She had been thirty-one when she signed that drawing. She remembered the day. A hot
Friday, in the Department's drafting room, which was in the basement and had no window.
The director had come down himself, which he never did, and put the drawing on her desk,
and said, without sitting down: *It's this one or none. The Council won't approve another.
You choose.*

She had chosen. She had spent the whole weekend doing another calculation, that of a city
with no wall at all, and on Monday morning she had signed.

"I signed," she said, into the microphone. Her voice came out steady, and that surprised
her a little. "Because the alternative was no wall. I did the math for both. The low wall
held almost every tide in the design horizon. It didn't hold the biggest. I knew that. I
wrote that. And I signed, because almost every one is more than none."

"And on the night of the thirty-first," said Councilor Viegas, "the biggest came."

"It came."

"With eighty more centimeters of wall, would Gate Two have held?"

Joana was silent. The room was silent with her.

That was the question. She had known it would come, had known since the night in the
tower, and had spent twenty days doing the math and redoing it, with the real data from
the tide gauge and the buoys, before dawn, at the kitchen table. She had arrived at a
number. It wasn't a good number or a bad number. It was a number with a band around it.

"Probably," she said. "The overtopping would have been between a fifth and a third of what
it was. The pumps would have handled most of it. The canal would have risen less. But I
can't say yes. The surge that night was above the biggest in the design horizon, even of
the original design. I can say *probably*. I can't say *yes*."

"So you don't know."

"No. Nobody knows. I'll spend the rest of my life not knowing."

Councilor Viegas looked at her over her glasses for a long time. Then she took the glasses
off.

"Engineer, I'm going to ask the question this whole room wants to ask, and I apologize for
it. You could have refused to sign."

"I could have."

"And there would have been no wall."

"No."

"And with no wall at all, how many would have died that night?"

Joana looked at her. Then, for the first time, she turned in her chair and looked at the
room. At the plastic rows. At the people leaning on the back wall. She recognized some
faces. The woman with the baby from Caulkers Street. Mr. Lauro, his arm still in its
splint. Two young men who had been sitting on a ledge on Ropemakers Street with their legs
hanging out. Mrs. Ilda, short and broad, in the third row, arms crossed, looking at her
without blinking.

She had the answer. It was in the spreadsheet she had made that weekend, sixteen years
before, the one for the city with no wall. It was in the file. Councilor Viegas must have
read it too.

"More," said Joana. "Many more."

"How many?"

"I won't say the number." She turned back to the horseshoe. "It isn't fair to the
thirty-one."

---

Téo spoke after her. She hadn't expected it. He hadn't been on the first day's list, and
she found out later that he had asked to speak.

He sat in the small chair, in a Department shirt too, and told the night his own way,
without numbers, the way an operator tells it. Buoy seven going dark. Feeder two going
down. The green line climbing.

"The engineer said something at ten forty," he said, at the end. "I asked if the wall
would hold. She said: *It's going to get wet.* I asked how wet. She said: *Wetter than the
pumps can clear.*" He stopped. "She knew an hour before. She knew sixteen years before. I
was beside her the whole night and she didn't pretend about anything, not once. I just
wanted that on the record."

Councilor Viegas thanked him. She had it put on the record.

Joana looked at her own hands.

---

The vote came in the afternoon, after the break, and it wasn't about her.

It was about Lowtown.

The Department's report, which she had helped write over the last twenty days, said three
things, and Joana could recite all three with her eyes closed. The first: rebuilding Gate
Two would cost what the whole wall had cost sixteen years before, adjusted. The second:
rebuilding the gate wasn't enough, because the wall, even with a new gate, would be
overtopped again at the next big tide with a surge, and the next one, by the model's
reckoning, would come in less than three years, and after that more often. The third: a
wall that could hold the sea of the next thirty years needed a six-meter crest and new
foundations, and would cost four times what the city spent in a year on everything.

The conclusion wasn't in the report. The conclusion was the Council's.

Joana heard it from her seat in the front row, no longer in the Department shirt, which
she had taken off in the bathroom during the break and folded and put in her backpack,
because all of a sudden she couldn't anymore.

"*The area of the lower city lying between the Shore Barrier and the four-meter contour,
as shown on the attached map, is hereby declared a retreat zone,*" read Councilor Viegas,
from the paper. "*In the retreat zone, from twenty-five days after this date, the supply of
treated water and electricity and the collection of waste will cease, and health and
education services will be provided exclusively in the upper city. Residents of the
retreat zone will be offered relocation places in the housing estates of the Plateau, with
transport and moving costs paid by the Council. Gate Two will not be rebuilt.*"

Six votes to three.

The room made no noise at all this time.

Joana turned around again. She looked for Mrs. Ilda in the third row. Mrs. Ilda was
standing. She hadn't stood up to leave. She was standing in the middle of the plastic
chairs, short, arms crossed, looking at the horseshoe, and the people around her, one by
one, stood up too. The young men from the ledge. Mr. Lauro, with his arm in the splint. The
woman with the baby. People Joana didn't know. Nobody shouted. Nobody said anything. They
stood, looking at the Council, for as long as it took Councilor Viegas to say that the
session was closed, and then a little longer, and then they went out, slowly, through the
corridor.

Mrs. Ilda passed the front row without looking at Joana.

At the door, she turned.

"Your father's still on the roof," she said. Not loudly. In the voice of someone passing
on information.

And she left.

---

Joana waited for the room to empty. She didn't know why she waited. Maybe so she wouldn't
have to walk down the corridor in the middle of everyone. Maybe because the window was
still there, with the sea and the pencil line and the brown stain, and she hadn't finished
looking.

When the room was empty, she went up to the horseshoe. Councilor Viegas was putting papers
into a folder. Joana took her Department badge out of her pocket, a thin plastic card with
her photo from ten years before, her hair shorter, and put it on the table in front of
her.

Councilor Viegas looked at the badge. Then she looked at her.

"Nobody asked for this, Engineer."

"I know."

"The inquiry report is going to say the failure was one of decision, not of design. It's
written. Your name isn't in the conclusion."

"My signature is on the drawing."

Councilor Viegas was quiet for a while. Then she picked up the badge and put it in the
folder with the other papers, without saying anything, and Joana liked her for that more
than she had liked anyone that day.

---

In the corridor, leaning on the wall beside the elevator, a man was waiting.

She recognized him before he turned. By his back, by his shoulders, by the way he pushed
his hands into the pockets of his coat as if the pockets were too deep. Raul. Fifty, his
hair all gray now, a well-cut dark coat that hadn't been a Department employee's coat for a
long time. They had worked side by side for eleven years, in the same windowless room in
the basement. He had left for the private sector when Davi was six, and after that they saw
each other twice a year, at a birthday, at a wake, and it was always as if it had been last
week.

"I watched on the screen," he said. "Out here. I didn't want to come in. I figured you
wouldn't want one more face."

She didn't say anything. He took his hands out of his pockets and opened his arms, and she
walked into them without thinking, and stayed there, her forehead on the shoulder of the
dark coat, which smelled of rain and of an expensive lotion, and for the first time in
twenty days felt her legs not quite hold.

"It wasn't you," Raul said, low, close to her ear. "Joana. Listen. It wasn't you. It was a
room of nine people on a Tuesday. You did the math right."

"I signed."

"Everybody signs." He held her away a little, his hands on her shoulders, and looked at her
with that face she knew from eleven years in the basement, the face of someone who has
already thought of everything and is waiting for you to get there. "You signed because it
was that or nothing. I was there. I remember."

She nodded. She didn't ask where exactly he had been, back then. She remembered him in the
basement, at the next desk, with headphones on, doing something else. She didn't remember
much more.

"Where are you going now?" he asked.

She looked out the window at the end of the corridor. You could see the sea from there too.
The brown stain of Lowtown was getting smaller, slowly, as the tide went down, and the
streets were appearing, made of mud.

"To my father's roof," she said.
