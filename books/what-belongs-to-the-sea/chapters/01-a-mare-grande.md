The Barrier was four meters twenty high, and Joana knew by heart the eighty
centimeters it was missing.

She didn't think about them every day. She had gone years without thinking about them,
the way you go years without thinking about a tooth that only hurts with something cold.
But that night, at ten past nine, in the control room at the top of the tower, with the
rain hitting the glass sideways and the whole building humming low in the wind, she
looked at the line of the crest drawn on the big monitor and saw, above it, without
meaning to, the other line. The one she had drawn first. Five meters even above mean sea
level. The one the Council had erased at a Tuesday meeting sixteen years before, to make
it fit the budget.

"Buoy seven stopped sending," said Téo.

He was at the other desk, his back to her, the duty jacket still wet on his shoulders.
He was thirty-something and had the calm of someone who had never seen a truly bad
night. Joana liked him for that, and was a little afraid of him for it too.

"When?"

"Just now. Last reading was four meters thirty of wave."

"It didn't stop. It sank or it broke its mooring." Joana pulled the buoy panel onto her
own screen. Seven yellow dots scattered on the dark sea, eight, twelve, twenty
kilometers out, each one blinking its number. Seven was gray. "What are the others
giving?"

"Four ten. Four and a half, at twelve kilometers."

She didn't say anything. She didn't need to. They both knew how to do the math.

---

The tower stood at the tip of the Quay, where the Barrier turned south and became a wall
against the open sea. It was an eight-story concrete cylinder with the control room at
the top, and from the big window, by day, you could see the whole wall: three kilometers
and two hundred meters of gray concrete running along the coast, with the sea on one side
and the lower city on the other, and the three gates opening the mouths of the canals,
each with its curved steel gate, painted orange. At night you saw nothing. Only the rain
on the glass, and now and then, when a wave hit the base of the tower, a white flare of
spray climbing to the third floor and coming apart in the wind.

And the monitors.

The wall of the room was a single screen, curved, from end to end, and Joana knew every
piece of it the way she knew her own kitchen. On the left, the tide gauge: a blue line
climbing slowly up the grid, the real tide, and over it a dashed line, the predicted
tide, which went up to the peak and came down again. The peak was marked with a red
stroke. *High water 23:52.* In the middle, the wall drawn in section, with the crest at
four twenty, and the water rising against it in real time, a paler blue that trembled
with the waves. On the right, the three gates, with the sensors for each: pressure, steel
temperature, and deflection, a thin green line that said how much each gate was giving,
in millimeters, under the weight of the sea.

And below, taking up the widest part of the wall, the map.

The flood map was the lower city seen from above, street by street, the buildings in gray
and the streets in white. When water got into a street, the street turned blue. The model
ran on its own, every thirty seconds, working out where the water would be if the wall
were overtopped, and showed the forecast over it in pale blue. That night, at ten past
nine, the forecast covered only the edge of the Quay. A thin strip, one block deep.

Joana had helped calibrate that model. She knew what it knew and what it didn't. It knew
about water. It didn't know about steel.

---

At half past nine, she called home.

Davi picked up on the second ring, which meant he had the device in his hand, which meant
he was awake, watching the same thing she was. He was fifteen. The screen lit up with his
face too close, his hair wet, the blue light of his room behind him.

"Bad out there?" he asked.

"It's windy. Did you go out in the rain?"

"I went on the balcony." He turned the camera, and she saw the upper city at night, the
yellow streetlights running down the hill, and far below, at the end of them, a darkness
that was Lowtown, and past Lowtown a thin white line that moved. "You can see the spray
from here. Hitting the wall."

"Get off the balcony."

"I'm inside." He turned the camera back on himself. "Mom. Is Grandpa down there?"

She didn't answer right away.

"Did you call him?" Davi pressed.

"I'm going to."

"He won't leave."

"I know he won't leave. I'm calling anyway. Go to sleep."

"Are you going to sleep?"

She almost laughed.

"Go to sleep, Davi."

He hung up without saying goodbye, the way he had since he was thirteen, and she spent a
second looking at her own reflection in the dark screen. Forty-seven years old. Her hair
tied back any old way, the long-sleeved Department shirt with the Barrier logo
embroidered on the chest, a wave broken by a straight line. She had drawn the logo too.
One afternoon, in five minutes, on a napkin, before anyone asked.

---

Her father picked up on the sixth ring, with the camera off. He never turned the camera
on. He said his face hadn't changed since the last time.

"Joana."

"Dad. Can you hear the wind?"

"I can hear the sea. The wind I hear every day."

Behind his voice there was a thick, steady noise that took her a second to place: the
rain on the zinc roof of the boatyard. She knew the noise. She had slept under it until
she was eighteen, in a hammock in the loft, above the hulls.

"The forecast is bad," she said. "New-moon tide, the biggest of the year. And the surge
on top of it."

"I can read an almanac."

"The Department opened the shelter at the school in the upper city. There's room. I'll
send a car to pick you up at the end of Caulkers Street, in ten minutes..."

"This house saw the tide of sixty-one."

"In sixty-one the sea was a hand-span lower, Dad."

He went quiet. She could hear him breathing, short breaths, the breathing of a
seventy-eight-year-old man with a heart the doctors called tired, and over it the rain
on the zinc.

"Your wall's still there, isn't it?" he said at last.

It wasn't a question. Or it was, but not in the way she wanted to answer.

"It is."

"Well, then."

And he hung up. The same way his grandson did. She thought, with a quick anger that was
almost funny, that the two of them had learned it from each other, and that she, in the
middle, had never learned to hang up first.

---

At ten forty, the first wave went over.

It wasn't something you could see from the window. It was something you saw on the
middle screen: the pale blue of the water, trembling against the drawing of the wall,
suddenly rose in a narrow peak, touched the line of the crest, and a thread of blue ran
down the other side. It lasted a second. The overtopping sensor, a narrow channel on top
of the wall every hundred meters, measured it and sent the number: *0.4 L/s/m.* Four
tenths of a liter per second, per meter of wall.

"It's started," said Téo. He turned his chair toward her. "Four tenths."

"That's spray." Joana didn't take her eyes off the screen. "At four tenths, someone
walking underneath gets a shower and stays on their feet."

"And at how much do they not?"

"At fifty, the water knocks you down. At a hundred, the wall starts to suffer at the
back." She pointed, without looking, at the corner of the map where the Lowtown pump
station was, a small square behind the wall with three green dots. "The water that comes
over falls into the back street, and the pumps send it back to the sea. They can handle
twenty per meter, continuous. More than that, the back street fills."

Téo looked at the tide gauge. The blue line was an hour and twelve minutes from the red
stroke.

"And at high tide?" he asked.

She did the math. It wasn't hard. It was the same math she had done sixteen years
before, in a spreadsheet, in an office with a broken air conditioner, to prove to the
Council that five meters wasn't a luxury. The tide forecast for the peak, with the surge
on top, put the still water some forty centimeters below the crest. The waves of four and
a half meters, breaking at the base, ran up the concrete face well past that. With a
five-meter wall, there would have been a hundred and twenty centimeters between the
still water and the crest, and almost no wave would have reached the top. With four
twenty, there were forty.

Forty centimeters is what a four-meter wave climbs without effort.

"It's going to get wet," she said.

"How wet?"

"Wetter than the pumps can clear."

---

At five past eleven, the overtopping number went past thirty.

The map began to change. First the street right against the wall, Quay Street, which
turned blue from end to end, a thin unbroken line. Then the cross streets, one by one,
short, like fingers. The blue didn't move fast. It moved with the patience of water,
which always goes to the lowest place and always finds it.

On the pump screen, the three green dots turned yellow. *Maximum flow.* The sound they
were making, Joana knew, was the deep roar of big electric motors, something you felt
more in your chest than in your ears. She had been inside the station the day they
switched the pumps on for the first time, with the Council and the cameras, and her
father had come too, his shirt buttoned to the neck, and stood at the back with his arms
crossed, looking at the motors the way a man looks at someone else's boat.

At twelve past eleven, one of the three dots went red.

"Feeder two is down," said Téo, already typing. "Diesel's in. Pump two on the generator."

"How much fuel?"

"Eight hours."

"Then it isn't the fuel," she said. And she kept looking at the red dot, which had gone
back to yellow, and thinking, without meaning to, of the smell of burnt diesel that must
be coming out of the station's vents at that moment, mixed with the rain and the salt.

The map kept turning blue. Behind Quay Street there were four blocks of old warehouses
that nobody used for anything anymore, and after them the houses began. Lowtown. Nineteen
thousand people, according to the Department's last census. Fewer, really, because a lot
of people had moved up in the last few years, to the upper city, inland, to anywhere that
didn't get wet. But still a lot of people.

Joana picked up the device and called the Department, the Civil Defense duty desk, to
tell them what they already knew and to hear what she already knew: the boats were
ready, the shelters were open, nobody was going to send anyone into Lowtown with that sea,
and whoever had stayed had stayed.

She hung up. She looked at the right side of the wall.

---

Gate Two was the biggest of the three.

It closed the mouth of the Market canal, which ran through the middle of Lowtown like a
spine, with the streets coming off it on both sides. It was a sector gate: a slice of a
steel cylinder, eighteen meters wide, that turned on an axle and lay down on the bottom
of the canal when it was open, and rose, curved, against the sea when it was closed.
Joana had drawn the axle. She had drawn the bearings, the rubber seals at the sides, the
calculation for the steel. She had slept three nights on the site, at thirty-three, when
they raised the gate, to see the first closing with her own eyes.

On the screen, Gate Two's thin green deflection line was at eleven millimeters.

The design limit was forty.

She looked at the number for a long time. Eleven. Twelve. Eleven. With every wave it rose
and fell, like breathing. That was normal. Steel gives. Steel that doesn't give breaks.

"Téo. Give me the camera on Two."

The image came up grainy, gray-green, the night vision of a camera fixed to a post on the
bank of the canal. She was seeing the gate side-on, the dark curve of steel, and outside
it, on the left of the image, the sea. It wasn't postcard sea. It was something standing
up, white at the edges, that came out of the dark, hit the curve of the gate and burst
upward, sideways, over the top. On the inside, on the right, the canal was full to the
lip of its banks, smooth, almost calm, the rain punching holes in the surface.

Full to the lip. With the gate closed.

"The canal's rising on the inside," she said.

"Overtopping. The water that comes over the wall runs down the streets and drops into the
canal."

"I know where it's coming from." Her voice came out harder than she meant it to. "The
canal is the lowest point. Everything falls into it. And the pumps can't keep up."

She went back to the green line. Fourteen millimeters.

She did another calculation. The sector gate was designed to hold the sea on one side
only. The force came from outside, pushed on the curve, and the curve passed the force to
the axle, and the axle to the concrete bearings on the banks. It was beautiful. She still
thought it was beautiful. But with the canal full on the inside, and the waves hitting
the outside, the gate was taking water on both sides, and the water outside wasn't still.
It came in blows. Every big wave pushed the gate against the water inside, and the water
inside pushed back, and the gate shook in the middle, and the rubber seals at the sides,
which had fourteen years of salt and sun in them, were what was holding the shaking.

Nineteen millimeters.

"Joana." Téo had stood up. "It's still only halfway."

"Half the limit is twenty."

"You put in a margin. I saw the design, at school. Safety factor of one point five."

"For the gate I drew," she said. "For the wall I drew."

He didn't understand. She didn't explain.

---

At a quarter to midnight, seven minutes before high tide, her father called.

She looked at his name on the screen of the device and felt her stomach drop. He never
called. In forty-seven years she could count on her fingers the times her father had
called first.

"Dad."

"The water's at the door." His voice was calm. Too calm. "It came up the street, slow.
It's not from the canal. It's from above. It's coming down Market Hill, like rain."

"Go up. Go up to the loft now. Take the flashlight, take the radio..."

"I'm already in the loft. I can see it from here. The hulls are floating, Joana." And he
laughed, a short hoarse laugh she hadn't heard since before her mother died. "Severino's
hull is floating inside the boatyard. It came out good. I knew it had come out good."

"Dad, listen to me..."

On the wall screen, Gate Two's green line jumped. Twenty-six. Twenty-eight. She heard,
or thought she heard, through the rain, through the glass, through six hundred meters of
night, a sound that wasn't a wave. A deep crack, metal, like a big bell struck once,
muffled under water.

Téo said something. She didn't hear it.

On the Gate Two camera, the gate was crooked. Not open: crooked. The steel curve had
turned on its axle, on one side only, and lain over on a diagonal, and over the low side
the sea was coming in. Not in waves. In a block. A ramp of dark water, smooth, with white
on top, sliding down into the canal as if the canal were a hill.

The green line stopped sending.

On the map, the Market canal turned blue all at once, from end to end, a spine lit up in
the middle of the lower city. And then, from both banks at the same time, the streets.

"Joana?" said her father, on the device. "What was that noise?"

She looked at the map. Caulkers Street came off the canal six hundred meters from the
gate, on the east side, the third on the right after the footbridge. She knew exactly how
long water took to travel six hundred meters in a full canal, with the sea coming in at
the mouth at high-tide height. She had done that math too, sixteen years before, for
something else. To show what happened if there were no wall at all.

Twelve minutes. Maybe ten.

"Dad. Listen. The gate gave way. Water's going to come up the canal now, a lot of it,
fast. The loft isn't enough. Get onto the roof. By the back ladder, the iron one. Get up
there and stay up top, on the ridge side, and don't come down. Don't come down for
anything. Do you hear me?"

On the other end, a silence. The rain on the zinc.

"I hear you," he said. And then, lower, in a way she would never be able to forget,
because it wasn't fear, it was almost wonder: "I can hear it coming."

The line went dead.

---

She called again. Once, twice, five times. *Out of range.* The tower on Caulkers Street
had to be under water, or without power, or both. She called Davi and hung up before it
rang, because she didn't know what she was going to say.

"I'm going down there," she said, and she was already on her feet, already had her
jacket in her hand.

Téo stepped in front of the door. He didn't touch her. He just stood there.

"You're not," he said. "Nobody goes into Lowtown now. Not even the Civil Defense boats.
You know that. You wrote the protocol."

She stood looking at him. He was right, and they both knew it, and she hated him for one
whole second with a force that frightened her, and then the hatred passed and all that
was left was the tiredness.

She sat back down.

There was only one thing she could do, and it was nothing. The tide was at its peak.
Opening the other two gates to relieve the canal would be opening two more mouths to the
sea. Pumping was pumping into a bucket with a hole in it. The only thing that would take
the water out of Lowtown was the sea going down, and the sea would go down when it
pleased, at six in the morning, slowly, and would take with it whatever it took.

So she sat and looked at the map.

She had spent her whole life looking at that drawing from above. Plans, sections,
contour lines. She knew by heart the height of every corner in Lowtown, because she had
measured them. The corner of Caulkers Street and the canal was one meter ten above mean
sea level. The boatyard floor, one meter forty. The loft, four thirty. The ridge of the
zinc roof, seven eighty.

The blue got into Caulkers Street at nine minutes past midnight.

She watched it get in. She could do nothing but that, and she did all of it, without
looking away, because it seemed to her the least she could do. The street turned blue
from the mouth of the canal to the middle, and from the middle to the end, and the gray
square of her father's boatyard was surrounded by blue on all four sides, and stayed
there, gray, in the middle of the blue, like an island. The model couldn't say how high
the water was inside the houses. Only that there was water.

At twenty-two minutes past midnight, half an hour after the red stroke, because the
surge was holding the water against the coast, the tide stopped rising.

The blue line touched the red stroke, lay along it for a while, and then, almost without
anyone noticing, began to go down.

Téo brought coffee. She didn't drink it. She sat holding the cup in both hands, because it
was hot, and because her hands needed to hold something.

Outside, the rain was still hitting the glass sideways. On the middle screen, water was
still coming over the wall with every wave, less now, a thread of blue at a time. Four
meters twenty. She looked at the drawn crest and, above it, without meaning to, the other
line, the five-meter one, which had never existed anywhere except in her head and in an
old spreadsheet.

Eighty centimeters.

She didn't know yet whether they would have been enough. She would spend the rest of her
life not knowing. But she would spend the rest of her life doing the math.
