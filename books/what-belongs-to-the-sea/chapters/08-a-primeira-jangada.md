Mrs. Célia Ferreira's house keeled over at eighteen minutes past three in the afternoon, in
front of fifty people, and it was Joana's fault.

She knew it at once. Not afterward, doing the math. At once, in the second she saw the water
tank slide on the roof. Before the house began to go over. Before Clara screamed. She knew
the way you know a glass is going to fall off the table an instant before it falls, your
hand already moving, already late.

But that was in the afternoon. In the morning, the house floated.

---

It had taken three weeks to build the platform.

It was a rectangle six meters by five, made of thick white foam panels from the Market's cold
rooms. The Market's ground floor had had six cold rooms, for fish and for meat, each lined
inside with foam boards twenty centimeters thick, glued to sheet metal, and the sea had burst
the doors but hadn't been able to do anything to the boards. They floated. It was the only
thing in Lowtown that floated without needing anyone. Davi and Nilo had spent two weeks
taking the boards off the cold-room walls one by one, with a crowbar, at low tide, and
carrying them by boat to the boatyard.

Forty-eight boards. Joana had calculated the buoyancy of each one, and added them up, and
subtracted the weight of the structure, and the weight of a house, and of four people, and
there had been plenty left over. The boards went underneath, wrapped in heavy tarp so the
sun wouldn't eat the foam, strapped to each other with cargo straps. On top, a grid of wooden
beams, from the dismantled market stalls. On top of the grid, a plank floor.

Davi had made the grid. With his grandfather's adze, dressing the beams where they met each
other, and with the marking gauge, scoring the line of each joint before cutting. Joana
watched him work on the ground floor of the boatyard, at low tide, with the beams up on
sawhorses, and sometimes forgot to do whatever she was doing. He held the adze the way her
father had held it. Low, near the blade. She had never taught him that. Neither had her
father, she thought. He had watched.

The house was hers.

She had drawn the house at night, in the loft, with the slide rule on the table, the one from
the drawer, which she had gone to fetch from the apartment in the upper city and brought down
without knowing why. A real house. Not a shack on a raft. Four walls of cement board, which
there was plenty of in the Department's depot and which Téo, one Wednesday night, had brought
by truck to the top of the hill without asking what for. A roof of clay tiles, from houses the
sea had smashed. Two windows. A door. And on top of the roof, a five-hundred-liter water tank,
full of water from the street's window stills, so Mrs. Célia would have a tap inside the
house. So she would have pressure.

She wanted it to be a house. She wanted Mrs. Célia and Clara to walk in and think it was a
house, and not a boat, and not something temporary. Mrs. Célia had lived thirty years in a
one-room single-story house on Caulkers Street, which the sea had filled to the ceiling on
the night of the big tide and again in the surge, and since then she had slept on a mattress
on the floor of the second story of Mrs. Ilda's green house, with her twelve-year-old
daughter, and complained about nothing, and Joana had never seen anyone so tired of not
complaining.

"Mom," Davi had said, on Thursday, looking at the drawing. "The tank goes up there?"

"It does."

"Five hundred liters?"

"Five hundred kilos. I know. It's in the math."

He had kept looking at the drawing.

"Weight low," he said.

"It's in the math, Davi. The platform's six meters in the beam. I calculated the metacenter.
With the tank full on top and four people inside, the metacentric height is still positive.
It comes back."

"In still water."

She had looked at him. He hadn't lowered his eyes.

"The canal is still water," she had said.

---

The platform went into the water on Saturday morning, with the tide coming in.

They had built it all on the ground floor of the boatyard, on the mud, and the idea was
simple: the water would come in through the open door, as it came in twice a day, and lift
the platform, and when the water was high enough, they would pull the platform out through
the door with ropes, to the Market canal, to the place they had chosen, between two old iron
lamp posts, on the east bank, in front of the green house.

Half of Lowtown came to watch. Joana hadn't expected it. At eight in the morning, with the
water still low, there were already people on the roofs of Caulkers Street, sitting on the
ridges with a leg down each side, the way her father liked to sit. People on the terraces.
People on the planks over the street. Jairo, on Mr. Lauro's roof, with a thermos. Mrs. Ilda
in her beach chair, on the terrace of the green house, with Mrs. Célia standing beside her,
hands clasped.

The water came in through the door at eight forty. It reached the foam boards at ten past
nine. At twenty past nine, the platform moved.

It was nothing. A creak of wood. One of the corners rising a finger. But up on the roofs
someone clapped, and then someone else, and then the whole street.

At nine forty, the platform was floating. All of it. Level. With the house on top, the
cement-board house, with the clay roof and the two windows and the door, and the black plastic
water tank on the roof, still empty, and the brown water passing underneath it.

Joana stood looking. She didn't clap. She couldn't. She stood watching the house float, and
for a second, just one, she felt something she hadn't felt since the night they raised Gate
Two, when she was thirty-three and slept on the site.

---

They pulled the platform to the canal at eleven, at high tide. Davi in Amaro's boat, with a
rope at the bow. Nilo and two more young men in a dinghy, with another. People on the banks,
walking along the planks, holding long ropes so the house wouldn't hit the walls. It took
half an hour. They brought the platform in between the two iron posts, on the east bank, in
front of the green house.

The posts were Davi's idea. Or her father's. From the henhouse.

At each corner of the platform on the bank side, a thick steel ring, the kind that had held
the gate chains, which Téo had also brought from the depot without asking. Each ring went
around a post. The ring ran up and down the post, freely, with slack. When the tide rose,
the house would rise with it, sliding on the posts. When the tide fell, the house would come
down, and settle on the mud, on two hardwood skids that Davi had dressed with the adze and
fixed under the boards. It wouldn't drift. It wouldn't capsize. It wouldn't bang.

At one in the afternoon, with the tide standing at the top, they filled the water tank.

It was a ceremony. Joana hadn't planned for it to be, but it was. The neighbors from Caulkers
Street brought it over, by boat, by plank, by bucket, the water from their windows, and
poured it into the tank on the roof by the ladder, one bucket at a time. Each person one
bucket. Mr. Lauro. The young men from the ledge. Jairo, with two. Mrs. Ilda sent Nilo with a
whole pot. Five hundred liters.

The platform sank two fingers. Joana looked at the waterline on the boards. It was where the
math said.

Mrs. Célia went into the house at half past one. She opened the door. She looked. She walked
to the sink. She turned on the tap. Water came out.

She didn't say anything. She stood with her hand under the water, and Clara beside her,
looking at her mother, and Joana saw it from the bank, through the open door, and had to look
somewhere else.

---

At eighteen minutes past three, a boat came up the canal too fast.

It was an aluminum dinghy from the upper city, with a combustion engine, the kind there still
were, belonging to a man who came down to Lowtown now and then to sell things. It made noise
and it made a wake. Joana saw it coming up the canal, from the direction of the gate, and
thought nothing of it. She was talking to Mrs. Ilda on the terrace. The dinghy passed in
front of Mrs. Célia's house without slowing. Its wake, a small wave, a hand-span high, rolled
along the canal and hit the platform side-on.

The platform heeled.

A little. A hand-span. The way the bottle frame had heeled in the boatyard when Davi pushed
on the end. The way anything that floats heels when it takes a wave on the side.

And it came back. It started to come back.

And the water tank slid.

Joana saw it. That was when she knew. The tank was sitting on top of the clay roof, on a
plank base, tied with two ropes, and the roof was pitched, and with the heel the tank ran on
its base, a little, a hand-span, toward the low side. Five hundred kilos. A hand-span
sideways, four meters up. And the moment the platform had to come back with, the positive
metacentric height she had calculated at the table with her father's slide rule, became, in
one second, smaller than the moment of the tank.

The platform didn't come back.

It kept going over. Slowly. Far too slowly. With the house on top, tilting, and the clay tiles
sliding off the roof one by one and falling into the water with a sound like stones, and the
door open, and inside it Clara, who had stayed at the sink playing with the tap.

Clara screamed.

Davi was already in the water. Joana didn't see him jump. She saw him already swimming, from
Amaro's boat to the door of the house, which was on its side now, half in the water, and
going in through it, and disappearing inside, and the house still going over, the water tank
now hanging by its ropes off the outside of the roof, pulling down, and the foam boards on
the high side coming out of the water, white, dripping.

The posts held. That was the one thing. The steel rings, on the iron posts, jammed at the
angle, and the platform stayed caught there, lying over at almost forty degrees, without
capsizing all the way, with the house hanging off it.

Davi came out through the upper window, the one that now faced the sky, with Clara in his
arms. Clara crying, whole, wet, her hand still closed around something. Joana would see
later that it was the soap.

---

Nobody said anything to her.

That was the worst of it. She stood at the edge of the canal, the water at her waist, with no
memory of having come down from Mrs. Ilda's terrace, and the people on the roofs looked at
the house lying over, and looked at the water tank hanging, and looked at her, and nobody said
anything. Mrs. Célia was on the stair of the green house, with Clara in her arms, wrapped in
a towel. She wasn't crying. She had the face of someone who had known from the start that it
wouldn't be this time, and hadn't had the heart to say so.

Davi came out of the water and sat on the bank, beside Joana. Dripping.

She waited for him to say it. *Weight low.* He had the right. He had said it on Thursday,
looking at the drawing. He had said *in still water*.

He didn't say it.

"The tank," she said. "It was the tank. The math was right for the tank sitting still. I
didn't put the tank sliding in. I tied it on a pitched roof and didn't put in the math that
it could slide."

"I know."

"You said."

"I said weight low." He picked up a piece of clay tile that was in the mud beside him and
turned it over in his hands. "I didn't know why. Grandpa said it and I repeated it. You knew
why. You just didn't believe it."

She looked at him. Fifteen. The adze held near the blade.

"The house was meant to be a house," she said. "Not a boat."

Davi dropped the piece of tile.

"Mom." He spoke low, so the roofs wouldn't hear. "It floats. It's a boat. There's no way for
it not to be."

---

They righted the platform the next day, at low tide, with block and tackle and rope and twenty
people pulling, and the cement-board house came off it in pieces. The foam boards were whole.
Davi's grid of beams was whole. Not one joint had opened.

They rebuilt it in twelve days.

The platform grew. Seven meters by six. Wide in the beam. Joana drew it again, at night, in
the loft, but this time Davi sat beside her, and she drew and he spoke, and when he spoke she
stopped and did the math to see why, and nearly always he was right, and when he wasn't she
explained and he understood faster than any intern at the Department. The water tank didn't
go on the roof. It went under the floor, lying between the beams, in the middle, at the lowest
point of the house. Five hundred liters of ballast. The drinking water became the weight that
held the house upright. Mrs. Célia wouldn't have pressure at the tap. She would have a hand
pump, foot-operated, which Nilo found in a fishing boat abandoned at the foot of the wall.

The walls weren't cement board. They were woven bamboo with tarp on the inside and planks on
the outside, on the bottom half, and mesh on the top half, to let the wind through. The roof
was zinc. From the boatyard. The half that was left of her father's roof, which had been
stacked on the ground floor, buckled, and which Davi straightened with a hammer in two days.

On the last day, before putting the house in the water, Davi took the plumb bob out of the
toolbox.

Joana watched him climb onto the platform, still up on the sawhorses in the boatyard, and go
to the middle of the floor, and hold the cord up high, and let the brass cone down, slowly,
until it almost touched the planks. The cone swung. It stopped. He looked at the cone, and
looked at the line of the floor, and looked at the bamboo wall beside him, and went to the
other end and did it again.

"It's true," he said.

She had a laser level in the bottom of her backpack. From the Department too. She didn't take
it out.

---

The house went into the water on a Saturday of a spring tide, a new moon, with the water
rising higher than usual, and there was no ceremony at all. They pulled it with rope to the
canal, to the two posts, and put the rings on. Nobody brought a bucket. The tank was already
full, under the floor.

In the afternoon, the same man in the aluminum dinghy came up the canal, fast, making a wake.
Joana was on Mrs. Ilda's terrace and saw him coming, and didn't move, and didn't breathe.

The wake hit the platform side-on.

The house heeled a hand-span. The ballast water under the floor gave a thick sigh that could
be heard from the bank. The house came back. It rocked twice, three times, and stopped.

Inside, Clara was at the sink, her foot on the pump pedal. She didn't even look up.

That night, Mrs. Célia slept in the house. Joana knew because she stayed awake on the terrace
of the green house until the early-morning tide, watching, and saw the house rise on the posts,
slowly, with the water, the rings sliding on the iron without a sound, and then stop at the
top, and then come down, and settle on the mud, on its wooden skids, and stay there, level,
dark, with a flashlight lit in the mesh window, waiting for the sea to come back.
