The tide bottomed out at six thirty-five, as the table said, and started rising again at
six thirty-six.

Joana saw both things from the tip of the Quay, standing on the wet concrete beside the
three gray Civil Defense boats tied up on the ramp. She had come down from the tower at
five, when the wind dropped, and had spent an hour walking the crest of the wall in the
dark, with a headlamp, as far as it went, looking at the inside. She had seen almost
nothing. A city without lights, smelling of mud.

When the day came up, she saw.

Lowtown wasn't under water. That was the first thing, and for a moment she almost felt
relief. The streets showed between the houses the way they should, streets, long and
straight, only brown. Covered in a smooth, shining silt that reflected the white sky, and
in things. A refrigerator lying on its side. A whole door, with its handle. A market
vendor's handcart, wheels in the air. Branches. Plastic of every color, snagged on
everything tall enough to snag it. And over it all, high on the bars of the
ground-floor windows, almost at the lintel, a dark mark, straight, unbroken, running from
house to house as far as she could see.

The night's waterline. Joana read it the way she would read a signature.

Then, at six thirty-six, the water came back.

It didn't come the way it had in the night. It came the way the tide comes, which is the
most patient thing in the world. She saw it first in the Market canal, which was almost
dry, its bottom mud showing and a thread of water down the middle: the thread thickened.
Then she saw it at the mouths of the cross streets, where the mud went smoother and then
went to mirror. Gate Two was there at the far end, crooked, half lying down, and over its
low side the sea came in without hurry, a little more every minute, as if it had been
invited.

The math came on its own. That day's tide would rise to nearly a meter and a half above
mean sea level. The corner of Caulkers Street was twenty centimeters below it.

Lowtown would flood again at noon. And at midnight. And the next day, twice. And every
time the moon was full or new, more. And the eighteen-meter gate she had drawn was lying
on the bottom of the canal like a dead animal, holding nothing.

"Engineer."

It was the Civil Defense boy, a kid in an orange vest who couldn't have been twenty-five,
his face swollen from not sleeping.

"We go now, with the water rising. The boats don't draw much. By about nine there'll be
water in the streets to get all the way to the end." He looked at the paper in his hand,
laminated, wet. "You're in boat two. East route. Market canal, Caulkers, Ropemakers, the
Hill."

Joana nodded. She had asked for that route. Nobody had asked her why.

---

Davi showed up at ten past seven.

She didn't see him arrive. First she saw the Civil Defense boy talking to someone at the
edge of the ramp with his arms spread, the way you talk to someone who isn't going to
leave, and then she saw the green jacket, the hood, the legs too long for the pants. He
had walked down from the upper city. An hour of hill, in the dark, alone.

"Go home," she said, before he got close.

"No."

"Davi."

"I called him all night." His voice was hoarse. "You didn't call me. I called you too. You
didn't pick up."

She hadn't picked up. She had seen his name flash on the device, five, six times, between
midnight and three, and hadn't picked up, because she didn't know what to say, and because
every time the device buzzed she thought it might be her father, and it wasn't.

"I don't know if he's alive," she said.

It was the first time she had said it out loud. Davi stood still. His face didn't change,
but something under his face changed, and she saw it, and wanted to take the sentence
back.

"Then I'm going," he said.

The Civil Defense boy waited, the laminated paper in his hand. She looked at her son. Fifteen, a meter eighty, his
grandfather's chin.

"Sit in the middle," she said. "And keep your hands out of the water."

---

Boat two was thick gray rubber, with an electric motor at the back that buzzed like a big
insect and made no wake. It held four: the pilot, a Civil Defense woman with a mask on her
face and a first-aid box in her lap, Joana in the bow, Davi in the middle. They went in by
the Market canal at eight forty, with the brown, smooth water climbing the banks, and Gate
Two went by beside them, huge, crooked, the orange steel peeling at the bend and one of
the rubber seals hanging out like a tongue.

Joana looked at it and didn't look away. She had promised herself, in the tower, that she
wouldn't look away from anything that day.

The axle on the east side had come out of its bearing. She could see the hole in the
concrete of the bank, the steel plate torn off, the anchor bolts bent like crooked nails.
It hadn't been the steel. The steel had held. It had been the bearing, pushed sideways by
a force that no calculation had predicted, because no calculation predicted the gate
with water on both sides.

She kept the image. She was going to need it.

"Is that where it broke?" Davi asked.

"Yes."

He looked too. He gripped the rope along the side with both hands.

---

The first thing she counted was the windows.

It wasn't on purpose. Her head did that: it counted. On Ropemakers Street, the first after
the footbridge, the houses were two stories, built wall to wall, painted colors that had
once been strong. In every one, the ground-floor windows were open or smashed, and the same
smell came out of all of them, mud, sewage, salt and something sweet, food going bad. In
almost every one, in the upstairs windows, there was someone.

A woman with a child on her lap. An old man without a shirt. Two young men sitting on a
ledge with their legs hanging out, as if they were waiting for a bus. A dog on its own,
which barked at the boat and then stopped and stood watching. People who waved, people
who didn't wave, people who watched the boat go by with a face Joana couldn't read, and
then could: it was the face of someone who had done the math too.

The Civil Defense woman was marking things on a tablet. House, number of people, injured,
who wanted to leave. Most didn't. The ones who did, she marked with a color and told them
the big boat would come in the afternoon.

"Why don't they want to leave?" Davi asked, quietly.

"Because the house is theirs," said the Civil Defense woman, without taking her eyes off
the screen. "And because the last time they left, when they came back, there was nothing
inside anymore."

At the corner of Ropemakers and the hill, the pilot cut the motor and let the boat glide.
There was something caught on the bars of a low window, half in, half out of the water.
Joana saw it before Davi did, and moved in the bow to put herself in his line of sight,
and it didn't work, because he was taller than she was.

The Civil Defense woman marked it on the tablet. A different color. She said a number into
the radio on her shoulder. The pilot started the motor again.

Davi didn't say anything. Joana put her hand on his knee, over the wet pants, and he let
her.

That day, Civil Defense counted thirty-one. Joana learned the number that night, on the
device, in a five-line bulletin, between *Gate Two: out of service* and *The Shallows:
water to the knee, no casualties*. At the time, in the boat, she counted two.

---

Caulkers Street began at the third on the right after the bridge, and she knew they were
close before she saw the sign, because she recognized the bakery on the corner, Mr.
Laércio's, where she bought bread when she was eight. The bakery's iron door was bent
inward. The display window no longer existed.

The water was deeper there. It would have come to the waist of anyone standing, if anyone
had been standing. The boat moved up the middle of the street, steering around a
plastic table floating legs up, and Davi was already kneeling on the seat, neck stretched,
looking ahead.

The boatyard was at the end of the street, on the left. It was the biggest building on the
block: a long shed, brick halfway up the walls and wood from there to the top, with a
pitched zinc roof and a sliding door that opened onto the street. The door was open. Wide
open. Joana saw, before she saw anything else, that it wasn't the water that had opened
it. It was open all the way, run along its track to the end, the way you open a door when
you want to get something big through.

And on the zinc roof, on the ridge, sitting side by side with a leg down each slope, there
were ten people.

Davi shouted. Not a word. A sound.

At the end of the ridge, on the street side, a thin man with a button-up shirt open to his
belly raised his arm slowly. In no hurry at all. The way you raise your arm to a neighbor
across the street.

Joana couldn't do anything with her face. She stood looking.

---

Her father had spent the night working.

She learned it in pieces, while the pilot brought the boat up against the wall of the shed
and the Civil Defense woman climbed the iron ladder at the back to see to the injuries, and
Davi climbed after her without asking. She learned it from a neighbor, Mrs. Ilda, a short,
broad woman of seventy-one with white hair cut short, who was sitting in the middle of the
ridge with a blanket on her shoulders and a voice that hadn't tired at all.

"The water came in all at once," said Mrs. Ilda, from up there, in the tone you use with
someone you knew a long time ago. She had. She had sold corn cake outside the school Joana
went to. "Ten to midnight, more or less. Slow at first, from above, down the hill. Then it
came from the canal, and there was that bang. In ten minutes it was halfway up my stairs.
I went up on the roof with Nilo." She pointed to a young man of about twenty, sitting
further along, who gave a short wave. "And then we stayed there. Not knowing where it
would stop."

"And my father?"

Mrs. Ilda looked aside. Her father was sitting at the end of the ridge, his back to them,
with Davi crouched beside him, talking low.

"Your father showed up at two in the morning," said Mrs. Ilda. "Rowing."

Joana looked into the shed.

The boatyard had water to a man's waist, and rising. Light came in through the open
door and through the gaps in the zinc in stripes, and fell on the brown water, and on the
water, tied with two ropes to an iron column, a hull was floating. A wooden boat, about
seven meters, high in the bow and wide in the bottom, the hull painted white with a blue
stripe along the gunwale, and inside still raw, the wood pale, unvarnished. Joana knew it.
She had seen that hull on the slipway three months before, on the Sunday of Davi's
birthday, still without its top planks.

Severino's hull. *It came out good. I knew it had come out good.*

"He let the boat off the cradle with the water," said Mrs. Ilda, behind her. "Opened the
door, God knows how, with the water over his head. And went out rowing down the street
in the dark. Seventy-eight years old and that heart. He picked us up on my roof. Then the
Ferreiras, who were at the window, the girl and her mother. Then Mr. Lauro, who'd fallen
and was hanging on to a post. Four trips, it was. He brought everyone here because it's the
widest roof on the street." She stopped. "I told him to stop after the third. He said the
boat was still dry inside, so it could do one more."

Joana looked at the hull floating, quiet, tied, the water lapping
gently at its side. A blue stripe on the gunwale. Seven meters of wood her father had
taken eight months to make, alone, by hand, the same hands that now shook a little when
they held a cup.

She had made three kilometers and two hundred meters of concrete, and eighteen meters of
steel, and a model that recalculated the city every thirty seconds.

He had made a boat.

---

She climbed the iron ladder last. The rungs were cold and slippery, and the zinc of the
roof, when she put her head out, was hot from the sun, and smelled of rust and salt. She
went along the ridge on all fours, hands on the sheets, to the end, and sat down beside her
father.

He didn't look at her. He was looking at the street. The water had stopped rising and was
smooth, still, reflecting the houses upside down.

Davi was on his other side. He had taken off the green jacket and put it over his
grandfather's shoulders, and his grandfather had let him, which meant he was colder than
he admitted.

"Dad."

"It'll go down in a bit," he said. He pointed at the water with his chin. "Quarter to one,
more or less, it stops. Then it goes down. At night it comes back."

"I know."

"I know you know." He turned his head and looked at her for the first time. His eyes were
red at the rims, from salt and from not sleeping, but they were clear. "I'm saying it
because that's how it is now. It comes and goes. Twice a day."

"Dad, the big boat's coming this afternoon. Civil Defense will take everyone who wants to
go to the shelter at the school. You're coming with us. Davi will stay with you..."

"No."

He said it the way he had said it his whole life. Without raising his voice. Without
explaining.

"Dad, look around." She opened her arm to the street, to the houses with mud up to their
waists, to the dark line on the bars. "This street floods every tide now. Every one. The
gate doesn't exist anymore. The Council isn't going to fix it in a month, in six months, I
don't know if they'll fix it at all. There's no power. In two days there won't be water in
the tap. You're seventy-eight and you've got a heart that..."

"The hull held," he said.

She stopped.

"I made her to float with six people and a full net," her father said. "She took four at a
time, and an old man who'd fallen, in their laps. Four trips. In a current. In the dark.
She didn't take on water." He looked down, through the gap in the roof, into the shed,
where the hull floated tied to the column. "She came out good."

"I know she came out good, Dad."

"Well, then."

She didn't understand. She waited for the rest, and the rest didn't come, and she realized
that that *was* the rest. That for him the sentence was whole.

Davi was watching his grandfather with a face Joana hadn't seen on her son since he was
small. She didn't know its name. She knew it wasn't for her.

"Grandpa," said Davi. "Will you teach me?"

"Teach you what?"

"To make one that floats."

Her father smiled. A short, sideways smile that gathered all the lines of his face into one
place.

"Every boat floats," he said. "The hard part is making one that comes back."

---

The big boat came at three in the afternoon, with the water already going down again, and
took Mrs. Ilda, and Nilo, and the Ferreiras, and Mr. Lauro with his arm in a splint. It
took a woman with a baby from a house across the street. It took four people from a house
at the end of the street that Joana didn't know, and a dog.

It didn't take her father.

Joana stayed on the roof with him until the light started to go orange. Davi did too.
Nobody said much. The Civil Defense woman left them two jugs of water, a box of packaged
food, a flashlight, a small crank radio, and a piece of paper with a number to call, and
looked at Joana before she climbed down the way you look at a person who should know
better.

At half past five, the street was dry again. Mud as far as she could see, shining orange.
Severino's hull had settled with the water inside the shed and was resting on its side in
the mud of the floor, still tied to the column, crooked, waiting.

"It comes back at midnight," her father said. "A little higher than this morning's. The
moon's still growing for it."

"Dad. Please."

He got up slowly, with his hand on Davi's shoulder, and stood on the ridge, on top of the
shed that had been his father's, and looked down Caulkers Street from end to end, covered
in mud and things, and the hill, and the canal, and past the canal, far off, the gray line
of the wall, whole, with the sun hitting it from the side.

"This house is older than your wall," he said. There was no anger in his voice at all.
"It'll be older than anybody's. It knows how to get wet."

He went down the iron ladder, one rung at a time, facing it, the way you go down a
ladder on a boat.

Joana stayed on the ridge, sitting, her hands flat on the hot zinc, and looked at the wall
until the sun went down on the other side, behind the upper city, and the concrete went
dark.
