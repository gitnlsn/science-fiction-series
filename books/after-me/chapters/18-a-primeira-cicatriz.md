The building on Aster Street is the thirty-first.

I do the arithmetic every morning, in the transit, from Ostrel to Corvel, watching
the windows go by. Thirty-one of three hundred and twelve. Two hundred and
eighty-one to go. In four years. That's one and a bit a week, if nobody gets sick
and the mesh doesn't find any column cut in half that someone in 2033 decided to
hide behind a built-in cupboard.

The mesh finds one, nearly every week. And I know where to look, nearly every week.

Noor says I'm getting faster. Breno says *well, fuck me* less often, which,
according to Noor, is the highest praise he knows how to give. The three of us have
lunch on Tuesdays now, on the low walkway in Corvel, and I order *the usual*, and
Breno orders the same as me and complains about the spice.

I sign the reports with my thumb, on the edge of the light table, and the edge lights
up green with my name. Only my name.

---

I live in the apartment in Ostrel.

Half of it is mine, it's in writing, and the other half is Irene's, and Irene didn't
want to live there. She said the twenty-second floor was too high for her and that
Harlo was her neighborhood and that we could split it some other way. So we split it
some other way. She comes on Sundays. I cook on the manual hotplate, with the metal
pans, and burn the garlic, and swear at the garlic, and she laughs, and she brings a
succulent, always, and now there are succulents in every window of the apartment,
green and fleshy, that hardly ever need water.

The fourth time, she ate the egg.

She didn't say anything. She burst the yolk with her fork and mixed it into the
pasta, slowly, the way her grandmother used to mix it at six, and ate all of it,
and I saw, and didn't say anything either. There are things that aren't said in this
family.

The house calls me *Ms. Helena*. I asked it to call me just Helena, and it does, now,
just Helena. It doesn't know how to say *Mrs. Cecília* anymore. I asked once, in the
middle of the night, alone in the living room, whether it remembered. The house said
that Mrs. Cecília's records were archived and that it wasn't authorized to access
them. It said this in its usual calm voice. Then it said, without my asking, that
Mrs. Cecília slept better when someone was on the rug.

I don't know if somebody programmed that. I don't ask.

The room that doesn't know my name still doesn't. I sleep in it. The cotton sheet,
rough, smelling of storage. The paneled ceiling, with joints. The succulent Irene
brought to the first lunch, which didn't die, on the nightstand. And, in the window,
the shoe. Navy blue, canvas, with the Velcro that doesn't hold. Upright. With the toe
pointing in.

The armchair stays where it was, by the window. I don't know its name. It doesn't
know mine. I sit in it at night, sometimes, with the light off, looking at the city,
and it's the only thing in the apartment that hasn't changed, and that's why I sit
in it.

---

Davi comes on Thursdays.

He didn't stop. He came the first Thursday after the funeral, with the guitar under
his arm, and stood in the doorway not knowing if he could come in, and I said he
could, and he sat on the rug, in front of the empty armchair, and played.

He played a whole song. From the beginning to the end. A song from before, that I
knew, by a band I liked when I was twenty and that I hadn't heard in a hundred and
ten years, and that he had found I don't know where, in a file that opened, by some
miracle, and had learned by himself, with his third finger standing up, like a
hammer.

When he finished, he stopped.

He sat for a while with his hand still on the strings, looking at the empty armchair.

"It's over," he said, surprised, as if he'd discovered something. "You know because
it stops."

I couldn't say anything. He didn't expect me to. He put the guitar in its case,
which he had sewn himself, and left, and came back the next Thursday, and the one
after. He plays one whole song every Thursday. He says he's learning all the ones
that have an end.

---

Ravi comes looking for me one afternoon, a few weeks ago.

He doesn't call. He turns up. He's on the sidewalk on Tessel at the end of an
afternoon, under the seventy-year-old trees, in the same sand-colored shirt with no
buttons, or another one just like it, and the smear of blue ink on the cuff, or
another one, and the usual dark circles. He's waiting. I know it's for me because
he's looking up, at the mesh on my building, the way he's never looked at anything.

"I don't know what the floor is made of," he says, when I get close. "But I wanted
to see the building."

We stand looking at the building for a while. The copper beetles climbing up and
down the mesh, at the end of the afternoon, shining.

"Irene let me know," he says. "The same day. I'm sorry."

"I know."

"I wanted to come sooner. I didn't know if I should."

"Why?"

He takes a while. He looks at his own hands. It's the first time I've seen Ravi look
at his hands.

"Because I knew," he says. "From the start. I read the contract before you woke up.
I told you the year, and her age, and took you to see the city, and handed you over
on the platform, and I knew what you were going to. And I couldn't say." He
breathes. "I've been doing this for eleven years. You were the first person I handed
over knowing."

"You said *look after her*."

"I did."

"I looked after her."

Ravi looks at me. His face, which I never knew how to read, is suddenly easy to
read. It's relief.

"I know," he says. "Irene told me. About the bath." He smiles, tired, the good smile
of someone who repeats his sentences and still thinks they're worth saying. "I
didn't know if you'd go back. From Tessel. When she asked for emancipation, the
clinic let me know, and I thought: *that's it, she'll go*. Nearly everyone goes,
when they can."

"I went."

"And came back."

"I came back." I look at the building. "Nobody asked."

He nods slowly. Then he looks at the sky, which is low and gray, and says it's going
to rain, and I say no, the forecast says zero percent, and he touches two fingers to
his wrist and confirms it, zero percent, and we say goodbye.

It doesn't rain. I knew it wouldn't. I don't know how I knew.

---

The accident is on a Tuesday, on Aster Street.

On the sixth floor, in a slab where the mesh read too much steel in one corner, and
I know why before I see it: someone, in 2032, left a rebar stub sticking out of the
slab for a staircase that was never built, and then covered it with some filler or
other, and the stub stayed there, inside, rusting, a hundred years. The mesh found
it. The beetles opened up the filler. And there it is, coming out of the concrete, a
twenty-centimeter stump of rebar, bent, brown, its end cut with a torch by someone in
a hurry.

I crouch to look. I put my hand on it.

I grab it by the wrong end.

I know the instant I grab it. It's the same thing. The same gesture, the same hand,
the same rebar, a hundred and twenty-five years later: the end cut with a torch,
with a burr, and my right hand closing over it without thinking, and the burr going
into the side of my index finger.

It hurts.

I look at my hand. The blood comes. Red, bright, running down the side of the
finger, over the palm, dripping onto the concrete from 2032. It's the first time I've
bled since I woke up. This body has never bled. Not even at the clinic, with the
bracelet that took blood without a puncture. Never.

It bleeds now. In the same place.

"Helena!" Noor shouts, from the other side of the slab.

I don't answer. I'm looking at my finger. At the side of my right index finger. A
cut of two centimeters, more or less. Exactly where it was.

---

The clinic closes the cut in an afternoon.

It's the neighborhood clinic, in Corvel, and the doctor is young, and uses something
that looks like a pen and that passes over the cut, slowly, and the cut closes behind
it, like a crack in the walkway. It takes four minutes. It doesn't hurt.

"There," says the doctor. "Tomorrow there won't be anything. Do you want me to erase
the mark? It takes another ten minutes. There won't be any trace."

I look at my finger. There's a thin line, pink, still a little swollen, on the side
of the index finger, where the pen went. Two centimeters. It'll turn white, I know.
It'll turn white in a few months and then it'll stay like that forever, a pale line
on the side of the finger, that anyone will see if they know where to look.

"No," I say.

The doctor looks at me without understanding. Nobody says no. I can see on her face
that nobody says no.

"It'll leave a scar," she says, like someone warning of a risk.

"I know," I say. "Let it stay."

I think of Ciça, in the armchair, looking at her hands, telling the doctor she didn't
want the spots erased. *Each one was a summer.*

This is my first.

---

At night, at home, I lie down on the living room rug.

On my back, with my arms spread, in the place where I used to lie and count the
joints with her. The armchair is beside me, empty, with the throw folded over its
arm. The succulents in the windows. The guitar leaning against the wall, where Davi
leaves it on Thursdays now, because he says the guitar likes it here.

I raise my right hand and look at it, against the ceiling light.

My father's broad, short nails. The left thumb that bends back. The mole the size of
a lentil on my wrist. And, on the side of my index finger, a pink line, new, two
centimeters, still swollen, that hurts a little when I bend the finger.

It isn't the same one. The other was from 2016, from a site that doesn't exist
anymore, from a twenty-three-year-old girl who was proud of it. That one went with
the old body, into the vault, and didn't come back. This is another one. It belongs
to this body. It's the first thing that ever happened to it. The first line of its
story.

My body is three years old. And, as of today, it has a scar.

I look at the ceiling. It has joints. It has the patch by the door to my room,
darker, thicker, that doesn't follow the pattern of the others. Someone broke
something up there, a long time ago, and fixed it, and couldn't make it the same.
And the ceiling is standing. And I'm under it. And it doesn't fall.

Outside, the rainy season is about to begin. A few weeks to go. The forecast, on my
wrist, says zero percent until the end of the month.

I lie there, looking at my finger, thinking that one day, a long time from now, when
this line is white and old and I don't even remember how it happened, it may start
to itch.

Before the rain.

And then I'll know before.
