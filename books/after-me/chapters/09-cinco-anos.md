Noor has lunch with me on Tuesdays.

I don't know when it started. In the third week, maybe, after a whole morning on
the twelfth floor of a building on Aster Street, looking together at a beam
nobody understood, she said *let's eat* and I went. Since then, on Tuesdays, at
half past twelve, we come down from whatever building we're in and walk to a low
walkway in Corvel where there's a place that makes a dish I don't know the name
of and that Noor calls *the usual*. I order the usual. It's a kind of rice with
something green and spicy that I didn't know in 2031 and that I now want every
day.

She's the first friend I've had since I woke up.

She's forty-six, with an eleven-year-old son who wants to be a musician, a mother
who lives in the south and calls every Sunday to complain about the heat, and a
way of laughing through her nose that I recognize from someone in 2031 and can't
place. She asks me about before, sometimes. Not much. She asks small things,
things I didn't expect. What the food was like. Whether we slept with the windows
open. Whether it was true that people walked in the street under the rain
holding a roof in their hand.

"An umbrella," I say.

"That's it." She laughs through her nose. "My son doesn't believe it."

Today she doesn't ask anything. She eats quietly, and I eat quietly, and when we
finish she pushes her plate away and folds her arms on the table.

"Breno's going to call you this afternoon," she says.

"What for?"

"He's going to offer you Corvel."

I don't understand. She sees I don't understand and unfolds her arms.

"The whole neighborhood. The program. The city approved it yesterday. It's three
hundred and twelve buildings from before, all of them with the mesh reading
things it can't explain. Breno won the bid because of you, Helena. Because he
said he had someone who was there."

"I wasn't there in three hundred and twelve buildings."

"No. But you know how they did things. You know where to look. You know where
somebody in 2030 would have screwed up at four in the morning on a pour in the
rain." She smiles. "Nobody else knows. The books don't say. The files don't
open."

"And how long is the program?"

Noor looks at me like someone about to say something she knows is big.

"Five years."

---

Breno calls me at three.

He's sitting on his usual crate, on the ninth floor of the Tessel building, which
has become his office, with the light table floating in front of him and my
building turning inside it. The yellow line of the cold joint on ten now has
something green around it, new reinforcement that the mesh is going to grow from
the inside, slowly, over the next months.

He doesn't make a speech. He's not the type.

"Five years," he says. "Chief origin consultant. The job didn't exist, so I made
it up, like you told me to. You decide where the mesh looks first. You train
the team to know what to ask a building from before. You sign the reports
together with Noor."

He points at something written in the air, beside the table, which I don't read.

"The pay is that. I don't know if it's a lot. I've never paid anyone from
before."

I look at the number. I don't know if it's a lot either. I still don't know the
price of a coffee. But I know what Ciça told me, one night, without my asking,
watching the rain: that the family's money had all gone on bringing me back, and
that she didn't regret it, and that I shouldn't think about it. I do think about
it. I think about it every time the café counter says *dependent: Cecília*.

"And the authorization?" I ask.

"Already asked. She authorized it in two minutes." He gives his dry laugh. "She's
quick, your daughter."

"She is."

"Five years, Helena." He looks at me, serious, in a way he doesn't usually look.
"That's a long time for someone who just got here. If you want to think about
it..."

I don't want to think about it.

Five years. I do the arithmetic without meaning to, because the arithmetic comes
by itself. In five years I'll be forty-three. The body will be seven. The
clinic's contract says that in five years the dependency ends by itself, if
nobody has asked for emancipation before then. In five years I'll order a coffee
and the counter will say my name.

And in five years Corvel will be standing. Three hundred and twelve buildings
from before, splinted, their cold joints found, their extra steel explained. A
whole neighborhood the way we used to build, kept standing the way they do now.
The city from before under the city of now, and someone, for the first time,
looking after both at once.

"Where do I sign?" I ask.

Breno points at the edge of the table. I press my thumb to it. The edge waits,
and I don't shake, and it lights up green.

---

I leave Tessel at five twenty, before the walkways get busy, and I don't take the
transit.

I walk.

It's the first time I've done the route from Corvel to Ostrel on foot, along the
walkways, and it takes an hour and a half, and I don't mind. I walk over the city
at the end of the afternoon, with the heat starting to ease, with the low sun
hitting the façades from the side and lighting up the empty channels of the dry
season. I walk among the people going home. And I notice, halfway, on a wide
walkway over the neighborhood of gardens, something that makes me stop.

I didn't look up.

I crossed three forty-meter spans without a column and didn't look up once. I was
thinking about something else: a slab on Aster Street, Noor's son who wants to
be a musician, what I was going to make for dinner. I walked over a walkway I
don't know what is made of the way I used to walk, in 2031, over a slab I had
calculated myself. Without thinking it could fall. Because it doesn't.

I stand still in the middle of the walkway and people step around me.

I belong to this, I think. I don't know when it happened. It happened.

---

In Corvel, on the way, there's a shop.

I saw it the first time I walked along Tessel and didn't go in. It's a small
shop, on a ground floor, on one of the few streets in Corvel where people still
walk down below, with a window of real glass and, behind the glass, old things.
Things from before. A watch with hands. A phone with a cracked screen, which I
recognize, the model my sister had, displayed in a glass case like a jewel. A
stack of paper books. And, hanging on the back wall, a guitar.

Today I go in.

The owner is a very old man who looks at me with an attention nobody else has
here, because he looks at things. I take the guitar down from the wall. It's
light-colored wood, the varnish worn where somebody's arm rested on it for years,
and it has six strings, steel, rusted, and a label inside, through the sound
hole, with a date I read by tilting the instrument to the light. 2019.

Ciça studied music. She taught her whole life. I don't know what. I never asked,
and she never said, and in her house there's something that plays music in the
air, from anywhere, with no instrument at all, and no instrument. None. A music
teacher of a hundred and fifteen without an instrument in the house.

"How much?" I ask.

The old man tells me a number. I touch my wrist to his counter, which is wood and
shouldn't know anything, and does. *Helena*, it shows, for the first time, in
small letters. Not *dependent*, like at the café. My name. Maybe because the
counter is old like its owner and doesn't know about contracts; maybe because the
money is from my work and something, somewhere, decided to take that into
account. I don't ask. I stand looking at my name until it fades.

I leave the shop with the guitar under my arm, the way Noor says people used to
carry a roof in their hand.

---

I get home at seven fifteen.

"I'm home," I say, loudly, to the whole house.

I take my boots off in the hallway, one at a time. I lean the guitar against the
wall. I can't wait; I go into the living room in my socks, with the hard hat
under one arm and the guitar under the other, and Ciça is in the armchair, by
the window, with the late-afternoon light in her white hair, and she turns her
face to me.

"Five years," I say. "Ciça. Five years."

She doesn't understand right away. Then she does. I see her understand, and I
see something cross her face, very fast, that I can't read, and then her whole
face opens in a smile I haven't seen since she was six and I came home from the
site with a present.

"Breno gave you Corvel."

"You knew."

"I authorized it." She laughs, breathless. "He said it was the whole
neighborhood. Mom. The whole neighborhood."

"Three hundred and twelve buildings."

"In five years."

"In five years." I kneel in front of the armchair, the way I did at the clinic,
on the first day, and I put the hard hat on the floor and the guitar in her lap,
on the throw, slowly. "And this is for you. I don't know if it still plays. The
strings are rotten. We'll change them."

Ciça looks at the guitar.

She looks for a length of time I can't measure. She puts her hand on it, the
thin, spotted hand, on the light wood, where the varnish is worn. She runs her
fingers over the rusted strings, and the strings sound, out of tune, a low,
crooked chord that fills the room.

"Two thousand nineteen," she says, reading the label through the sound hole,
without needing to tilt it. "It's older than I am."

"Everything here is newer than you."

"Except this." She smiles at the guitar. "And except you."

She tries to lift the guitar, to set it properly in her lap, in the playing
position. She tries with both hands. The neck comes up, and the body rests
against her leg, and she curves her back over it, slowly, the way she must have
curved it thousands of times in a classroom, and puts the fingers of her left
hand on the neck, on the frets, making a chord I don't know.

And stops.

She doesn't stop like someone who stops playing. She stops like a machine that
switches off.

Her fingers stay on the neck of the guitar. Her face stays turned down, toward
the strings. And I hear, before I understand, a sound coming from her, from her
chest, a small sound, like a door trying to open and not opening.

"Ciça?"

She doesn't answer. Her mouth is open. The air isn't going in.

"Ciça."

She slides. The guitar slides with her, hits the floor, and the rotten strings all
scream at once, and she slides off the armchair forward, toward me, and I catch
her, the way I caught her at the clinic, before the floor, and she weighs
nothing, and she isn't breathing.

"House!" I shout. "House, she isn't breathing!"

The calm voice appears in the middle of the room.

"Cardiorespiratory event. Clinic notified. Team on the way. Estimated time, four
minutes."

"What's wrong with her? What is this? Tell me what to do!"

"Lay Mrs. Cecília on her back, Ms. Helena. Tilt her head back." A minimal pause.
"I'm not authorized to discuss Mrs. Cecília's medical condition."

"What condition?" I shout, laying my daughter on the rug, tilting her head, her
white hair spread over the place where I slept last month counting joints. "What
condition? Has this happened before?"

The house doesn't answer.

The arm comes down from the ceiling faster than I've ever seen it come down, and
it brings something at its tip, something smooth, which it presses to my
daughter's chest, over her dress. The thing hums. Her body jumps once, small,
like a hiccup.

I hold her hand. It's cold and light and doesn't squeeze mine back.

On the floor, beside my leg, the guitar from 2019 is lying on its side, with the
sound hole to the wall and the label hidden, and nobody sets it right.
