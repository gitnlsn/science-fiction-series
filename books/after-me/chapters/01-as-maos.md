I wake up and look at my hands.

They're mine. I know that before I know anything else—before the room, before
asking what room this is, before remembering that I had died. I know the shape
of the nails, broad and short like my father's. I know the left thumb, which
bends back farther than it should and which I used to show off at children's
parties to make the other kids scream. I know the mole on my wrist, the size of
a lentil, where the watch strap always stopped.

The hands are mine. They just don't have the scar.

I had one here, on the side of my right index finger: a white line two
centimeters long. It was on my first building site, at twenty-three, when I
grabbed a length of rebar by the wrong end. I got four stitches and came back
the next day with my finger bandaged, proud, because on a site the people with
scars were the people who had been there. I run my thumb over where it used to
be. The skin is the same as the rest of the finger. Smooth. No memory at all.

I stay looking at the finger. I don't know for how long. Time, here, doesn't
have a size yet.

Then I look up, because I'm an engineer and up is where I look when I walk into
a place.

The ceiling is a single piece.

I look for the joint. Every ceiling has one—panel against panel, slab against
slab, some line where the material can expand in the heat without cracking. I
spent my whole life looking for those lines, and drawing them, and fighting with
foremen who wanted to hide one behind the plaster. This ceiling has none. It's
white, continuous, slightly curved at the corners, as if the room had been
poured all at once and not assembled. And when I look longer, I see it isn't
white. There's a light inside it. Faint, even, no bulb. The light comes from
the material.

"That doesn't exist," I say.

The voice that comes out isn't mine. It is, and it isn't. It has my timbre, but
it comes out hoarse in a way that isn't the hoarseness of someone who's been
asleep: it's a new throat. A throat that has never shouted across a site, never
had the flu, never sung in the shower.

"It does, actually," someone answers. "But it's good to hear you say so. It
means you're looking."

---

He's on the other side of a glass wall, sitting in an ordinary chair, elbows on
his knees. A man of about forty, shaved head, a sand-colored shirt with no
buttons at all. What frightens me isn't him. It's the voice, which doesn't come
through the glass: it arrives close to my left ear, low and clear, as if he were
sitting at the head of the bed. I turn my head to look for the speaker. There
isn't one.

"The voice only goes to you," he says. "If there were anyone else in the room,
they wouldn't hear it. Sorry. I forget that it's frightening."

"Who are you?"

"Ravi. I work with people who come back."

He says *people who come back* the way you'd say *people coming in from the
airport*. Like a kind of person that exists, that there are enough of to need a
name.

"You're going to ask me if this is the first time we've talked," he says. "It
isn't. It's the twelfth."

"I don't remember."

"I know. The other eleven were short, and they weren't sticking yet. That's
normal. Today it sticks." He smiles, and the smile is tired in a good way, the
smile of someone who has said that sentence to a lot of people and still thinks
it's worth saying. "The third time, you looked at the ceiling first too."

I sit up. Or I try. The body obeys, but late, like a structure that takes the
load and needs half a second to answer. The bed moves with me, raises the head
to exactly the angle I need before I ask. The mattress isn't a mattress; it's
something warm that settles against my back and then goes firm.

"Where am I?"

"At the clinic. In an isolation room. That's why the glass." He touches two
fingers to the transparent wall, on his side, and the glass goes darker for a
moment where he touches it. "You're not sick. It's the opposite. Your body
doesn't know anything. No viruses, no bacteria, not one cold. It's getting to
know them a little at a time, with the vaccines. Until then, the only things
that come in here are filtered air and me."

"You don't come in."

"Not yet."

I want to ask everything. The questions pile up in the new throat and none of
them gets through. So I ask the silliest one.

"What day is it?"

"Tuesday."

I laugh. A strange sound comes out, higher than my laugh, and I stop, startled
by it. Ravi waits. He knows how to wait. It seems to be half his job.

---

I fall asleep again without deciding to. When I wake up it's still Tuesday, or
it's another day already; the light in the ceiling hasn't changed, and there's
no window. The wall across from the glass is an opaque gray, and I understand,
without anyone telling me, that it doesn't have to be. That there's something
behind it, and that someone has decided I shouldn't see what yet.

I'm alone. I push back the sheet.

The body under the clinic gown is my body and it isn't. It's my body as I've
never seen it, because nobody sees their own body like this: without a single
mark. The knees don't have the two dark patches from a whole adolescence of
indoor volleyball. The shin doesn't have the scar from the bicycle. On my right
shoulder, where the sun of every site I ever worked left one freckle after
another, the skin is all one tone, pale, almost paper. This body has never been
in the sun. This body has never taken a fall.

I lift the gown up to my waist, and I already know what I'm going to find
before I look, and I look anyway.

The C-section was on a Thursday morning, in the rain. The scar sat low, just
above the hair, a twelve-centimeter line the doctor called pretty, as if that
were a compliment. For six years it itched before every rain. I knew it was
going to rain before the sky did. I'd say: *it's going to rain, get the
umbrella,* and Ciça would laugh at me, and then it would rain, and she'd look
at me as if I were a magic person.

I put my hand there.

Nothing. A smooth belly. The belly of someone who has never—

I don't finish the sentence. Not even inside.

I keep my hand spread on the skin, still, like someone waiting to feel a kick
that doesn't come, and doesn't come, and isn't going to come. Then I pull the
gown back down and cover myself with the sheet up to my chin and stay like that.

Ciça.

It's the first time I've thought of her by name. Until now she was in me the way
the floor is: so much that I didn't need to look. Now the name arrives and I
hold on to the sheet, because I know the arithmetic that comes after the name,
and I don't want to do it yet.

---

I decide to stand up.

It isn't a good decision, and I know that while I'm making it. But I spent my
life climbing scaffolding to see with my own eyes what the report said, and I'm
not going to lie here waiting for someone to tell me how big the floor is.

I put my feet over the side of the bed. The floor is warm. Not the warm of a
heated floor, which heats evenly; it's a warmth that appears under my feet when
my feet arrive, and that gets firmer where I put my weight, as if the floor knew
where I was going to step a moment before I did. I brace my hands on the
mattress. I push.

The legs hold. They don't hold well. They shake in the thighs, a fine tremor, the
muscles of someone who has never climbed a stair, and I stand up the way a child
stands up: surprised to be up there. The room sways once and stops.

Three steps to the glass. I count. A meter and a half, more or less, and it
takes me as long as crossing a wide street would. On the second step the floor
goes harder under my left foot, which is the one that's failing, and I
understand that it's holding me up. I don't like it. I don't like being held up
by a floor.

I reach the glass and put both hands on it.

On the other side Ravi's chair is empty, and behind it the room is dark, and
because of the dark the glass turns into a mirror. I see myself.

It's me.

It's me at thirty-eight, the age I was. The face is my face, the nose a little
crooked to the left, the wide mouth from my mother's side of the family, the
thick eyebrows I never learned how to shape. But everything the face had earned
is missing. There are no two lines between the eyebrows, from frowning up into
the sun. There's no acne mark on the chin, from adolescence. There are no dark
circles from six years of waking up in the middle of the night, and none of the
others, the last ones, the ones from the illness, which were dark and deep and
which I stopped looking at in the bathroom mirror toward the end. The skin is
all one tone. The eyes are my eyes, and that's how I recognize myself, and even
so they look at me out of a face that seems just taken out of the box.

It's the face I would have had if I hadn't lived.

I rest my forehead on the glass. It's cold, and it's the first cold thing I've
felt since I woke up. I stay there, breathing, and my breath fogs the glass in a
small circle, and the circle disappears too fast, as if the glass were also
looking after me.

"You should be lying down," says Ravi's voice, close to my ear, in no hurry.

"I know."

"The seventh time you got up too. You fell on the fourth step."

"Today it was three."

"Today it was three," he agrees, and I think he's smiling, wherever he is.

I go back to the bed the way I came, counting. The floor holds my left foot
again, and this time I let it.

---

An arm comes down from the ceiling.

It doesn't come down: the ceiling opens along a thin line I hadn't seen—so
there was a joint, I think, absurdly relieved, *so there was a joint*—and out of
it comes a slender arm, jointed at five points, bone-colored. It makes no sound
at all. It stops beside my head and waits, like a well-mannered animal.

"May I?" asks Ravi's voice, close to my ear. He isn't at the glass. He's
somewhere, watching.

"May you what?"

"The vaccine. It's the eleventh. You don't remember the others, but your arm
does."

The bone arm presses a transparent square, the size of a stamp, to the top of
my shoulder. It's warm. It sticks to the skin with no glue that I can feel, and
for a few seconds it tingles, and then it's gone. It doesn't fall off: it's
gone. The skin absorbs the whole square, and there's a pink mark left that fades
while I watch.

"There's no needle," I say.

"There hasn't been for a long time."

"How long?"

He doesn't answer. The arm withdraws, the ceiling closes, the joint disappears
again, and I keep looking at the place where it was, trying to fix the exact
position in my memory, the way I used to with the cracks in an inspection
report. One meter twenty from the glass wall. Parallel to the bed.

Then comes something else, which slides along the sheet to my left wrist: a kind
of open bracelet, smooth, that closes by itself and squeezes lightly, and warms,
and lets go. I know they've taken blood because I see, at its edge, a thin red
line climbing inside a transparent channel. I didn't feel anything. I look at my
wrist. There's no puncture.

That frightens me more than the ceiling. A body that doesn't bleed where they
take blood.

"Ravi."

"I'm here."

"Was there time?"

He understands. I can see he understands from the pause, which is short and
exact, the pause of someone who recognizes the question because he's heard it
before, from other mouths.

"There was," he says. "Eleven minutes."

---

Fourteen minutes.

That was the number the doctor gave me in 2031, sitting on the edge of my bed, in
my home, with his hand on top of mine, as if he were giving me good news. He was
a young man, badly shaven, and he spoke slowly so I'd understand, but I
understood fast, because I'd spent my life calculating how long things hold
before they give.

*After the heart stops, Mrs. Helena, we have about fourteen minutes. The team
will be in the next room. You won't feel anything. It's the ones who stay who
feel the fourteen minutes.*

I remember the kitchen that morning. The light coming in through the window over
the sink, which was the reason we'd rented that apartment and not another one. I
remember the team's cases in the hallway, big, silver, leaning against the wall
like the luggage for a trip that wasn't mine. I remember Ciça's shoes by the
door, the Velcro ones, one of them tipped on its side.

I remember saying something to her.

I don't remember what.

I try. I close my eyes and go back to the kitchen, to the window, to the cases,
to the shoe on its side, and the sentence is there, at the edge, the size of a
short sentence, and I can't reach it. I know it was important. I know I'd been
thinking about it for days. I know she heard it.

"Ravi. The last thing I remember. Does that come back?"

"Sometimes." He takes a while. "Sometimes the last day is what stays most whole.
Sometimes it's what stays most broken. The reading doesn't choose. What was
there is here."

"The reading."

"Of your brain. The original." He says this carefully, like someone stepping
onto a staircase he doesn't know. "It was read and then written into this one.
It took seven weeks to write. You really woke up three days ago. Today is the
first time it's sticking."

I touch my own head. The hair is short, soft as a child's hair, hair that has
never been dyed or burned by the sun.

"And this body?"

"It's two years old."

I'm thirty-eight years old in a body that's two. I laugh again, the high laugh,
and this time I don't stop, because it's ridiculous, it's ridiculous and it's
true, and Ravi waits for the laughing to end without laughing along, and when it
ends I'm crying, and he waits for that to end too.

---

When I can, I ask what I didn't want to ask.

"What year?"

He gets up. For the first time he comes to the glass, and stands on the other
side, close, and I see he has dark circles and a smear of blue ink on his shirt
cuff. He puts his hand on the glass. I don't know if it's a protocol gesture or
if it's his.

"Two thousand one hundred and forty."

I do the arithmetic.

I do it twice, the way I do with a beam load, because the first time you always
do it with hope. A hundred and nine years. I don't make mistakes in
subtraction. I didn't in 2031 and I don't now, with this new brain where they
put everything that was in the old one, subtraction included.

A hundred and nine years.

I think of my father, who was already dead. I think of my sister, who had two
sons, and of the two sons, who would be, who were, who got old and then stopped
being. I think of Joana, from the office, who laughed too loud and taught me not
to apologize in meetings. I think of the building on the avenue, the fourteen-story
one, where I calculated the slabs and which was meant to last fifty years, and I
do its arithmetic too, and it's gone past its term twice. I think of everyone,
one by one, and everyone turns into the same thing.

And then I get to her. The arithmetic I'd been putting off since I saw my belly.

Ciça was six.

Six plus a hundred and nine.

I don't do it. I don't need to. Nobody lives that long. In 2031 the oldest
person who had ever existed didn't make it to a hundred and twenty-three, and
the magazines still wrote about her as if she were a miracle. Ciça lived, grew
up, had her whole life without me, and at some point in that life, which I
didn't see, she died, and nobody told me, because I wasn't anywhere you could be
told.

That I can bear. I find out that I can. It was the price I knew I was paying,
sitting on the bed, with the doctor's hand on top of mine. I knew that if I came
back, I'd come back to after everyone.

"Who asked for it?" I say. "Who paid to bring me back?"

Because someone paid. Nobody does this for free, not even in 2140; I saw the
silver cases.

Ravi looks at me through the glass. There's something on his face I don't know
how to read, and that bothers me, because I spent my life reading the faces of
foremen who were hiding a problem.

"Your daughter," he says.

"My daughter was six."

"I know."

"Then it's a granddaughter. A great-granddaughter. Someone in the family who
found my name in a record, who..."

"Helena."

He waits for me to stop. I stop.

"She's alive. She's a hundred and fifteen."

I look at my hands. They're the only thing in this room I know, and now not even
them.
