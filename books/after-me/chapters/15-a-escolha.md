Irene climbs the nine floors by the stairs.

I hear her before I see her. I'm in Breno's office, on the ninth floor on Tessel,
sitting on his crate, alone, because Breno left early and Noor is on Aster Street,
looking at my building turning on the light table, and I hear someone on the fire
stairs, far below, climbing slowly. A step. A long stop. A step. A breathing I
recognize without knowing, short, the breathing of an old person who isn't used to
climbing.

When she appears on the ninth-floor landing, she's white. Really white, the color
of the wall, with her forehead wet and her hands clutching a cardboard box in front
of her body, and she doesn't look to the sides. She doesn't look at the window with
no glass, where the whole city hangs far below, or at the open elevator shaft. She
only looks at me.

"You're afraid of heights," I say.

"I am." She breathes. "Nine floors. I've never been up this high. Not even in the
transit."

"Why didn't you leave it with Noor?"

"Because I told Grandma I could do it."

She crosses the empty floor, slowly, without looking down, and stops in front of
me, and holds out the box. It's an old cardboard box, the kind that doesn't exist
anymore, with tape marks at the corners, tape on tape, and the lid loose.

"It's Grandma's," she says. "It's for you."

I take the box. It's light.

Irene doesn't leave. She stands there, her hands empty now, not knowing what to do
with them, and then she pushes them into her coat pockets.

"I'm going down," she says. "Before I pass out. But I wanted to tell you
something."

"Tell me."

"She spent the night telling me things about my mother. Things I never knew. Until
five in the morning." Irene looks at the box in my hands. "I think she's saying
goodbye to everything. One thing at a time."

I don't say anything. She nods, as if I had, and turns, and goes to the stairs, and
goes down, a step, a long stop, a step, and I keep listening until I can't hear her
anymore.

---

I open the box on the fourth-floor landing.

I don't know why there. I go down with it in my hands, and on the fourth floor I
stop, and sit on the step, beside Rafael's letters, *R.S. 28*, worn by a century of
feet, and put the box in my lap, and lift the lid.

The shoe is on top.

Navy blue. Canvas. With the Velcro that doesn't hold. Upright, with the toe pointing
out of the box, toward me. It fits entirely in the palm of my hand. I bought that
shoe in a store at the mall, on a Saturday afternoon, with Ciça trying on three
pairs and wanting the light-up ones, and me saying the light-up ones broke, and her
crying, and then forgetting, and then loving these.

She kept it. A hundred and nine years. Just the one shoe.

Under the shoe, four yellowed sheets, ruled. The sheets I bought at the shop in
Corvel to make sketches on and never used. Folded. Written in pencil, in large,
shaky handwriting that goes down and up along the line like a seismograph.

I recognize the pencil before the handwriting. The broad, flat stroke that thickens
on the curve. It's my carpenter's pencil.

I read.

I read sitting on the step, beside Rafael's letters, with the shoe in my other hand.
I read it once, fast. I read it again, slowly. I read it a third time, the way I do
with a beam load, and by the third time I can't see the letters properly anymore,
and it isn't because of her handwriting.

*It was a blanket.*

*It's paid with interest.*

*I know what it is to go away out of love.*

*After me, nothing.*

*Now you're the one who decides which way.*

I sit with the sheets in my lap and the shoe in my hand until the light changes in
the landing window, until the end of the afternoon, until the rain starts outside,
a little after four, exactly when she must have said it would start.

---

On Monday, I order a coffee.

On the low walkway in Corvel, at the place with *the usual*, with Noor beside me,
pretending she isn't watching. I touch my wrist to the counter.

*Helena.*

That's all. In small, polite letters. No other word. Not *dependent*, not *Cecília*,
nothing. My name, on its own, and the coffee appearing.

I stand looking at my name until it fades. Then I pick up the coffee and don't
drink it.

"And then?" asks Noor. She learned that from Irene, I think, from the messages. Or
from her son. Everybody learned *and then* from somebody.

"And then I'm free," I say.

"And is that good?"

I think. I look at the city through the glass of the walkway, the green roofs, the
empty channels, the other walkways up above, the people who don't look up.

"I don't know," I say. "I've never been."

---

Noor offers me a room.

In her place, in Corvel, in a building from before, one of the three hundred and
twelve, which she says I'll have to fix anyway. Her son, the one who wants to be a
musician, would like it, she says. He has questions about before. He wants to know
if it's true that songs had a beginning and an end.

I say I'll think about it. And I think.

I think for sixteen days.

I work. I go to Aster Street, to an eight-story building in Corvel with a column
that someone in 2033 cut in half to run a pipe through, and I find the column, and
the mesh lights up yellow, and Breno says *well, fuck me*, and I don't feel
anything. I sleep in the office on nine. I eat little. One night, alone, I lie down
on the floor of the ninth floor, on my back, with my arms spread, and look at the
ceiling, which is the slab of the tenth, my slab, and I look for the cold joint, and
find it, a thin dark line crossing the concrete on a diagonal, from a corner to
nearly the center, and I lie looking at it until the small hours.

The cold joint. The pour that stopped halfway because it rained and the pump broke.
Yesterday's concrete that had already set when today's arrived. Two parts of the
same slab, made on different days, that didn't bond properly, and that I had
scabbled and treated, and that have been together for a hundred and twelve years.
Not whole. Not the way they were meant to be. But together. Holding the same
weight.

You can live on top of a cold joint. I know that. I ran the numbers.

---

It isn't the sentence that decides me.

I thought it would be. I thought, in the first days, that I'd keep reading *I'll
come back when you need me* until it turned into something, until I found out
whether it was a promise or a blanket, whether I'd said it believing it or not, and
that that would tell me what to do. It won't. I don't remember. I'll never remember.
The reading doesn't choose.

It isn't the contract. The contract is over. She asked for emancipation. I'm not
anybody's dependent anymore, and the café says my name, and nobody in the world can
make me walk into that room again.

It isn't the debt. She gave it back. It's paid with interest.

That's it. That's exactly what decides me, and I only understand it on the
sixteenth day, lying on the floor of nine, looking at the cold joint: that now
nobody is asking.

She took everything away. The contract, the sentence, the debt, the dependency. She
took one thing away at a time, like Irene said, saying goodbye. And what was left,
when she'd taken everything away, was a thirty-eight-year-old woman lying on the
floor of a building she calculated, with no obligation at all, free, with a child's
shoe in her hand.

And the woman wants to go back.

*The hand on the forehead only counts when nobody asked.*

Nobody is asking.

---

I leave Tessel late in the afternoon on the seventeenth day, before the rain, and I
don't take the transit.

I walk. Along the walkways, over the city, from Corvel to Ostrel, an hour and a half,
with the box under my arm, the way people used to carry a roof in their hand. The
rain catches me halfway, on a wide walkway over the neighborhood of gardens, and
beats on the milky roof above me, in weight, and I don't look up. I don't need to. I
know what's holding it.

I walk into the building in Ostrel at two minutes past seven.

I go up in the transit, this time, because it's twenty-two floors and I'm no girl
anymore. I stop at the door. The door recognizes me and starts to open before I get
there, and this time I let it.

In the hallway, I take off my boots.

One at a time, with a hand on the wall. They're full of mud from Corvel. I stand
them side by side by the door. And, beside them, I take the navy canvas shoe out of
the box, and put it on the floor, upright, lined up, neatly lined up.

With the toe pointing in.

"I'm home," I say.

Loudly. To the whole house.

---

Irene is on the couch. She sits up with a jolt, the blanket over her shoulders, and
looks at me, and opens her mouth, and closes it. She doesn't say anything. She
gestures with her chin, toward the window.

Ciça is in the armchair. Asleep. Sitting up, her head fallen to one side, the throw
over her knees, her mouth slightly open, and her breathing short with the wheeze at
the end. She's smaller than six weeks ago. I don't know how it's possible to get
smaller than she already was. She has.

I cross the room. I don't make a sound.

I stop in front of the armchair and look at her. At the thousand lines of her face,
at the spots on her hands, each one a summer, at the ring held on by the knuckle, at
the nearly faded mole at the corner of her left eye.

And I put my hand on her forehead.

Nobody asked.

She opens her eyes. Slowly. She isn't startled. She looks up at me, with her pale,
wet eyes, and doesn't say anything for a while, and my hand stays there, on her
forehead, warm, with the calluses at the base of the fingers, not shaking.

"You came back," she says.

Her voice is almost nothing.

"I came back."

"Why?"

I think of a lot of answers. Because you need me. Because I promised. Because you
brought me back. Because I'm your mother. They're all true, and none of them is the
one I came to say.

"Because I wanted to," I say. "Nobody asked."

Ciça closes her eyes. The tears run down the thousand lines, finding no straight
path, and she doesn't wipe them, and I don't take my hand away.

Behind me, on the couch, Irene is crying too, quietly, with her wide mouth pressed
against her fist.

In the hallway, beside my boots, a child's shoe is standing upright, with the toe
pointing in.

Someone came in.
