Irene was sleeping on the couch.

She had been for three weeks. She had arrived the next night, with a small
suitcase and a succulent that she put on the windowsill, and she didn't ask if she
could stay; she stayed. She slept badly, on a couch too short for her, with her
feet sticking out, and she woke at three in the morning every night and went over
to the armchair and stood looking at her grandmother. She didn't tell anyone.
Cecília knew, because she woke too, and saw her granddaughter in the dark counting
something with her lips, without a sound.

The breathing. Irene was counting her breathing, the way she had counted Lívia's.

Her mother hadn't come back.

She was on Tessel. Irene knew; Noor had let her know, on the second night, a
short, polite message from someone who didn't know whether she should get
involved: *she's here, on the ninth floor, sleeping in the office. She's okay. She
doesn't want to talk.* Since then, Irene had heard from Noor every two or three
days. Her mother was working. Her mother was eating little. Her mother had spent a
whole night sitting on the fourth-floor landing, beside some letters scratched
into the concrete, and Noor couldn't explain why.

Cecília didn't ask. Irene told her anyway, in the morning, with the coffee, in a
low voice, like someone giving news of a relative in the hospital.

---

That night, at three, Cecília opened her eyes and spoke.

"You're counting."

Irene jumped. She was standing in the middle of the living room, barefoot, in
pajamas, with the couch blanket over her shoulders, and she froze like a child
caught in the kitchen.

"I wasn't..."

"My breathing. I used to count your mother's."

Irene didn't say anything. After a while, she pulled the chair from the table by
the window over to the armchair, without a sound, and sat, and stayed there with
the blanket over her shoulders, looking at her grandmother in the dark.

"I know," she said. "I used to see you. At three in the morning. I thought it was
the saddest thing in the world."

"And now?"

"Now I think it's the only thing you can do."

Cecília smiled in the dark. Her granddaughter didn't see it, but heard it,
perhaps, because she smiled too; Cecília heard the air change.

"Your mother called me Mom for the first time in the backyard of a house that
doesn't exist anymore," said Cecília. "She was a year and two months old. She was
sitting in the dirt, with a spoon in her hand, digging. I was at the window. She
looked up and said *Mom*, and then went back to digging, as if it were nothing."

"I didn't know that."

"Nobody knows. I never told anyone." Cecília breathed, and the air went in short,
with the wheeze at the end. "I have a whole lot of things I never told, Irene. I
kept them all. I thought keeping was looking after."

"And it isn't?"

"Sometimes it is. Sometimes it's just keeping."

Irene was quiet. Outside, the city was dark, with the walkways lit in a row, like
a seam of light between the buildings.

"Tell me another one," Irene said at last, quietly. "About Mom."

And Cecília told her. She told her until five. She told her about the loose tooth
Lívia wouldn't let anyone pull, and the first boyfriend, who played the trumpet
badly, and the fight the two of them had when Lívia was seventeen and walked out
of the house slamming the door and came back three hours later with a bag of
bread. Irene listened with the blanket over her shoulders, without interrupting,
laughing sometimes, once crying, and Cecília thought that she had never told her
granddaughter any of this because she thought her granddaughter didn't want to
know. And her granddaughter did. She had just never known what to ask.

At five, Irene fell asleep in the chair, sitting up, with her head fallen to one
side, her mouth slightly open. Just like her mother. Just like her
great-grandmother at the hospital.

Cecília sat watching. She didn't count anyone's breathing.

---

At seven, before Irene woke up, Cecília called the clinic.

Not Ravi. The woman's voice, the one from the clauses, the one that had read the
contract aloud nine months before with a pause after every sentence. The sober
panel lit up on the wall, with the clinic's name at the top.

"Sponsoring party, Cecília," said the voice. "How can I help you?"

"I want to request emancipation."

There was a pause. Not hesitation; procedure.

"The sponsoring party requests the emancipation of the restored person, Helena,
before the five-year term set out in the guardianship clause. Emancipation is
irrevocable. The restored person acquires full legal capacity, her own
registration, and independent access to resources. The sponsoring party ceases to
have any responsibility for or authority over the restored person. Do you
understand?"

"I understand."

"Do you wish to give a reason? The field is optional."

Cecília thought. She looked at the door of the room that didn't know anyone's
name, which had been closed for three weeks, with the wooden bed made, the cotton
sheet pulled smooth, Irene's succulent on the nightstand, which hardly ever needed
water.

"She isn't mine," she said.

The voice waited.

"The reason field accepts free text," it said at last. "Do you wish to record that
sentence?"

"I do."

"Recorded. Do you confirm the signature?"

She pressed her right thumb to the edge of the panel. The thumb shook. The edge
waited for the shaking to pass, as it always waited, with the patience of things
that are in no hurry because they aren't going to die. And it lit up green.

Somewhere in Corvel, on the ninth floor of a building in a splint, on a wrist that
was asleep, something changed its name.

---

That morning, Cecília asked for the box.

Irene brought it. She didn't need the ceiling arm: she climbed on a chair, afraid,
and took the box down from the top of the wardrobe, and came down slowly, and put
it in her grandmother's lap. She didn't ask what was inside. She looked at the
tape reinforced with more tape and didn't ask.

Cecília opened it. The yellow headscarf. The badge with no photo. The
fourth-birthday card. The shoe.

And the pencil.

It was a carpenter's pencil, flat, red, its point sharpened with a box cutter, the
way her mother used to do it, with short strokes, from the outside in. Her mother
always had that pencil behind her ear on site. She had left it on the kitchen table
on the last day, and Aunt Marta had kept it, and given it to Cecília, along with
the photograph, at sixteen.

Cecília had never written with it. She was afraid of using it up.

"Irene. Paper."

"Paper?"

"Real paper. There's some in the kitchen drawer. Mom's. She brought it from the
shop in Corvel."

Irene went to get it. She came back with a pad of yellowed sheets, ruled, which her
mother had bought at that shop of things from before, along with the guitar, to
make sketches, and had never used, because the light table did everything.

Cecília rested the pad on the lid of the box, in her lap. She picked up the pencil.
Her hand shook. It shook so much that the first letter came out like a
seismograph line, and she stopped, and waited, and breathed, and tried again.

It took two hours.

Irene sat on the floor, beside the armchair, not looking at the paper, looking at
the window. Now and then the house said something about her heart rate, and
Cecília made the *shut up* gesture, and the house shut up. Now and then Cecília
stopped and held the pencil still in the air for a long time, and Irene didn't say
anything. Once, Cecília cried, without a sound, and the tears fell on the paper and
blurred a word, and she wrote the word again, underneath, more crooked.

When she finished, there were four sheets. The handwriting was large, shaky, a
child's or an old woman's, both. The pencil had worn down a centimeter.

---

*Mom,*

*you said I ordered you. You're right. I chose the box on the contract. I knew
what I was writing. I brought you back to look after me until I die and I didn't
tell you, and I watched you look after me, and every time you did I was happy and
ashamed at the same time. That's true and I'm not going to say it isn't.*

*But I want to tell you something I couldn't say that night, because the house was
talking and I had no air.*

*I didn't bring you back only because I needed you. I brought you back because you
said you'd come back. I was six and I asked, and you said it. And I kept it. I kept
it so hard that it turned into something you owed me. And I called it in.*

*You told me the sentence was what you say to a child. That you never thought you'd
wake up. I believe you. I spent the whole night thinking and I believe you. You
said what I needed to hear to leave that room and go have ice cream with Aunt
Marta. It was a good thing you did. I spent a hundred and nine years thinking it
was a promise, and it was a blanket.*

*So I'm giving it back to you.*

*The sentence. You don't owe me anything. I already called it in, and you came, and
you didn't do braids, because my hair won't braid anymore, but you made pasta, and
wiped my chin, and slept in a hospital chair with your mouth open, and counted the
joints with me, and thought I was magic. It's paid. It's paid with interest.*

*This morning I requested your emancipation. You aren't my dependent anymore. The
café will say your name. You can go wherever you want. You can stay on Tessel, you
can live in Corvel, you can fix the three hundred and twelve buildings and the ones
after that, and you never have to come back here again, and I'll understand, Mom. I
swear I'll understand. I had a mother who went away so I wouldn't see her stop
being herself. I know what it is to go away out of love.*

*I've left you half the apartment. Irene agreed. The other half is hers. It isn't
much. It's what's left.*

*And I'm not going to be preserved. I've already told Dr. Sabino. When I go, I go.
Nobody is going to be waiting in the next room with a silver case. Nobody is going
to have to pay anything or call anything in. After me, nothing.*

*It isn't sadness. It's that I know what it is to be the person left waiting for
someone to come back. And I don't want anyone to wait for me.*

*I'm sending along something of yours. You left it on its side by the door. I set
it right. A week later. By myself. I stood it up, with the toe toward the door, for
someone to put on and go out. Or come in.*

*Now you're the one who decides which way.*

*Ciça*

---

Before lunch, Dr. Sabino appeared on the wall.

Cecília had asked for him. She wanted to say it out loud, to someone who would
record it, with his face in front of her, and not only on paper. He was in his
office, on the other side of the city, with a white light behind him, and he looked
at her with thirty years of attention.

"Are you sure?" he asked. "The directive is irrevocable after death. The clinic can
do the preservation. You know it can. Your head is sound. You're exactly the case
it's meant for..."

"I know who it's meant for."

"Mrs. Cecília. With all due respect. You brought your mother back."

"I did."

"And you don't want anyone to...?"

"No." Cecília held his gaze. "After me, nothing."

Sabino was quiet. Cecília saw, in his face, the question he wasn't going to ask,
because he was a polite man, and because thirty years entitle you to a great deal
but not to everything. *Why?*

She answered it anyway.

"Because someone would have to stay in the next room," she said. "With the case.
And someone would have to have ice cream at the ice cream shop, without knowing.
And someone, a hundred years from now, would have to have the money and the reason.
I don't want to leave that to anybody, Doctor. Not even a seed. Not even a tree you
don't know will grow."

He nodded slowly.

"Recorded," he said. "Non-preservation directive. With your voice and your image."

"Thank you."

"Mrs. Cecília." He hesitated, his hand already at the corner of the panel, to
switch off. "Does your mother know?"

Cecília looked at the four yellowed sheets in her lap, folded, on top of the lid of
the box.

"She'll know today," she said. "In writing. It's the only way I had the courage."

---

Irene took the box at three.

Cecília put the four sheets inside, folded, and the shoe, upright, with the toe
pointing out of the box. She took out the rest: the headscarf, the badge, the card,
the pencil. Those were staying. Those were hers. She closed the lid. She didn't
tape it.

"Will you go all the way to Tessel?" she asked.

"I will."

"Up to the ninth floor?"

Irene took a while.

"It's high," she said.

"It is." Cecília looked at her granddaughter, who was afraid of heights and lived
on the third floor and had climbed on a chair that morning to get a box down from
the top of a wardrobe. "If you can't, leave it with Noor."

"I can."

Irene picked up the box. She held it in front of her body, with both hands, the way
she had held the succulent, the way she had held the printed contract. She stood
in the doorway.

"Grandma." Her voice came out strange. "What if she doesn't come back?"

Cecília thought about it. She had thought about it all night, and all morning, and
through the two hours of the pencil.

"Then she'll be fixing buildings," she said. "Which is what she does. And I'll have
had nine months of a mother I didn't have. And I'll have called in something that
wasn't a debt. And I'll have given it back."

"And you'll be alone."

"No." Cecília held out her hand, the spotted, thin hand that shook, and Irene,
after a moment, bent down and took it. "You're here."

Irene squeezed her grandmother's hand. She didn't say anything. She had her
great-grandmother's wide mouth pressed into a line, like someone holding back
tears, like a ceiling with no joint.

Then she went out, with the box.

The door closed by itself.

Cecília stayed in the armchair, looking at the window. It wasn't raining yet. It
wouldn't be long. Her left knee was starting to hurt, thin, inside, where bone
meets bone.

"It's going to rain," she said, to the house.

"The forecast shows rain at four ten, Mrs. Cecília."

"I know," said Cecília. "I knew before."
