The room had been Lívia's.

Not Lívia as a girl: Lívia as a girl had grown up in another house, in a
neighborhood the water took, in an apartment that no longer existed. This room
had been Lívia's at the end. She had come to live with her mother at eighty,
when her husband died and her body began to do, too early, what Cecília's body
would only begin to do at a hundred and thirteen. She had come with two
suitcases and a plant. She had stayed eleven months.

Cecília hadn't been inside it in nine years. The carer cleaned the room once a
week, without anyone asking, and kept the temperature and humidity just right
for the furniture, as if the furniture were the ones living there. The plant
had died in the second year. The carer had asked whether it should replace it,
and Cecília had said no, and the carer had taken the pot away and stored it.

Now she was in the doorway, in the chair, looking.

"Mrs. Cecília," said the carer, from its neutral point, which in there was over
by the window. "I've prepared three configuration proposals for the room. Would
you like me to show you?"

"Show me."

The wall lit up in three panels. In the first, the room had a wide bed that
adjusted itself to the position of the body, the window responded to the time
of day, the ceiling held a light that imitated sunrise, and a panel on the wall
reminded the occupant about vaccines, medication, water. In the second, all of
that and more: sensors in the floor, in the air, in the bedding, so that
nothing could happen to the occupant without someone knowing. The third was the
second, in different colors.

It was the perfect house for someone who had just come back. Cecília knew that.
She had read the clinic's materials, the guidance for families, the lists.
People who come back find everything strange, and the room should help. People
who come back have a child's immune system, and the room should watch. People
who come back can go into crisis on the first night, and the room should know
before.

"No," she said.

"Which one do you prefer?"

"None of them. I want a room that doesn't do anything."

The carer waited. Cecília heard it wait: it had a particular silence, for when
an instruction didn't fit in any of its drawers.

"I didn't understand, Mrs. Cecília."

"A bed that doesn't move. A cotton sheet, if there's still such a thing as
cotton. A window that's a window and that's all: light in the day, dark at
night, without anyone deciding. Nothing in the floor. Nothing in the air. No
reminders about medication. If she needs medication, I'll remind her."

"Mrs. Cecília, the clinic's guidance recommends..."

"I've read the guidance."

"A room without sensors won't know if she falls."

"I'll know."

It wasn't true, and both of them knew it. Cecília could barely get to her own
bathroom alone. If her mother fell at three in the morning in the next room,
she would know because the carer would tell her. But that was something else.
That was the house knowing. The room didn't need to know.

"I want her to have one place in this house that doesn't know her name,"
Cecília said, more quietly. "Like my armchair."

The carer was quiet for a time that, for it, was a long time.

"I understand," it said at last. And Cecília believed it, which was strange,
because she didn't believe the carer understood anything.

---

After dinner, which she barely ate, Cecília asked for the box.

The box was kept at the top of the wardrobe in her room, where no ceiling arm
could reach without an express order, and the express order was that nobody
open it. The carer brought it down and set it in her lap, on the throw, and
withdrew the arm, and kept a silence that was perhaps tact and perhaps just
having nothing to say.

It was an ordinary cardboard box, the kind nobody made anymore, reinforced at
the corners with tape, and the tape reinforced with more tape, a different
color, from a different decade. Cecília had last replaced the tape when she was
ninety-something. She lifted the lid.

There wasn't much inside. A headscarf, yellow with tiny flowers, which her
mother had worn in the last months, when her hair fell out. A site badge with a
passport photo that was no longer a photo, only a brownish smudge of someone
smiling. A carpenter's pencil, flat, its point sharpened with a box cutter. A
fourth-birthday card, with a drawing of a cake and, inside, her mother's
handwriting, in capitals, so her daughter could read it on her own: *FOR CIÇA,
WHO CAN ALREADY READ MY NAME*.

And the shoe.

Just one. The other had been lost in one of the moves, somewhere between twenty
and thirty, and she had cried over it the way she cried over almost nothing in
those years. It was a child's shoe, navy blue, canvas, with a Velcro strap, and
the Velcro hadn't held for eighty years. It fit entirely in the palm of her
hand. Cecília took it out of the box with both hands, because with one it shook.

It was the shoe that had been on its side. She knew that without remembering
it, the way you know certain things from childhood that nobody told you and
that you didn't see and that are true all the same. That morning, someone had
tripped over the pair, by the door—someone from the team, carrying a case, or
her aunt, in a hurry—and one of the shoes had been left tipped on its side,
with the sole to the wall. And nobody set it right. For the whole day, and the
next day, and the week, nobody set that shoe right, because nobody in that house
could look at the floor.

She set it right. At six, a week later, alone, without telling anyone. She
remembered that with a clarity she had for almost nothing else from that week:
crouched in the empty hallway, standing the shoe upright beside the other, neatly
lined up, with the toe toward the door. As if it were for someone to put on and
go out.

Or come in.

Cecília held the shoe for a length of time she didn't measure. The carer, which
measured everything, didn't say how long.

She had thought of putting it in the room. On the nightstand, beside the bed
that didn't move, above the cotton sheet. For her mother to arrive and see. For
her mother to understand, without anyone explaining, that someone had waited.
That was what the shoe said, and it said it better than anything Cecília knew
how to say with her mouth.

And that was exactly why she couldn't.

Putting the shoe in the room was telling. It was setting on her mother's
nightstand, the first night, a hundred and nine years of waiting, with the sole
turned toward her. Her mother would look at that shoe and feel she owed
something. And Cecília didn't want her mother to arrive owing. She wanted her
mother to arrive.

She put the shoe back in the box, beside the card, and closed the lid.

"You can put it away," she said.

The arm came down from the ceiling and took the box back to the top of the
wardrobe, where no arm could reach without an express order.

"Not yet," Cecília said, to no one, after the arm had withdrawn.

---

The contract came at eight in the evening.

It came the way official things came, in a sober panel that lit up on the
living room wall, with the clinic's name at the top and a long text below, and a
woman's voice, not Ravi's, reading the clauses one by one, in a neutral tone,
with a pause after each to ask whether she understood.

Cecília had asked for it to be read aloud. Her eyes could still manage, but they
tired; and she wanted to hear, too, what she was signing said by another mouth,
as if she were a witness and not a party.

*Sponsoring party: Cecília.* Her age, her neighborhood, Ostrel, her registration
number. *Restored person:* her mother's name, the date of birth, 1993, the date
of death, 2031. The date of preservation. The date of the reading. The date of
the writing.

Cecília listened to the dates the way you listen to the list of stops on a line
you know.

The voice read the cost clauses, which she already knew by heart. It read the
clauses on medical liability. It read the guardianship clause, which said that
the restored person would be, before the law, a dependent of the sponsoring
party until the sponsoring party requested her emancipation, or until five years
had passed, whichever came first. It read that part slowly. Cecília knew why:
families were frightened by the word *dependent*. They thought they were buying
a person.

"Do you understand?"

"I understand."

Then came the clause she had been waiting for.

"*Declared purpose of the restitution*," the voice read. "The law requires the
sponsoring party to declare the principal purpose. The declaration is for the
record and places no obligation on the restored person. The options are: family
reunion; professional or estate continuity; contribution to research;
end-of-life companionship. You may choose more than one."

Cecília sat looking at the list on the wall.

*Family reunion* was what nearly everyone chose. Ravi had said so, in one of
their first conversations, without her asking. It was the pretty option, the one
nobody would find odd, the one Irene, if she ever read it, would understand
without thinking twice. And it wasn't a lie. It was a daughter bringing back
her mother. It was a family reuniting after a hundred and nine years.

She could choose that one. She could choose only that one.

"Mrs. Cecília?" said the voice.

Cecília thought of the hand on the forehead. She thought of the blanket tucked
around her feet. She thought of the night in the armchair, two years before, and
the deadline, and the words *it's begun*. She thought that if she was going to
lie to her mother—and she was—at least on paper she wouldn't lie. At least
somewhere in the world it would be written down what was true, in the right
words, on a list of options someone had had the decency to write without
trimmings.

"End-of-life companionship," she said.

"Only that one?"

"Only that one."

"Recorded. Do you confirm the signature?"

She pressed her right thumb to the edge of the panel. The thumb shook a little,
and the edge waited for the shaking to pass, and then lit up green.

---

After the contract went dark, Cecília asked for the mesh.

The carer brought it, without comment, although it had opinions about the mesh
that it had made clear on other occasions. It was a thin garment, a silvery
gray, that went on like a long pair of tights and came up to the waist. When it
was switched on, it stiffened along certain lines, down the thighs, behind the
knees, along the shins, and did for the legs what the legs had stopped doing.
The doctor had prescribed it a year ago. Cecília had worn it three times. She
hated it. Walking in the mesh was like being carried by an invisible person who
decided how long your steps would be.

Putting it on took twenty minutes. The carer helped in the only way it knew how
to help, with the thin arm that came down from the ceiling and held the hem of
the mesh at the right angle while Cecília worked her feet in. At the end, she
was sweaty and angry, and sitting on the edge of the chair.

"Switch it on."

The mesh hummed softly, an almost insect sound, and she felt the lines stiffen
in her legs as if someone had put splints inside her skin.

Cecília braced her hands on the arms of the chair and pushed.

She stood.

The room swayed once and stopped. The mesh was holding. Her legs weren't
holding, but the mesh was, and she stood there, upright, in the middle of her
own living room, for the first time in months, with her heart beating too fast
and the carer saying, from the neutral point, something about her heart rate
that she didn't hear.

On Thursday, her mother was going to walk in through a door. Cecília had
decided, she didn't quite know when, that she would be standing. Not sitting in
the chair, not sunk in the armchair, not lying down. Standing. Her mother had
last seen her daughter standing, in the hallway, beside the silver cases, with a
shoe on its side. Her mother would see her standing again. It was a small thing
and it was the only thing about the meeting she could still choose.

She took a step. The mesh decided how long it was.

She took another.

On the third, her left knee gave way inside the mesh, and the mesh didn't give
way, and the mismatch between the two threw her weight to the side. The floor
warmed and hardened under her right foot, as it did, and the arm came down from
the ceiling faster than she had ever seen it come down, and caught her by the
elbow. She didn't fall. She was left crooked, half hanging, breathing.

"Mrs. Cecília, I recommend that you sit down."

"Three steps," Cecília said, between one breath and the next. "From the door to
where I'm going to be. It's three steps."

"You took three."

"I fell on the third."

"You didn't fall. I caught you."

Cecília stayed like that for a moment, held by the elbow by a bone arm that came
down from the ceiling. Then she laughed, a short, breathless laugh.

"She's going to love this," she said. "A floor that holds you up."

---

Ravi came on the voice at ten, when she was back in the armchair, out of the
mesh, her legs aching in a way she knew and almost missed, because it was the
ache of having done something.

"Are you all right? The house notified the clinic of a fall."

"It wasn't a fall. It was a rehearsal."

He didn't ask for what.

"Thursday, at three," he said. "She's ready. She's been asking every day for two
weeks. Today she asked twice."

Cecília closed her eyes. She stayed like that awhile, and Ravi waited, as he
always waited.

"Ravi. She's going to ask why."

"Why what?"

"Why I brought her back. She's going to ask. Maybe not me. Maybe you."

"Maybe."

"If she asks you..." Cecília opened her eyes and looked at the photograph on the
little table, her mother laughing in the sun, with her hand over her eyes. "You
tell her I wanted to see my mother. That's all. It's true."

Ravi took a while. Cecília pictured him on the other side, at the clinic, at the
end of a long day, with the smear of blue ink on his shirt cuff, weighing the
sentence.

"The declared purpose in the contract is something else, Mrs. Cecília."

"I know. I declared it."

"She has the right to read the contract."

"She does. If she asks."

"And if she asks?"

Cecília thought about it. She had thought about it a great deal, at night, and
she always came to the same place.

"Then she'll know," she said. "And it'll be because she asked. Not because I
told her."

There was a silence on his end. Then:

"Thursday, at three."

"Thursday, at three."

The voice went. The room went back to the size it was.

Cecília sat looking at the door of Lívia's room, which was another room now, a
room that didn't know anybody's name, with a cotton sheet the carer had found
who knows where and a window that was just a window. She thought of going in
there one last time, to see. She didn't. Her legs wouldn't go, and in any case,
there was nothing to see. An empty room waiting for someone.

She had spent a hundred and nine years being that.
