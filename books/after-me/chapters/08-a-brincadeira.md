Her mother came home at seven.

Cecília knew the time without looking, because at two minutes past seven the
door opened, and at three past her mother took off her boots in the hallway, one
at a time, with a hand on the wall, and at four past she said *I'm home* to the
whole house, loudly, as if the house were a big family and not an old woman in an
armchair and a voice in the middle of the room.

It had been like this for six weeks. Six weeks of work on Tessel and on two other
buildings in Corvel that Breno had found afterward, and her mother came back
every day with concrete dust in her short hair and something new in her face,
which Cecília took a while to recognize. It was tiredness. The good kind, of
someone who has spent the whole day straining to keep something standing. Her
mother in 2031 came home like that. Cecília had forgotten that she remembered.

"I'm home."

"There's soup," said Cecília.

"Who made it?"

"The house."

Her mother made a face that Cecília heard without seeing, and then appeared in
the living room doorway, barefoot, in socks, with the hard hat still under her
arm. She had bought the hard hat in the second week, with her first pay, which
landed on her wrist and not on Cecília's, and had shown it to her daughter as if
it were a jewel. It was white. It was like the one in the photograph.

"The house makes good soup," her mother admitted. "But don't tell it."

"It's listening."

"I know. I'm teasing it."

Her mother ate the soup at the table by the window, fast, hungry, telling her
about the day. A slab in a building on Aster Street whose steel was more corroded
than the mesh had read, because someone, in 2034, had let a leak run for years in
a corner. An argument with Breno about a shore. Noor, who now called her *Doc*,
with an irony that was turning into something else.

Cecília listened from the armchair, not paying attention to the words, paying
attention to the voice. The voice had changed since the first day. It was still
hoarse, it still went high when she laughed, but it had gained something, a
weight, as if the new throat were learning to be the old one.

"And you?" her mother asked. "What did you do?"

"Nothing."

"Nothing how?"

"Nothing. I stayed here. I listened to music."

It wasn't true, and it was. She had stayed there. She had listened to music. And,
in between, at ten past two in the afternoon, her heart had raced without
warning, and the air had gone, as if someone had shut a door inside her chest,
and the carer had called the clinic in private mode, and a doctor she didn't
know had appeared on the wall and talked to her calmly for eleven minutes, until
the door opened again. It was the fifth time that week.

"Nothing," Cecília repeated.

---

After the soup, her mother lay down on the floor.

She did that sometimes, since she'd arrived. She lay on her back on the living
room rug, with her arms spread, and looked at the ceiling. The first time,
Cecília had been frightened: she thought she had fallen. Then she understood.
Then she remembered.

"Play with me," her mother said, from the floor.

"I can't lie down there."

"You don't have to lie down. Play from there."

It was the joints game. Cecília hadn't thought of it in a century. At five, at
six, her mother would lie down on the living room floor of the apartment with the
kitchen window and call her, and she would lie down beside her, with her head on
her mother's arm, and the two of them would look at the ceiling. And her mother
would say: *now look for the joints*. And she would look. The thin lines between
the panels, where the ceiling was pieced together. And for every joint she found,
her mother told her something. What was on the other side. What it was holding.
Where the weight went. Why the house didn't fall on their heads.

*This one here is an expansion joint. It's where the ceiling breathes. In the heat
it grows a tiny bit, and in the cold it shrinks, and if it didn't have a joint it
would crack, like us when we hold back crying.*

Cecília had learned, at six, that houses breathed. She had believed it her whole
life, long after she knew it wasn't quite like that.

She looked at the ceiling of her own living room, from up in the armchair. It was
an old ceiling, made of panels, from when the building was put up, before the
city thought about every drop. It had joints. She had never counted them.

"That one," she said, pointing with her chin, because her hand shook. "By the
window."

"That one's easy. It's between two ceiling panels. It doesn't hold anything up,
it only hides what's above it." Her mother pointed at another one, from the
floor. "And that one over there, the crooked one, by the door to my room?"

Cecília looked. She found it. It was a darker line, thicker, that didn't follow
the pattern of the others.

"What's that one?"

"That one's a patch." Her mother smiled at the ceiling. "Someone broke something
up there, a long time ago, and fixed it, and couldn't make it the same. It's a
scar."

They stayed like that awhile. Her mother on the floor, arms spread, concrete
dust in her hair. Cecília in the armchair, hands in her lap. The two of them
looking for joints in an old ceiling, and her mother saying what each one held,
and Cecília listening as if she were six, and being a hundred and fifteen, and
knowing both things at once, the way you know both banks of a river.

It was the happiest moment she had had in many years. Maybe in forty. Maybe since
Tomás died; maybe since before. She knew it while it was happening, which was
rare, because happiness is almost always recognized only from behind, on its way
out.

And her knee hurt.

---

It wasn't the usual pain. The usual one was a dull pain, in the background, that
lived in both knees and in her hips and that she didn't even hear anymore, the
way you don't hear the refrigerator. This was different. Thin. Sharp. In the
left knee, inside, where bone meets bone. It had started late in the afternoon
and had been growing slowly, like a noise coming closer.

Cecília knew that pain. She had learned to know it in her late eighties, when her
knees started knowing things before she did.

"It's going to rain," she said.

Her mother laughed, on the floor.

"It's not."

"It is."

"Ciça, the rainy season ended six weeks ago. There hasn't been a drop since I
started on Tessel." Her mother touched two fingers to her wrist, the way she'd
learned, and looked at something in the air that only she could see. "The
forecast says clear skies through the end of the week. Zero percent."

"It's going to rain."

Her mother turned her head on the rug and looked at her daughter. And Cecília
saw, in her face, her mother understand. Not understand that it was going to
rain; understand what Cecília was doing.

"Where does it hurt?" her mother asked, quietly.

"In my knee. The left one."

Her mother kept looking at her. Then she looked at her own belly, under the pale
clothes, where there was nothing. She put her hand there, spread, the way Cecília
had seen her put it a hundred times in 2031, in the kitchen, before saying *get
the umbrella*. And she felt nothing, Cecília knew she didn't, because there was
nothing to feel.

"Now you're the magic," her mother said.

"I always thought it was you."

"I know." Her mother took her hand off her belly. "I never told you it was the
scar. I let you think I just knew."

"I found out. At twelve. My aunt told me."

"And were you angry?"

"I was. A whole year." Cecília smiled. "Then I thought it was even more magic. A
mother who knew about the rain from the belly where I had been."

Her mother didn't say anything. She turned her face back to the ceiling, and
Cecília saw, in the light from the window, that her eyes were wet, and that she
wasn't going to cry, because she was holding it in, like a ceiling with no joint.

---

At ten forty, the air went again.

Her mother had gone to take a shower. Cecília could hear the water running in the
bathroom, on the other side of the wall, and her mother singing under it, the
same slightly off-key song as always, and then the door inside her chest shut,
and she couldn't breathe.

She didn't make a sound. She had learned not to. She gripped the arms of the
armchair with both hands and opened her mouth and nothing came in, and her heart
beat fast, fast, like a trapped bird, and the carer spoke, very quietly, only in
her ear, in private mode:

*Cardiac event. Clinic notified. Breathe with me, Mrs. Cecília. One. Two.*

She breathed with the carer. Nothing came in. One. Two. A thread came in. Three.
The water ran in the bathroom. Her mother sang. Four. Another thread came in. The
unfamiliar doctor appeared in a corner of the wall, without sound, with a calm
face, making a gesture with his hands that meant *slowly*. Five. Six.

The door inside her chest opened.

Cecília sat there, sweating, her hands aching from gripping the armchair. The
doctor said something she didn't hear and vanished. The water stopped in the
bathroom.

On her wrist, a message lit up, small. It was from Irene. *Grandma, the clinic
let me know. Are you okay? Do you want me to come?*

Irene was the registered responsible party. Irene got everything. Irene had
known, for weeks, about every time the door shut, and had said nothing to her
great-grandmother, the thirty-eight-year-old woman who sang in the shower,
because Cecília had asked her not to, and because, perhaps, Irene didn't know
how to say it either.

Cecília touched her thumb to her wrist and answered: *I'm fine. Don't come.*

The bathroom door opened. Her mother came out with a towel on her head, in
pajamas, her face red from the heat of the water.

"You're sweaty," she said, stopping.

"It's stuffy."

"It is." Her mother went to the window, looked out. "Clear sky. Not a cloud." She
looked at her daughter, with half a smile. "Sorry, magic. Today you got it
wrong."

"Wait."

---

At eleven twenty, it rained.

Not the heavy rain of the season. A fine rain, quick, out of season, that nobody
had predicted, not the forecast on her mother's wrist, not the city, which had
its channels closed and the mouths of the cisterns capped for the dry season,
and which for a few minutes didn't know what to do with that water. The rain hit
the window. It ran down the glass on a diagonal.

Her mother was on the floor again, lying on the rug, half asleep, after the
shower. She opened her eyes at the noise. She sat up. She looked at the window.
She looked at her daughter.

She started to laugh.

She laughed the way Cecília hadn't seen her laugh since she'd come back. A whole
laugh, loud, that started high and came down, and didn't stop, and turned into a
cough, and went back to being a laugh. She laughed with her hands over her face.
She laughed lying back on the rug again, with her arms spread, looking at the
ceiling of joints, while the out-of-season rain beat on the glass.

"Magic," she kept saying, between one laugh and the next. "Magic. My daughter's
magic."

Cecília laughed too, from the armchair, an almost airless laugh, that hurt in
her chest where the door had shut two hours before. She didn't mind. She let it
hurt.

Her mother fell asleep on the floor. Right there, on the rug, in the middle of
the laughing, the way children fall asleep in the middle of a party, all at
once. She lay on her side, curled up, with the towel still wrapped around her
hair, and her breathing went slow and deep.

Cecília sat looking at her.

She wanted to cover her. There was a throw on the arm of the armchair, her throw,
and her mother was on the floor, with nothing, and the night was going to cool
down after the rain. Cecília wanted to get up, and bend her knees, and lower
herself, and put the throw over her mother's shoulders, and tuck the end over
her feet, the way her mother had done for her a thousand times, without anyone
asking.

She couldn't. Her knees didn't bend down to the floor anymore. Her hands couldn't
hold the weight of the throw spread out. If she tried, she would fall, and her
mother would wake up, and would know.

"House," Cecília said, very quietly.

"Yes, Mrs. Cecília."

"The throw. Put it over her. Slowly. Over her feet too."

The arm came down from the ceiling without a sound. It took the throw from the
arm of the armchair, carefully, and opened it in the air, and lowered it over her
sleeping mother, over her shoulders, over her curled back, and then, with the
tips of its bone fingers, settled the edge over her bare feet. And withdrew.

Her mother didn't wake up. She sighed, and burrowed into the throw, and went on
sleeping.

Cecília sat looking.

It was the first time, in a hundred and nine years, that she had covered her
mother. She had done it with a bone arm that came down from the ceiling, because
she didn't have another. And she stayed there, in the armchair, until the small
hours, with the out-of-season rain stopping outside, and the pain in her knee
going away with it, and the door inside her chest quiet for now, watching her
mother sleep.

"How many times this week?" she asked, around three, very quietly, of the carer.

"Cardiac events? Six, Mrs. Cecília. The week before, four."

Cecília nodded in the dark.

Six.

She didn't say anything. Her mother slept on the floor, under the throw, with a
smile still at the corner of her mouth, the wide mouth from her grandmother's
side of the family. She was thirty-eight. She had a new white hard hat, and a
job, and a ceiling full of joints to count, and a magic daughter. She was happy.

She didn't know a thing.
