Her mother was cooking.

Cecília could hear it from the armchair. She couldn't see, because the kitchen
was behind the wall, but she could hear, and she had been hearing it for a
month, and she still wasn't used to it. The sound of a knife on a board. A pan
being put on the heat. The water starting to boil, that hiss that rises and
stops and rises. Her mother talking to herself, quietly, to things: *where have
you gotten to*, to a spoon, *ah, there*.

The house kitchen could do everything. It could make a whole lunch without anyone
touching a thing, done just right, at the right time, with the salt the doctor
had prescribed. Cecília had used the kitchen that way for sixty years. But she
had kept, out of stubbornness or out of not bothering to have it removed, a
manual hotplate in the corner of the counter, and a drawer of metal pans that
nobody used, and a wooden board, and three knives.

On the third day, her mother had found all of it, and had looked at her daughter
with a face Cecília hadn't seen since she was six: the face of someone who has
found treasure at the back of a cupboard.

Since then, her mother cooked. Badly, sometimes. The hotplate was a model she
didn't know, and it heated faster than she expected, and she burned the garlic
almost every day and swore at the garlic as if it were the garlic's fault. The
carer had offered to help eleven times. Her mother had thanked it eleven times,
with a politeness that got drier each time, and refused.

Today she was making the pasta.

Cecília knew from the smell, before her mother said. Tomato, olive oil, onion,
and then, at the end, the smell of the egg in the frying pan, the edge going
crisp. It was her dish at six. Pasta with tomato sauce and a fried egg on top,
the yolk runny, to burst it and mix it in. She hadn't eaten it in a hundred and
nine years. Not for lack of chances; for lack of nerve.

"What time is Irene coming?" her mother asked, from the kitchen, loudly.

"Noon."

"Does she like eggs?"

Cecília didn't know. She thought about it with a kind of start: she didn't know
whether her granddaughter liked eggs. She knew everything about Irene, the dates
of the two divorces, the doctor's name, the blood pressure medication, the fear
of heights she hid from everybody, and she didn't know whether she liked eggs.
Maybe nobody in that family had ever cooked an egg for anybody.

"Make it," she said. "If she doesn't eat it, I will."

---

The first week, Cecília had woken at three in the morning to voices in the
living room.

It wasn't a dream. It was her mother, very quietly, and the carer, in its usual
neutral tone, which in the small hours went lower, as if the house also knew it
was the middle of the night. Cecília lay in the dark of her own room, with the
door ajar, listening.

"How did she sleep last night?" her mother was asking.

"Mrs. Cecília slept four hours and ten minutes, in three periods. The longest
was one hour and fifty minutes."

"Is that normal?"

"For her age and her condition, it's within the expected range."

"What condition?"

There was a pause. Cecília held her breath.

"I'm not authorized to discuss Mrs. Cecília's medical condition with third
parties," said the carer.

"I'm her mother."

"You are registered as Mrs. Cecília's dependent. Not as a responsible party."

Another silence. Cecília pictured her mother in the dark living room, barefoot,
standing in the middle of the rug, looking at the neutral point where there was
nothing, with the face of someone who has just been slapped by a wall.

"Okay," her mother said at last. "Then teach me what you can teach me. What
medication she takes. What time. What she eats. What she likes."

"What she likes is not in my records."

"Then tell me the rest. I'll find out what she likes."

And the carer told her. Cecília lay there listening to the whole list of her own
life in a neutral voice, at three in the morning: the medications, the times,
the doses, the salt, the water, the mesh she didn't wear, the bath she took
sitting down. Her mother repeated each item quietly, like someone memorizing,
and sometimes asked again. *Twice a day or three times? Before or after eating?*
Cecília knew that voice. It was her mother's voice learning the times tables
with her, at the kitchen table, by the window over the sink.

She didn't sleep any more that night.

In the morning, before her mother woke up, she spoke to the carer.

"One thing. The bad nights."

"Yes, Mrs. Cecília."

"The times my heart races. When I can't breathe lying down. When you call the
clinic."

"Yes."

"Don't tell her."

The carer waited, with the silence of instructions that didn't fit in any
drawer.

"Do you want to activate private mode for cardiac events?"

"I do."

"Recorded. Mrs. Cecília's cardiac and respiratory events will be reported only
to the clinic and to the registered responsible party, Irene."

Cecília nodded, alone, in the armchair. She didn't feel good. She felt she had
closed a door inside her own house, and that she was going to spend the next
months with her hand on the knob.

---

Irene arrived at ten past twelve, and stood in the doorway the way her mother
had stood in the doorway of the clinic room: without quite coming in.

She was well dressed, which in Irene was a sign of nerves. She had done her
hair. She was carrying a plant in a small pot, something green and fleshy that
Cecília didn't recognize, and she held the pot with both hands, in front of her
body, like a shield.

Her mother came out of the kitchen drying her hands on a dish towel. She was
barefoot, in the pale clinic clothes and an apron she'd found who knows where,
and she had a smear of sauce on her cheek, and she was thirty-eight.

The two of them looked at each other.

Cecília, from the armchair, watched them look at each other and felt something
she couldn't name and that perhaps had no name, because nobody before her had
been in this position: sitting, old, watching her thirty-eight-year-old mother
and her sixty-eight-year-old granddaughter meet for the first time in the
doorway of her living room. Irene had white hair, slightly drooping shoulders,
Lívia's creased forehead. Her mother had short dark hair, soft as a child's, and
smooth arms, and the smear of sauce. One looked like the other's grandmother. The
one who looked like the grandmother was the great-granddaughter.

"Hi," said her mother.

"Hi," said Irene.

Neither of them knew what came next. Cecília saw that and waited, and didn't
help, because she didn't know how.

It was her mother who moved. She took two steps, stopped in front of Irene,
looked at the plant.

"What's that?"

"A succulent. It hardly ever needs water." Irene held out the pot, stiffly, her
arm straight. "For you. For your room."

Her mother took the pot. She stood looking at the plant, and then at Irene, and
then, suddenly, she laughed.

"You've got my mouth," she said.

Irene didn't understand. Cecília understood. She looked at her granddaughter and
saw, as if she'd never seen it, the wide mouth, a little big for the face, which
Irene had inherited from Lívia, which Lívia had inherited from nobody knew whom,
because Cecília had her father's narrow mouth. Nobody knew whom. Now they knew.

"The mouth from my mother's side," her mother said, almost to herself. "It
skipped Ciça. It came back in you."

"Ciça?"

Irene said the word as if it were from another language. She looked at her
grandmother.

Cecília felt her face go hot, at a hundred and fifteen, like a girl caught out.
Sixty-eight years of being her granddaughter and Irene had never heard that name.
Nor Lívia. Nor Tomás. Nobody.

"It's what she called me," she said.

"What I call her," said her mother, without looking at her, still looking at
Irene, in a tone so natural, so unceremonious, that Cecília had to squeeze her
hands together in her lap.

Irene was silent. Cecília saw something cross her face, quickly, something she
knew well, because she'd seen it on Lívia's face all her life: jealousy. Not of a
person. Of a place. There was a place in her grandmother that Irene had never
known existed, and now someone was living in it.

"Lunch is ready," said her mother. "Do you like eggs?"

"I do," said Irene, who didn't.

---

They ate at the table by the window, the three of them, with the city bright
outside, before the rain.

Her mother served her daughter first. She put the plate in front of her, pasta,
sauce, the egg on top with the yolk whole, yellow, trembling, and stood beside
the chair, waiting, with the dish towel over her shoulder. Cecília picked up
the fork.

Her hand shook. It always shook, but today it shook more, because she was looking
at that egg, and because her mother was standing beside her, and because Irene
was watching too. She pushed the fork into the yolk. The yolk burst and ran into
the sauce, and she mixed it, slowly, the way she used to mix it at six, and
lifted the fork to her mouth.

She couldn't taste it properly. Her mouth didn't taste things the way it used
to; everything arrived muffled, like music heard from another room. But she felt
the texture of the soft yolk on the pasta, and the heat, and something, far down
in the muffle, that was the tomato, and that was the kitchen in 2031, the window
over the sink, her mother in an apron.

She swallowed. She put the fork on the plate.

"It's good," she said.

Her voice didn't come out right. Her mother didn't say anything. She took the
dish towel off her shoulder and wiped her daughter's chin, where a little yolk
had stayed, with a quick, absent gesture, without asking, the way you wipe a
child's chin in the middle of a conversation.

Cecília closed her eyes.

That was it. That was exactly it. A dish towel on the chin, without anyone
asking. She had brought her mother back for that, and that was happening, and it
was as good as she had imagined on the nights in the armchair, and it was also
something else, which she hadn't imagined: it was unbearable. Because her mother
didn't know. Her mother had wiped her chin out of love, and Cecília had received
the gesture knowing it was what she had bought.

She opened her eyes. Irene was looking at her from across the table.

---

It was after lunch that Irene said it.

Her mother was in the kitchen washing the dishes by hand, another bit of
stubbornness, and singing quietly a song Cecília hadn't heard since she was a
child and which must have been famous once. Irene had pushed the whole egg to
the side of her plate, untouched, and her mother had seen and hadn't said
anything, and Cecília liked her for that.

Irene spoke quietly, so her mother wouldn't hear.

"She doesn't know, does she?"

"Doesn't know what?"

"How much it cost."

Cecília looked at her granddaughter. Irene had her hands in her lap, squeezing
the napkin, and Lívia's creased forehead, and her great-grandmother's wide mouth.

"It's none of her business," said Cecília.

"It's mine."

"I know it is."

"The apartment downstairs. The one in Harlo, that was Mom's. The whole fund,
Grandma. The fund Grandpa left." Irene said it without anger, tired, like someone
reciting a list she has recited many times on her own. "I'm not asking for it
back. I know there's no getting it back. I just want to understand."

"I know."

"Then explain it to me."

The singing in the kitchen stopped. The tap stopped. Her mother appeared in the
doorway, with the dish towel in her hand, and she had heard. Cecília knew she
had heard from the way she was standing.

"How much did it cost?" her mother asked.

Irene looked at Cecília. Cecília didn't say anything. Irene looked at her mother.

"Everything," said Irene.

Her mother came into the room. She sat down in the chair she had eaten in,
facing Irene, and put the dish towel on the table, folded, carefully.

"I'll work," she said. "I'm an engineer. I can pay."

"It's not the money."

"Then what is it?"

Irene took a while. Cecília watched her granddaughter decide, and knew what she
was going to say before she said it, and did nothing to stop her, and later she
would wonder why, and would decide it was because part of her wanted Irene to
say it. Wanted someone to say it. Wanted it not to be her.

"Do you know why she brought you back?" said Irene.

Her mother looked at her without understanding. It wasn't an act; Cecília knew
her mother's face when she was putting on an act, she'd been six but she knew,
and this wasn't that. It was the face of someone hearing a question in a
language she doesn't speak.

"Because I'm her mother," her mother said.

She said it simply. She said it the way you'd say that rain falls from top to
bottom. And she looked at Cecília, across the table, waiting for her daughter to
confirm the obvious.

Cecília held her mother's gaze. Then Irene's. Irene was waiting too, and there
was something on her granddaughter's face that Cecília had never seen there and
that was something like pity—for whom, she didn't know.

"Yes," said Cecília. "Because she's my mother."

It wasn't a lie. It was only the half of the truth that fit on the table.

Her mother smiled, relieved, and picked up the dish towel, and went back to the
dishes. The tap ran. The singing started again, quiet, a little off-key.

Irene sat looking at her grandmother for a long time. Then she got up, picked up
her bag, kissed Cecília on the forehead—a quick, dry kiss, like someone doing a
duty—and went to the door. She stopped at the door.

"Thanks for lunch," she said, loudly, toward the kitchen.

"Come back next week," her mother said, from in there. "I'll make something
else. No egg."

Irene laughed. It was a short laugh, surprised, like the one on the call that
afternoon, and she covered her mouth with her hand, the wide mouth, and went out.

Cecília was left alone in the living room, listening to her mother wash the
dishes and sing. The rain began. The succulent was on the table, in its small
pot, green and fleshy, a plant that hardly ever needed water, and Cecília sat
looking at it and thinking that Irene had chosen it carefully, for someone who
was going to stay a short time, or for someone who was going to stay a long
time, and that maybe not even Irene knew which of the two she'd meant.
