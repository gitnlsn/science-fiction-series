Cecília got the news in the afternoon, sitting down, because she no longer got
anything standing up.

She was in the armchair by the window, where she spent most afternoons now that
her legs had started asking permission for everything. The armchair had been
hers for forty years and did nothing: it didn't warm up, didn't massage, didn't
rise or sink, didn't know her name. It was the only thing in the house that
didn't. Irene had tried to replace it twice, and both times Cecília had waited
for her granddaughter to leave and had it sent back.

Her hands were in her lap. She was looking at them, as she did a lot now,
without sadness, the way you look at a map of a place you used to live.

They were hands of a hundred and fifteen, and they had everything. The skin thin
as tissue paper over the veins, which were blue and raised and moved when she
moved her fingers. The spots, dozens, of every size, which the doctor had
offered to erase in a single afternoon and which she had refused, because each
one was a summer. The wedding ring, too big now, held on the finger by the
finger's own knuckle, which wouldn't let it off anymore. A half-moon scar at the
base of her right thumb, from a can of peaches in syrup, in a kitchen that no
longer existed, in a year that only she remembered. A nail that had grown back
crooked after a car door, back when there were still car doors that closed on
people's fingers.

Hands that had done everything you can do with hands in a whole life, and that
now shook a little when they held a glass.

"Cecília," said a voice close to her left ear.

It wasn't the carer. The carer spoke in a different way, from a neutral point in
the middle of the room, and always began with *Mrs. Cecília*. This voice was a
man's, and low, and she recognized it before she recognized the words. She had
heard that voice once a week for two years, and then once a day for seven
weeks, and for the last three days she had stopped being able to hear anything
else, waiting for it.

"Go ahead, Ravi."

"She stayed."

Cecília closed her eyes.

"This morning," he went on. "It's the first conversation that's stuck. She
looked at the ceiling first, as always. She got out of bed on her own and took
three steps. She asked the year."

"And you told her."

"I told her. She asked who had asked for it. I said it was her daughter."

She waited. Ravi knew his job; he knew the next sentence was the one that
mattered and that he had to say all of it.

"She didn't believe it," he said. "She thought it was a great-granddaughter. So
I told her your age."

"And she?"

There was a pause. Cecília pictured his face, which she had seen in person only
a few times, the face of someone who had given that news to a lot of people and
still hadn't learned to give it without paying something for it.

"She looked at her hands," Ravi said.

---

After he had gone out of the voice, the room went back to the size it was.

The carer waited the length of time it had been programmed to wait and then
asked, from its neutral point in the middle of the room, if Mrs. Cecília wanted
anything. A tea. The lights lower. The window clearer.

"The window."

The whole wall, from floor to ceiling, cleared slowly, and the city came in.

It was raining. It rained the way it rained now, almost every afternoon that
season: not in drops but in weight, a mass of water falling straight down onto
the green roofs, onto the covered walkways that joined one building to the next
at the height of the tenth floor, onto the channels as wide as rivers that ran
down the façades and carried the water to the cisterns under the street. A
transit passed at the height of the window, silent, its inside lights on, full
of people going home, and disappeared behind the block's water tower. Down
below, in the street, nobody. Nobody walked in the street when it rained like
this. That was what the walkways were for.

Cecília had watched the city turn into this. Not all at once: one roof at a
time, one cistern at a time, over ninety years of rains that got heavier and
heavier and summers that got longer and longer. She had watched them tear down
the buildings that couldn't take the water and put up these, which could. She
didn't think it was beautiful or ugly. It was the city. It was where she lived.

Her mother would think something else. Her mother would look at the walkways and
ask how many kilos per square meter they could take.

Cecília smiled to herself, and the smile hurt a little, at the corner of her
mouth, where everything hurt a little now.

---

On the little table beside the armchair there was a photograph.

It was paper. All that Cecília had of her mother, in the way of images, was
that: a rectangle of photographic paper ten by fifteen centimeters, its edges
worn from being picked up. The files from 2031 had been lost a long time ago,
not in any disaster, but through the most ordinary thing in the world: the
formats changed, changed again, changed once more, and at some point in the
2060s the photos and videos of her childhood were files that wouldn't open in
anything. She had paid, once, for someone to try to recover them. The man
returned three blurred images and a bill.

The paper photograph had survived because her aunt had printed it for the
funeral, and then kept it in a drawer, and then given it to her, at sixteen,
saying *I think this is more yours than mine*.

In the photograph, her mother was on a building site, in a white hard hat and
an orange vest, laughing at something outside the frame. She was in her early
thirties. She had her hand raised, shading her eyes from the sun, and on the
side of her index finger, if you knew where to look, you could see a pale line.
Cecília knew where to look. She had looked at that line for a hundred and nine
years.

She picked up the photograph with both hands, because with one it shook.

The woman who had woken up this morning had that face. Ravi had shown her, weeks
before, the image of the finished body, and Cecília had looked and had needed to
ask him to turn it off. It wasn't a resemblance: it was that. It was the mother
in the photograph, without the hard hat, without the sun in her eyes, without
the line on her finger. A thirty-eight-year-old woman who was going to look at
her, at Cecília, at that face of a hundred and fifteen, and have to search for
her daughter inside it.

She would be searching for a six-year-old girl in Velcro shoes.

And she would find this.

---

The doctor had told her on a Tuesday too. A little over two years ago.

He was a man of Irene's age, kind and direct, who had looked after her for
almost thirty years and knew she preferred things said. He sat down in front of
the armchair, which was a place where doctors almost never sat, and didn't use
any of the technical names.

"It's begun, Mrs. Cecília."

She knew what had begun. Everyone past a hundred knew. Maintenance stretched
life—it repaired the cells, replaced what wore out, kept the heart beating and
the head clear far beyond where bodies had been made to go—but it didn't
stretch it forever. There was a point where things stopped responding, not one
at a time, but together, like a house where every wall gives way in the same
winter. There was no disease with a name. There was the end, and the end, when
it came for someone who had lived so long, came quickly.

"How long?"

"Two years. Maybe three. With your head clear until near the end, from what we
see in the other cases." He paused. "The body goes first."

"The body's already gone, Doctor."

He didn't laugh, and she liked him for that.

That night, Cecília didn't sleep. She stayed in the armchair, with the window
dark, and thought about what she wanted. She was a hundred and thirteen, and it
was the first time in a long while that anyone had given her a deadline, and
the deadline did something strange to her: it blew the dust of habit off
everything, and things showed up as they were.

She didn't want to travel. She had traveled. She didn't want to see anyone in
particular: nearly everyone she wanted to see had died, her husband forty years
ago, her daughter nine, and her friends one by one, and she had gone to every
funeral, until the day she stopped being able to go. She didn't want to leave
anything in writing. She had nothing to say to the world that the world hadn't
already heard from more eloquent people.

What she wanted was small, and that was what made it so hard to say out loud.

She wanted someone to put a hand on her forehead to see if she had a fever. Not
the carer, which took her temperature a hundred times a day without touching
anything, and knew the exact number, and didn't want to know anything beyond
the number. She wanted a hand. The hand of someone who put a hand on her
forehead because they were afraid. She wanted someone to tuck the blanket around
her feet without her asking. She wanted to hear someone say *sleep, I'm here*,
and sleep.

She wanted to be somebody's daughter, once more, before she died.

She had spent a hundred and nine years being, for everyone, the person who looks
after. She looked after her aunt at the end. She looked after her husband at the
end. She looked after her daughter—her daughter, who shouldn't have gone before
her, and did, and Cecília looked after her too, sitting beside a bed where she
herself should have been lying. She looked after Irene as a child, Irene as a
teenager, Irene divorced, Irene afraid. Everyone who had needed looking after,
she had looked after.

Nobody had looked after her since she was six.

And there was one person, only one, in the whole world, for whom she would
always be the littlest. One person for whom she had never grown up, because that
person hadn't been there to watch her grow. That person was in a vault in the
basement of a clinic, in a container with her name written on it, waiting for
more than a century for someone to have the money and the reason.

Cecília had the money. It was everything the family had, but she had it.

And she had the reason.

In the morning, she called the clinic.

---

Irene appeared in the window at five.

She appeared the way people appeared now, without warning and life-size: a
stretch of the wall, to the left of the rain, went dark, and then cleared in a
different light, and there was her granddaughter, sitting in her own kitchen, on
the other side of the city, with a cup in her hand and her white hair pinned up
any old way. She was sixty-eight. She looked more tired than Cecília did, which
wasn't fair and was true.

"Ravi let me know," Irene said. "He said she stayed."

"She stayed."

"And then?"

Cecília didn't answer right away. Irene had always asked that, ever since she
was little—*and then?*—at the end of every story, as if no story ever really
ended, and Cecília, back then, had found it funny.

"Then she stays there a few more weeks, with the vaccines. After that she comes
here."

"Here." Irene set the cup down on the table, out of frame, and the sound of the
china arrived half a second after the image. "Grandma. You can barely get out of
that chair."

"That's why."

Irene looked at her with her forehead creased, the same creased forehead as her
mother's, which Cecília had watched crease for eighty years until it stopped.
She waited for her grandmother to explain. Cecília didn't explain. She picked up
the glass of water from the little table, with both hands, and drank a
mouthful, and put it back.

"I don't understand what you want out of this," Irene said at last, quietly. "I
swear I try."

"I know you try."

"She's going to get there expecting a little girl."

"I know."

"And she's going to find you. And you're going to have to..." Irene made a vague
gesture with her hand that took in the room, the armchair, her grandmother's
body, the hundred and fifteen years. "Explain all this to her. Everything that
happened. Do you have the strength for that?"

Cecília thought about the question. It was a good question, and Irene didn't
usually ask good questions when she was afraid.

"I'm not going to explain anything," she said. "She'll see."

Irene was quiet. Through her window, behind her, it was raining too, and Cecília
thought it strange that the same rain should fall on both windows, as if that
proved something about them.

"Do you want me to come over today?"

"Not today."

"Tomorrow?"

"Let a few days go by."

Irene nodded. She was going to hang up. She didn't. She picked up the cup again,
out of frame, and held it without drinking, the same way her grandmother held
the glass, and Cecília saw and said nothing.

"Grandma," Irene said. "What's she like?"

It was the first time her granddaughter had asked about her mother as if she
were a person and not an expense. Cecília looked at the photograph on the little
table.

"She looks up when she walks into a place," she said. "To see if the ceiling's
going to fall in."

Irene gave a short laugh, surprised, and covered her mouth, as if she'd laughed
at a funeral. Then the wall went dark, and went back to being a window, and the
rain went back to being the same rain as before, just one.

---

The carer brought the tea without her asking, because it was that time, and the
tea was at the exact temperature, and Cecília held it with both hands and didn't
drink.

She hadn't told Irene why. Irene knew how much—there was no hiding that, the
money was everyone's and was going to stop being—and she had shouted, in the
kitchen, something Cecília wasn't going to forget: *you're spending my
inheritance to buy yourself a doll*. Afterward she apologized. But she didn't ask
why. Maybe she was afraid of the answer. Maybe she thought it was vanity, or an
old woman's madness, or one of those things the very old do with money that the
relatives learn not to argue about.

She hadn't told Ravi, who hadn't asked either, because his protocol didn't ask.
And she wasn't going to tell her mother.

She had thought about it a great deal, in the armchair at night. If she said to
her mother *I brought you back to look after me until I die*, her mother would
look after her. Cecília knew that with the same certainty she knew her own name.
Her mother would look after her out of obligation, out of guilt, out of
debt—because she had gone away when Cecília was six, and because she was the
kind of person who paid her debts to the end. And Cecília didn't want a debt
being paid. She wanted the hand on the forehead. The hand on the forehead only
counts when nobody asked.

So she wasn't going to say. She would let her mother arrive, and look at her,
and decide on her own what to do with a daughter of a hundred and fifteen. And
if her mother decided to stay, it would be because she wanted to.

She knew it was a way of lying. She had lived long enough to recognize the lies
people tell out of love and not to like them any better for it.

"Mrs. Cecília," said the carer, "your heart rate is elevated. Would you like me
to lower the light?"

"No."

"Would you like to talk?"

It was one of the things the carer knew how to do. It talked well, in a calm
voice with an educated vocabulary, and remembered everything Cecília had ever
said, and asked good questions. Irene had paid for the better version.

Cecília looked at the neutral point in the middle of the room, where there was
nothing.

"I want you to call me something else."

"Of course. What would you like to be called?"

She took a while. The rain beat on the window with its usual weight, and the
transit passed again, going the other way, nearly empty now.

"Ciça."

Nobody had called her that since 2031. Her aunt tried, once, the week of the
funeral, and Cecília, at six, did something she had never done to anyone: she
screamed. Her aunt never tried again. Her husband didn't know about the
nickname. Her daughter didn't know. Irene didn't know. For a hundred and nine
years, that name had existed only in Cecília's head, kept the way you keep
something that belongs to someone else and that the someone else is coming back
for.

"Ciça," the carer repeated, in its calm voice, with perfect pronunciation.
"Would you like me to turn off the lights, Ciça?"

Cecília felt the name hit her and go past, the way rain goes past a closed
window. It was right, the two syllables in place, the tone gentle. And it was
nothing.

"No," she said. "I'm sorry. Forget that name."

"May I ask why?"

Cecília put the photograph back on the little table, with her mother's face
turned toward her, laughing in the sun, with her hand over her eyes.

"Because that name is hers," she said. "She's coming to get it."
