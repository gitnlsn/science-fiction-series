Her mother knew.

Cecília didn't know how she knew, or since when. She knew it the way she had
known, in her late eighties, that it was going to rain: by a thin pain in a place
where there was nothing.

It wasn't anything her mother said. Her mother didn't say anything. She came home
from Tessel earlier, at five, at four, and no longer talked about her day, the
slabs, Noor, Breno on his crate. She cooked in silence. She didn't swear at the
garlic anymore. She sat on the rug, by the armchair, and didn't lie down to look
at the ceiling; she sat, with her back against her daughter's legs, and sometimes
stayed like that a whole hour without speaking, watching the rain, which fell
every afternoon now, in weight, because the season had begun.

And at night the hand came.

It started on a night of rain, not long after her mother came home soaked from a
Sunday in Harlo and said she'd decided to walk. Cecília was in the armchair, half
asleep, and felt a hand on her forehead. Warm, new-skinned, with calluses starting
at the base of the fingers. The hand stayed there awhile, without moving, and
then went away.

After that, every night. Sometimes twice. Her mother passed through the living
room, on the way to her room, and put her hand on her daughter's forehead, and
stayed a little, and went.

It was what Cecília had wanted. It was exactly that, the hand on the forehead,
without anyone asking. And Cecília received that hand every night with her eyes
closed, pretending to sleep, and knew, from the hand, that her mother knew.
Because the hand had changed. It wasn't the absent hand of the dish towel on the
chin anymore. It was a hand that weighed. That stayed too long. That shook a
little, faintly, at the fingertips, the way hers did.

A hand that was asking.

Cecília didn't answer. She waited for the hand to go away, and then opened her
eyes in the dark, and sat watching the rain, and thought: Irene.

---

That night, her mother was late coming home from Tessel, and Cecília was alone in
the armchair, and the rain was falling, and she remembered.

She didn't want to remember. She remembered the way she remembered now, all at
once, with a sharpness that was frightening, as if the hundred and nine years in
between were a thin curtain someone was drawing back by hand.

It was a Thursday.

She woke up early, before her mother, because she was six and woke up early, and
went to her mother's room barefoot, and her mother was awake. She was lying in
bed, on her side, with the yellow headscarf with tiny flowers on her head, and
she smiled when she saw her daughter in the doorway. It was a tired smile. Her
mother had smiled tired for many months.

*Come here. Come and I'll do your braids.*

She climbed onto the bed and sat with her back to her mother, and her mother did
the two braids lying down, with hands that shook, and the braids came out loose,
and crooked, and one higher than the other. Ciça ran her hand over them and felt
they were wrong, and didn't say anything, because she was six but she already
knew there were things that weren't said in that house.

Aunt Marta arrived at eight. She was her mother's sister, older, tall, with a deep
voice that Ciça thought sounded like a man's, and she came without the boys,
which was strange, because Aunt Marta never came without the boys. She came in
and went straight to the kitchen and stood there with her back turned, at the
window over the sink, making coffee, and Ciça saw, from the doorway, that she was
crying and making coffee at the same time.

The team arrived at ten.

Three people. Two men and a woman, in pale blue, with the silver cases. The cases
were big, bigger than Ciça, and they shone, and they leaned them against the
hallway wall, side by side, carefully, as if they were made of glass. One of the
men tripped over her shoes, which were by the door, and said *sorry* to the
shoes, and kept going. One of the shoes was left on its side.

The doctor came after. The badly shaven one. He was young and had a gentle voice,
and he crouched in front of her, in the hallway, until he was at the height of
her eyes, and said:

*Hi, Cecília. Your mom is going to sleep today. A very long sleep. You know that,
right?*

She knew. Her mother had told her, many times, in the months before, in words
Ciça understood and didn't understand. That her mother was sick and the sickness
wasn't going to get better. That a day would come when her mother would go to
sleep and not wake up. That it was going to hurt Ciça a lot, and that it was all
right for it to hurt.

And that maybe, one day, very far away, she would wake up.

Ciça had asked when. Her mother had said she didn't know. That maybe never. That
it was like planting the seed of a tree you don't know will grow.

*I know*, Ciça said to the doctor. And put one foot on top of the other.

---

Her mother called her into the bedroom at half past ten.

Alone. Aunt Marta stayed in the hallway, with the cases. The doctor stayed in the
living room. Ciça went into the bedroom and her mother was sitting up in bed,
propped on the pillows, with the yellow headscarf and white pajamas, and she had
tidied the room, Ciça realized that later, many years later: she had tidied the
room for that hour. The curtain open. The light coming in. A glass of water on
the nightstand, full.

*Sit here.*

Ciça sat on the edge of the bed, with her feet swinging, because the bed was
high, and her mother took her hand.

Her mother said a lot of things. Ciça remembered almost none of them. She
remembered the tone: low, slow, the way her mother talked when she told a story
before bed. She remembered that her mother said her name many times, *Ciça,
Ciça*, as if she wanted to use up the name before she went. She remembered that
her mother told her to do what Aunt Marta said, and eat her vegetables, and not
be afraid of the rain.

And she remembered the last thing. That one she remembered whole. Every word in
place, with the exact sound, with the smell of the room, with the light on the
curtain. She had kept that sentence for a hundred and nine years the way you keep
the key to a house that doesn't exist anymore.

She had asked. That was what she never told anyone: that it had been her who
asked. Sitting on the edge of the bed, with her feet swinging, she had looked at
her mother and asked, at six, the only thing she wanted to know.

*Mom. Will you come back?*

And her mother had squeezed her hand, and smiled the tired smile, and said:

*I'll come back when you need me.*

---

Then her mother said *now go with your aunt*, and Ciça went.

She didn't want to go. She remembered not wanting to. She remembered standing in
the bedroom doorway, with one foot on top of the other, and her mother saying,
from the bed, *both feet on the floor, sweetheart, or you'll fall*, and putting
them down, and going out.

Aunt Marta took her by the hand in the hallway and led her out to the street. She
said they were going to get ice cream. It was morning, and nobody has ice cream in
the morning, and Ciça knew the ice cream was a lie, and went anyway, because her
aunt's hand was squeezing very hard.

They had ice cream at an ice cream shop on the corner, which had a long counter and
a round clock on the wall, the kind with hands. Ciça asked for chocolate chip. Her
aunt didn't ask for anything. She sat across from her and kept looking at the
clock.

Ciça ate the whole ice cream.

She didn't know, at the time. She found out later, much later, in her early
twenties, when she asked Aunt Marta, old by then, to tell her the exact time. Her
aunt told her. The doctor had told her aunt it would be at eleven. Her aunt had
watched the clock in the ice cream shop from eleven until fourteen minutes past,
without blinking, while her six-year-old niece ate chocolate chip ice cream in
front of her, with her crooked braids.

The fourteen minutes had passed there. In a chocolate chip ice cream. Ciça hadn't
felt a thing. *It's the ones who stay who feel the fourteen minutes*, the doctor
had told her mother. And her mother had told her aunt. And her aunt, years later,
told Ciça. And Ciça thought, when she heard it: *but I stayed. And I didn't feel
it.*

It was her aunt who felt it. Her aunt watching the clock. That was why she didn't
order any ice cream.

When they got back, the silver cases weren't in the hallway anymore. The door to
her mother's room was closed. The shoe was still on its side, by the door, and
nobody set it right.

---

The root—she called it that, inside, *the root*, as if it were something growing
under her ground that nobody saw—came later. At sixteen.

The same year as the photograph. Aunt Marta gave her the photograph on her
birthday, saying *I think this is more yours than mine*, and that night, after the
cake, in the kitchen, her aunt drank a little more wine than usual, and told her.

She told her that her mother could have lived longer.

Not much. A few months. Four, five, maybe six. The treatment was still holding.
But the disease was moving to her head. The doctors had seen it in the scans. In a
few weeks, it would get there. And when it did, her mother would stop being her
mother, little by little, and would forget things, and then people, and then her.

And preservation was only any use for a sound mind. That was the rule. What was in
there was what would be kept. If the disease got there first, there would be
nothing to keep.

So her mother chose. She chose to stop treatment early. She chose the day. A
Thursday, with her head still whole, with the braids still in her hands, though
her hands shook. She traded the months she had for something that might never
happen. The seed of a tree you don't know will grow.

*She chose to go so she could come back*, Aunt Marta said, in the kitchen, with
her glass of wine. *That's what she told me. It was the only thing she ever said
to me that I couldn't forgive.*

Ciça was sixteen and didn't say anything. She got up from the table and went to
her room and spent the whole night looking at the photograph of her mother on the
building site, laughing in the sun, with her hand over her eyes.

Her mother had chosen. She could have stayed four more months, five, six. More
braids. More nights with the hand on the forehead. One more birthday. And she had
chosen not to stay, so as to keep her head in a vault, so as to maybe come back
one day, very far away, when Ciça was old, or dead, or never.

She chose to go.

And she had said: *I'll come back when you need me.*

Cecília spent a hundred and nine years with both things inside her. The anger of
one and the promise of the other. One didn't erase the other. The two grew
together, like two roots of the same plant, braided, one higher than the other,
crooked.

At a hundred and thirteen, Dr. Sabino said *it's begun*. And Cecília sat up the
whole night in the armchair. And in the morning she called the clinic.

Because she needed her.

Because her mother had said *when you need me*, and she needed her, and her
mother had chosen to go so she could come back, so let her come back. Let her come
back now. Let her keep her word. It wasn't only missing her. It wasn't only the
hand on the forehead. It was this too: a six-year-old girl in an ice cream shop,
a hundred and nine years later, calling in a debt her mother had made sitting on
a high bed, with her daughter's feet swinging.

She had never said this to anyone. Not even to herself, in those words. She said
it now, in the armchair, with the rain beating on the glass, in a low voice, to
the empty room.

"I called you, Mom. You said you'd come. I called you."

---

Her mother came home at half past nine.

Cecília heard the door, and the boots in the hallway, one at a time, and then
nothing. She didn't hear *I'm home*. Her mother hadn't said *I'm home* for three
weeks.

She closed her eyes. She pretended to sleep.

She heard her mother come into the living room. Stop. Stand still for a while, in
the middle of the rug, looking at her. Cecília felt the look, the way you feel the
sun through your eyelids.

Then the hand.

On her forehead. Warm. Heavy. With the calluses at the base of the fingers. The
hand stayed there, and stayed, and stayed, longer than ever before, and it shook
at the fingertips, and Cecília didn't open her eyes.

And then her mother said something, very quietly, almost without a voice,
something Cecília didn't expect and that almost made her open her eyes.

"I don't remember, Ciça."

The hand stayed a little longer. Then it went away. Her mother's footsteps went to
the room that didn't know anyone's name, and the door closed slowly.

Cecília opened her eyes in the dark.

*I don't remember.* Not what. Just that. And Cecília knew, with the sharpness of
things known at six, without anyone having told her, that her mother was talking
about the sentence. The high bed. The last thing.

Her mother didn't remember what she had said.

And Cecília had spent a hundred and nine years remembering it for her.
