There's something she isn't telling me.

I've known it for three weeks, since the morning at the hospital, since the
*okay* to everything. I know it the way I knew, on a site, that a foreman was
hiding a crack behind the plaster: not because I could see the crack, but because
he stood in front of the wall the whole time.

Ciça stands in front of a wall the whole time.

She came home after six days. She walks less. She doesn't wear the mesh anymore,
not even to practice. She sleeps more, sitting in the armchair, and wakes with a
start, and smiles when she sees me, quickly, like someone hiding something behind
the smile. At night, sometimes, through the thin wall, I hear her breathing stop,
and count to three, and come back, and I hear the house talking to her in a
whisper that doesn't reach me. In the morning I ask. She says she slept well.

The house tells me nothing. I asked again, the first night, and the second, and
the house repeated the same sentence, *I'm not authorized to discuss Mrs.
Cecília's medical condition*, in the same calm voice, until I stopped asking
because I realized I was shouting at a wall.

In the third week, I called Ravi.

I don't know why him. Maybe because he was the first voice. I touched my fingers
to my wrist on the sidewalk on Tessel, at lunchtime, with Noor waiting a few
meters away, and said his name, and the voice arrived close to my ear, the same
as always, calm, tired.

"Helena. Is everything all right?"

"I don't know. There's something she isn't telling me."

He was quiet for a while. It wasn't the delay of someone choosing words. It was
another kind.

"I can't talk about that," he said at last.

"So there is something."

"I didn't say that."

"Ravi." I looked up, at my building in its splint, at the copper beetles
stopped at the knots of the mesh. "You said something, on the platform. The day I
left. You said *look after her*."

Silence.

"Who were you saying it to?"

He breathed. I heard him breathe, close to my ear, as if he were beside me on the
sidewalk.

"You have the right to read the contract," Ravi said. "That's all I can tell
you. It's in the law. The restored person has the right to read the restitution
contract. All you have to do is ask."

"Ask who?"

"The sponsor." A pause. "Or whoever has a copy."

I hung up without saying goodbye. I stood on the sidewalk, with my hand on my
wrist. Noor was watching me from a distance, with the face of someone who knew not
to ask.

I didn't ask Ciça for the contract. I couldn't. I spent three days with the
sentence in my mouth, at breakfast, at dinner, when I said good night. *Can I
read the contract?* Five words. I've said much harder things in my life. And I
couldn't, because I knew that if she'd hidden it, it was because she didn't want
me to read it, and asking was saying I knew she was hiding it.

On the fourth day, Irene called me.

---

Harlo is a low neighborhood. It's the only one I know where the buildings don't
go above the sixth floor, and Irene lives on the third, and she tells me,
opening the door, without my asking, that it's because she's afraid of heights
and hasn't told anyone her whole life.

"Not even my grandmother," she says. "She thinks I like gardens."

"Do you like gardens?"

"I hate them."

The apartment is full of plants anyway. Succulents, mostly. In every window, on
every shelf, in small pots, green and fleshy, plants that hardly ever need water.

"They're easy," says Irene, seeing me look. "I'm not good at looking after
things. These look after themselves."

She sits me down in the living room, which is small, with a window onto the
street and an old couch, and goes to make coffee. She makes real coffee, in a
metal pot, on the stove, and I hear from the kitchen the sound I know, and realize
she learned this from me, or because of me, at the pasta lunch, and I don't know
what to do with that.

On the living room wall, there's a woman.

It isn't a photograph. It's an image that moves, slowly, on a stretch of the wall,
life-size: a woman of about sixty, laughing, on a balcony, with the wind in her
graying hair, turning her face toward whoever is filming and saying something
that can't be heard. The image lasts about five seconds and starts again.
Laughing. Wind. Turning her face. Saying.

She has the wide mouth. My mouth.

"That's my mother," says Irene, from the kitchen doorway, with two cups. "Lívia."

My granddaughter.

I stand looking. Laughing, wind, turning her face, saying. Laughing, wind, turning
her face, saying. A woman who was born twenty years after I died and who died nine
years before I came back, and who has my mouth, and who is saying something to
someone on a balcony that I'll never know.

"What's she saying?" I ask.

"*Stop filming, Irene.*" Irene sets the cups on the low table. "She hated being
filmed. It's the only video I have of her where she's laughing."

I drink the coffee. It's good. Irene sits facing me, on a chair, with her cup in
both hands, not drinking.

"Grandma looked after her at the end," she says. "Eleven months. In the room you
sleep in."

"I know. She told me."

"Told you how?"

"She said it was the worst thing."

Irene nods. She looks at the image of her mother on the wall, laughing, and then
at me.

"She sat beside her every night," she says. "Eleven months. In the room you sleep
in, and at the end in the hospital. Grandma was a hundred and five, a hundred and
six. She didn't sleep. I slept on the living room couch and at three in the
morning I'd go in there and she'd be awake, watching my mother. Counting. I never
knew what. One day I asked and she said: *her breathing*."

I put my cup on the table.

"Why are you telling me this?"

Irene doesn't answer right away. She puts her own cup on the table, beside mine,
and reaches into the pocket of her coat, which she hasn't taken off since I
arrived, and takes out a sheet of paper. Folded in four. The folds worn, as if
it had been opened and closed many times.

"Because I think you have the right to read this," she says. "And because I think
she's never going to show you."

---

It's the contract.

I know before I unfold it, because Ravi told me, and because Irene is holding the
sheet like someone holding something heavier than paper.

I unfold it.

*Restitution contract.* The clinic's name, at the top. *Sponsoring party:
Cecília.* The neighborhood, Ostrel. A number. *Restored person.* My name. My date
of birth, 1993. My date of death, 2031. I'd never seen my date of death written
down. It's an ordinary day, a Thursday, and I sit looking at it as if it were a
word in another language.

The clauses. Costs, which I read and don't understand, because I still don't
know the price of anything, and then I understand, because Irene had said
*everything*. Medical liability. Guardianship: *a dependent of the sponsoring
party until the latter requests emancipation, or until five years have passed*.
I knew about that one. Every coffee reminds me.

And below:

*Declared purpose of the restitution.*

Four options, with a box in front of each. *Family reunion.* *Professional or
estate continuity.* *Contribution to research.* *End-of-life companionship.*

Only one box is ticked.

I read it. I read it again. I read it a third time, the way I do with a beam
load, and the third reading doesn't change anything, and I sit with the sheet in
my hand, in my great-granddaughter's living room, with my granddaughter laughing
on the wall, laughing, wind, turning her face, saying *stop filming*.

*End-of-life companionship.*

---

I understand everything at once.

It isn't like discovering something. It's like when the beetles' mesh lights up
a yellow line in a slab and, suddenly, all the other strange things in the
building, which were loose, which made no sense, arrange themselves around that
line and make sense.

The room that doesn't know my name. Because she wanted me to have a place that
was only mine, yes, but also because she knew I'd need somewhere the house
wouldn't tell me what I was there for.

Irene's question on the day of the pasta, which I didn't understand and answered
the way you'd answer where rain comes from: *do you know why she brought you
back?*

The *okay* to everything. Her hand squeezing mine in the hospital and telling me
to go off to Corvel. The house's mode that doesn't tell me. Ravi, on the
platform, with that face I couldn't read, saying *look after her*. He said it to
me. He said it to me because he knew what I was there for, and thought I ought to
know too, and couldn't say it, and said what he could.

And the day I woke up. Ravi on the other side of the glass. *Who asked? Who
paid?* And him: *your daughter.* And me thinking it was love. That it was only
love. That a daughter of a hundred and fifteen had spent everything the family
had to see her mother again, out of missing her, and that was all.

It was love. I know it was. I'm sitting here with the sheet in my hand and I know,
with the same certainty with which I know how to calculate a slab, that it was
love.

But it wasn't for me to live. It was for me to look after.

"How long?" I ask. My voice comes out strange, flat, like Ciça's voice when she
talked about Lívia in the transit.

"Six months, the doctor said. The day of the hospital." Irene looks at her own
hands. "About five now."

Five months.

I signed a five-year contract with Breno a month ago. Ciça authorized it in two
minutes.

"She knew," I say. "When she authorized the five years. She knew she wasn't
going to..."

"She knew."

"And she authorized it."

"In two minutes." Irene almost laughs, and doesn't. "She was happy. She called me
that afternoon, before the guitar. She said: *my mother's going to fix Corvel*.
She sounded like a little girl."

I fold the sheet. Once, twice. I hand it back to Irene. She doesn't take it.

"Keep it," she says. "It's yours. You're the restored person."

I put it in my pocket.

---

"I thought it was vanity," says Irene, after a while.

We're both looking at Lívia on the wall. Laughing, wind, turning her face.

"When she told me how much she was going to spend. I thought it was an old
woman's madness. That she wanted a doll. I said that to her. *A doll.*" Irene
shakes her head, slowly. "And then I read this. And I thought: she didn't buy a
doll. She bought a mother. Because her mother went away when she was six and
nobody looked after her after that. She looked after everybody. Her aunt. My
grandfather. My mother. Me. I never..." She stops. "I never thought about looking
after her. It never crossed my mind. She was Grandma. Grandma looks after."

I don't say anything. I have nothing to say.

"The money was mine," says Irene. "The inheritance. I was angry for a year. And
now I think she spent it the right way, and I'm angry that I think so."

"She didn't want me to know," I say.

"No."

"Why?"

Irene looks at me. She has Lívia's creased forehead, the one on the wall, and my
mouth.

"Because she thinks that if you know, you'll look after her out of obligation,"
she says. "And she doesn't want obligation. She said it once, without meaning to,
one night when she had a fever, last year. *The hand on the forehead only counts
when nobody asked.* I didn't understand. I thought she was delirious." She looks
at the sheet in my pocket. "Now I understand."

*The hand on the forehead.*

I used to put my hand on her forehead. When she had a fever, at three, at four, at
five, in the middle of the night, and she woke up crying, and I went to her bed
and put my hand on her forehead and said *sleep, I'm here*. That's what I said.
Every time. The same sentence. I'd forgotten I said it.

She didn't forget.

---

I leave Irene's at five.

She walks me down to the door of the building, on the ground floor, and stops on
the last step, and I stop on the sidewalk. It's the first time I've looked up at
Irene, and she seems smaller that way, and older, and afraid.

"Are you going to tell her?" she asks. "That I showed you."

"I don't know."

"She'll know. She always knows."

"I know she knows," I say. "She's my daughter."

Irene smiles, for the first time since I arrived. A crooked smile, sideways, the
smile of someone not sure she's allowed.

"Do you always know too?" she asks.

"No." I look up, at the sky over Harlo, low, gray. "I never know anything. I just
calculate."

It starts to rain.

It's the first rain of the season, and it comes all at once, in weight, straight
down, the way Ciça said it came, and the people who were on the sidewalk in Harlo
vanish in seconds, into doorways, onto the stairs, up to the walkways overhead.
Irene steps back into the building. The mouths of the cisterns open in the middle
of the street, all at the same time, with a swallowing sound. The street begins
to fill, on purpose.

I stay.

I stand on the sidewalk in Harlo, in the rain, alone, the only person in the
street, with the water running through my short hair and down my face and into
my collar, and the folded contract in my pocket getting wet, and Irene calling me
from the door, and I don't go.

In a city where nobody walks in the rain, I walk.

I don't have an umbrella. Nobody does anymore.

I'm not going to say anything to her. Not now. Not today. I don't know yet what I
feel, and I'm not going to walk into that room and say something I don't know.
She waited a hundred and nine years to tell me what she wanted, and didn't tell
me, and still hasn't. I can wait a few days.

The water rises in the street. It reaches my ankles. I walk to the next
staircase, slowly, and climb up to the walkway, and walk over the city back to
Ostrel, wet, dripping, and people step around me, and nobody looks, and I don't
look up.

I'm thinking about the hand on the forehead.
