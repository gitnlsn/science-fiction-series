I'm singing when she stops.

I don't know on which line. I have my hand on her forehead and I'm singing the bath
song, quietly, the one I used to sing in 2031 on my knees on the bathroom floor with
her in the blue plastic tub, and I'm counting, without noticing I'm counting, her
breathing under my voice. The air goes in short. The wheeze at the end. The air
goes in short. The wheeze at the end.

The air goes in short.

I wait for the wheeze.

I go on singing. I don't know why. I finish the line, and the next, and my voice
goes on alone in the room, a little off-key, over the sound of the fine rain she
said was coming, and under my voice there's nothing anymore. No air. No wheeze. Her
forehead is warm under my hand, the same. Her mouth, slightly open, the same. Her
eyes closed, the same. Everything the same and nothing.

I stop singing in the middle of a word.

On the other side of the armchair, Irene raises her head.

---

"Cardiorespiratory event," says the house, from the neutral point in the middle of
the room, in its usual calm voice. "Mrs. Cecília."

And after a pause I had never heard, a pause longer than the others, as if the house
also needed a moment:

"Non-preservation directive on record. The clinic will not be called for
preservation. Would you like me to notify the death services?"

Irene looks at me. She doesn't speak. She has her grandmother's hand in hers and her
wide mouth pressed into a line, and she looks at me as if I'd know what to do.

I do.

It's the one thing in this city I know better than anyone alive.

"Wait," I say.

"Ms. Helena, the protocol recommends..."

"Fourteen minutes." My voice comes out steady, the voice from the site. "Don't
notify anyone. Don't call anyone. Don't say anything. Fourteen minutes."

The house is quiet. It's the silence of instructions that don't fit in any drawer.

"I'm not authorized..." it begins.

"Do what she says," says Irene. "I'm the responsible party. Do what she says."

The house falls silent.

It doesn't say *recorded*. It doesn't say anything. For the first time since I came
into this room, a year ago, the house simply stops talking, and there's only the
rain on the glass, and Irene breathing on the other side of the armchair, and me.

---

In the first minute, I don't do anything.

I keep my hand on her forehead. I don't take it away. Her forehead is warm. I know
it's going to cool and I don't want to be far away when it does.

In the second, I think of the badly shaven doctor. Sitting on the edge of my bed,
with his hand on top of mine. *It's the ones who stay who feel the fourteen
minutes.* I thought, in 2031, that it was a nice sentence. That it was one of those
things young doctors say because they've read it somewhere. It wasn't. It was the
most exact truth anyone ever told me, and I didn't understand it, because in 2031 I
was the one going, not the one staying.

In the third, I think of Marta.

I think of my sister. The letter says only *ice cream with Aunt Marta*, five
words, and I know the rest without anyone telling me, because I know Marta. I know
she sat across from Ciça and didn't order anything for herself. I know there was a
clock on the wall, because there always is, and that Marta watched it from eleven
until fourteen past, without blinking. With my six-year-old daughter in front of
her, in crooked braids, eating ice cream. Marta felt the fourteen minutes for me,
and for Ciça, and for herself, and I never knew. I died without knowing. I came
back without knowing. I found out today, with my hand on a forehead.

There's no team in the next room. There are no silver cases in the hallway. There's
a child's shoe standing upright, with the toe pointing in.

In the fourth minute, the rain gets heavier.

---

In the fifth, Irene speaks.

"Did she tell you about the first time my mother said *Mom*?"

I don't lift my eyes from Ciça.

"No."

"In the backyard. With a spoon. Digging in the dirt." Irene speaks quietly, as if
her grandmother were asleep and mustn't wake. "She told me the night of the letter.
She said she'd never told anyone. That she had a whole lot of things kept. That she
thought keeping was looking after."

"She was like that from when she was little," I say. "She kept stones. She kept
candy wrappers. She kept a shoe for a hundred and nine years."

Irene almost laughs. She doesn't.

"I didn't know anything about her," she says. "Nothing. Sixty-nine years as her
granddaughter. I knew her blood pressure medication and her doctor's name. I didn't
know you asked her not to be afraid of the rain. On the last day. She told me, that
night."

"I asked her that?"

"She said you did. That you asked her three things: do what her aunt said, eat her
vegetables, and not be afraid of the rain."

I look at my daughter's face, the thousand lines, the nearly faded mole at the
corner of her eye. I don't remember asking any of that. The day is hers. But it
sounds like me.

"I think she did as she was told," I say. "All three."

In the sixth minute, her forehead begins to cool. Very little. A degree, maybe. I
feel it in my palm. I don't take my hand away.

---

In the seventh minute, I say something to Irene I didn't know I was going to say.

"Thank you for the money."

She turns her face to me, suddenly, with Lívia's creased forehead.

"It wasn't mine."

"It was. It was your inheritance. She told me. Ciça told me. The apartment
downstairs, the one in Harlo, your grandfather's fund. Everything. You lost
everything so I could be here."

Irene is quiet for a while. The rain beats on the glass. Her hand stays in her
grandmother's.

"I was angry for a year," she says at last. "I said *doll*. I said she was buying a
doll with my inheritance." She looks at her grandmother. "And then you came, and
cooked, and burned the garlic, and made me an egg I didn't eat, and she laughed. I'd
never seen her laugh like that. Not with my mother. Not with me." Her voice breaks,
and she steadies it. "She spent it the right way, Helena. It took me a year to
admit it, and now I admit it. She spent it the right way."

"I owe you."

"You don't." Irene shakes her head. "Nobody owes anybody anything in this house.
She left it in writing."

In the eighth minute, Irene reaches her free hand across the armchair, across her
grandmother's lap, and I reach out mine, the one that isn't on the forehead, and we
hold on. Over her. Irene's hand is old, cold, with the ring from a marriage that
doesn't exist anymore. Mine is new. Neither of them shakes.

We stay like that. Three hands in one, and mine on the forehead.

---

In the ninth minute, I think about the sentence.

*I'll come back when you need me.*

I don't remember saying it. I'll never remember. But I think about it now, with my
hand on my daughter's forehead, and for the first time I don't care whether it was a
promise or a blanket. Because I came back. I came back twice. Once because she
called me, and once because I wanted to. And the second undoes the first, or
completes the first, or both, like a cold joint: two pours on different days, that
didn't bond properly, and that hold the same weight.

In the tenth minute, the rain stops.

Just like that, suddenly, the way it started. Out of season. The last drop runs down
the glass on a diagonal and is gone, and the city outside is shining, wet, with the
channels filling slowly and the walkways lit, and the sky clearing behind a cloud
that's already leaving.

She knew before. She always knew before.

In the eleventh, I think that this is the minute. In 2031, the team started at
eleven. Ravi told me, on the other side of the glass, on the first day: *there was,
eleven minutes*. In the eleventh minute of 2031, someone opened a silver case in the
room next to my bed, and began to keep my head for a future that might never come.

Today, in the eleventh minute, nobody opens anything.

*After me, nothing.*

I understand now. I didn't understand when I read it, sitting on the step on Tessel,
beside Rafael's letters. I thought it was sadness, even though she wrote that it
wasn't. It isn't. It's the opposite of sadness. It's a woman who spent a hundred and
nine years waiting for someone to come back and who decided that nobody, ever again,
would have to wait for her. No granddaughter. No great-granddaughter. No little girl
in an ice cream shop.

She switched off the future so that nobody would be left owing.

It's the most generous thing anyone has ever done for me, and it wasn't for me.

---

In the twelfth minute, her forehead is cold.

Not very. Enough. I feel the temperature change in my palm, slowly, a degree, another,
like concrete curing under a tarp, and I think, without meaning to, because I'm an
engineer and that's how I think, that everything that cools shrinks a little, and
that that's why there are joints. So that things can cool without cracking.

In the thirteenth, I take my hand away.

Slowly. Not because I want to. Because she's cold and my hand doesn't warm anything
anymore, and holding a cold forehead is something else, which isn't looking after.
I take my hand away and smooth her hair, still a little damp from the bath, back, the
way she combed it. I settle the throw over her knees. Over her feet too. Without
anyone asking.

In the fourteenth minute, I look at Irene.

She's crying without a sound, the way her grandmother cried. She still has her
grandmother's hand in hers and my hand in the other.

"And then?" says Irene, in a broken voice, laughing and crying, the usual question,
the one at the end of every story, the one Ciça found funny when she was little.

And I laugh. The high laugh of the new throat, which can't keep things apart. I
laugh and cry and there's no difference.

"And then it's over," I say. "She'd have liked knowing that. It has a beginning, a
middle and an end."

"How do you know when it's over?"

"Because it stops."

---

"You can notify them," I say to the house.

The house takes half a second. Then it speaks, in its usual calm voice, lower.

"Recorded. Time of death, twelve twenty-three. Death services notified. Clinic
notified." A pause. "Ms. Helena. Ms. Irene. My condolences."

I don't know if the house feels anything. I don't know if someone programmed that
sentence or if it learned it by itself, in sixty years of looking after Ciça. I
don't ask.

"Thank you," says Irene.

"Thank you," I say.

The house doesn't say anything more. And I realize, there, sitting on the rug, that
the house isn't going to say *Mrs. Cecília* anymore. Never again. The voice that
called my daughter by name a hundred times a day will go on existing, at the neutral
point in the middle of the room, and will say other names, and none of them will be
hers.

Only the armchair didn't know her name. And now the armchair is the only thing that
stays the same.

Irene gets up. She goes to the kitchen. I hear the metal coffee pot, the one from
Harlo, which she brought in one of her bags, being put on the manual hotplate. I hear
the hiss of the water starting to boil.

I stay. Sitting on the rug, beside the armchair, with my head resting against its
arm, in the place where Ciça used to rest her hand. I look at the ceiling.

It has joints.

I count. One by the window, between two ceiling panels, that doesn't hold anything
up. Another, crooked, by the door to my room, darker, thicker, that doesn't follow
the pattern of the others.

A patch. Someone broke something up there, a long time ago, and fixed it, and
couldn't make it the same.

A scar.

I sit looking at it until the coffee is ready.
