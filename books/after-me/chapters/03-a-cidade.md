The gray wall turns transparent on a Tuesday, at six in the morning, without
warning.

I'm awake. I sleep very little now and wake early, the way I slept in 2031 the
week of a pour, when my head wouldn't switch off thinking about the late truck.
I'm lying on my side, facing the wall, and it starts to clear from the bottom
up, slowly, like water rising in a glass.

First I see the sky. A sky white with heat, even this early, without a cloud.
Then the roofs, because the room is high up, and the roofs are green. Not green
paint: green growth. One after another as far as I can see, covered in tall
grass and low shrubs, with narrow paths through the middle and, here and there,
people walking among the plants, in wide hats, with baskets.

I get up and go to the wall. I walk well, now. Five weeks of physical therapy
inside a room four meters by five, and the floor doesn't need to hold me up
anymore.

I put my hands on the glass and look down.

The city is made for the water. I understand that before I understand anything
else, the same way you understand a foundation plan before you read the
dimensions. Every façade has channels, and the channels are huge, open troughs a
meter wide running diagonally down the buildings, floor by floor, joining one
another like tributaries, until they reach the bottom in round mouths in the
street. There's no roof without a channel. There's no channel without somewhere
to go. Someone drew this whole city thinking about the path of every drop.

And the walkways. Covered, wide, made of a milky material that lets the light
through, they join one building to the next at the height of the tenth floor,
crossing over the streets, and on both sides of them there are people, lots of
people, early in the morning, going somewhere. Down below, in the street, almost
nobody. The street is for the water. The city walks up top.

I do a calculation without meaning to. A span of forty meters, at least, between
one building and the next. No column in the middle. Loaded with people. I look
for the cables, the stays, the truss, something to explain how that thing stays
up, and I can't find anything. The walkway is thin as a leaf and it's there.

"That doesn't exist," I say, alone, to the glass.

"You're going to say that a lot today," says Ravi's voice, close to my ear.
"Good morning. Get dressed. We're going out at seven."

---

We go out at seven.

The clothes he's left for me are light, a fabric that looks like linen and
cools against the skin when the sun hits it. There's a wide-brimmed hat. There's
also something small and transparent that he shows me how to put in my nose, a
filter I can barely feel once it's in.

"The vaccines are done," he says. "But your body is still learning. The filter
stays in until the end of the day. Don't touch anything you don't need to touch,
don't eat anything that isn't from a kitchen, and if someone sneezes near you,
you move away without saying excuse me."

"I'm a baby."

"You're a thirty-eight-year-old person with a baby's immune system. It's worse.
A baby doesn't want to go and see everything."

He takes me down the clinic corridor, which I've never seen. It's long, curved,
with doors just like mine on both sides, and behind one of them, through glass
like mine, I catch a glimpse of an old man sitting on the bed, looking at his
own hands. I stop. Ravi stops with me.

"Who is he?"

"Someone who woke up yesterday."

The man raises his eyes to the ceiling. I look away and keep walking, because I
know what he's looking for and I don't want him to see me see it.

---

On the walkway, the heat.

It isn't the heat I knew. In 2031 the city got hot, really hot, with
thirty-eight-degree days we counted on our fingers and talked about in the
elevator. This is something else. It's a heat with weight, that presses against
the whole body at once, like a wet blanket. It's seven thirty in the morning.

The walkway is cool inside. The milky roof filters the light and something in it
draws the heat upward; I feel a current of air on my face that doesn't come from
anywhere. The floor is soft underfoot, slightly elastic, and when I step harder
it doesn't give: it answers. I crouch and run my hand over it. It's warm and
smooth, with a very fine grain, and at a seam, where one panel meets the next, I
see a darker line, irregular, like a scar on skin.

"It cracked," I say. "And it closed up on its own."

Ravi crouches beside me, in no hurry, and looks at the seam as if he'd never
looked at a seam in his life. He probably never has.

"Must have."

"There's something alive in this. Inside the material. That closes the crack."

He shrugs, politely.

"I work with people, Helena. I don't know what the floor is made of."

Nobody does, I think. I stand up and look around. People are passing us all the
time: a young man with a child on his shoulders, two women in blue uniforms, an
old man with a cane talking to himself, who I realize afterward isn't talking to
himself, he's talking to someone only he can hear. Nobody looks at the floor.
Nobody looks at the ceiling. Nobody looks at the forty-meter span without a
column that is holding everybody up in the air. They walk over it the way I used
to walk over a slab I'd calculated myself, without thinking it could fall,
because it doesn't.

I'm the only person on this walkway looking up.

That gives me a loneliness I didn't expect. Not the loneliness of not knowing
anyone. The loneliness of not knowing the floor.

---

We spend the morning walking.

Ravi shows me what he thinks I need to see, and I look at what I think I need
to. He shows me a covered square, with trees inside, which he calls a square as
if it were obvious that a square has a roof. I look at the square's columns,
which aren't columns, they're trunks, real trunks, wood, twenty stories high,
and the whole building on top of them.

"It's wood," I say.

"It is."

"A twenty-story building in wood."

"There are thirty-story ones. There are forty, down south."

In 2031 we argued about this at conferences. There were a few buildings like
that in the world, not many, and we went to visit them in groups, with badges,
and took pictures of the beams. Here nobody takes pictures. A woman walks by
resting her hand absently on one of the trunks, the way you'd rest it on a
handrail.

He shows me the transit. It's a long capsule, no window in front, no driver,
that stops at the walkway platform without any sound at all and opens its doors
before we get there, the way the clinic floor knew where I was going to step.
Inside it's cool and quiet. It moves fast, at the height of the floors, and I
watch the windows of the buildings go by, one after another, kitchens, living
rooms, a man doing exercises, a child leaning against the glass looking out, who
sees me go by and isn't interested.

"There aren't any cars," I say.

"There are, outside. Not in here."

"And the street?"

"The street belongs to the water." He says it the way Ravi says everything, as
something known. "In the rainy season, in the afternoon, the streets fill up.
They fill up on purpose. Then the water goes to the cisterns. Nobody walks in
the street in the afternoon."

I think of the flood of 2027, which I watched from the office, at the window,
with the water climbing the avenue up to the height of the car doors, and us not
knowing what to do except watch. Nobody had thought about the path of every
drop. Someone, later, did.

I don't know who. I'll never know. I'm grateful to that person anyway, the way
you're grateful to someone who plants a tree that won't give shade until later.

---

The café is on a lower walkway, at the height of the fifth floor, in a corner
where the walkway widens and there are tables.

Ravi orders two cups by touching his wrist to a counter, and the cups appear,
and I don't see who made them, or how, and I don't see anything that looks like
money passing from one hand to another. I know it cost something. I don't know
how much. I have no idea whether it's expensive or cheap, whether a coffee in
2140 is worth a minute or an hour of somebody's work, and it's the first time in
a long time that I don't know the price of a coffee. That, more than the
walkways, tells me I'm not from here.

The coffee is good. It tastes like coffee.

I drink it slowly, with the hat in my lap, looking at the city through the glass
of the walkway. And that's when I see the building.

It's far off, some six blocks away, to the right, between two new buildings much
taller than it is. It's lower, darker, a different color. Its windows are a
different size. It has the look of something made by other people, with a
different ruler. Around it there's a kind of cage, a thin, pale mesh that wraps
it from the ground to the roof, like a splint on a broken arm.

I count the floors. I count twice.

Fourteen.

"Ravi."

He follows my gaze.

"That building," I say. "The dark one, with the cage. What avenue is that?"

He thinks, or checks something I can't see.

"Tessel. In Corvel. The old center."

Tessel Avenue. His voice says the name as if it were any address, and for me it
isn't an address, it's a year of my life. Two years. The slabs on the ninth and
tenth floors, which gave us trouble. The foreman, whose name was Clóvis and who
called me *Doc* with an irony that later turned into respect. The pour on the
fourth floor, which was in the small hours, in the rain, and me there, in boots,
holding the flashlight for the boy running the vibrator through the concrete.

It was going to last fifty years. I calculated it for fifty years, with the
margin the code required, and a little more, because that's how I was. It's been
a hundred and eleven years since it was finished. It's gone past its term twice
and it's there. In a splint, but it's there.

"I made that building," I say.

It isn't quite true. Nobody makes a building. But I did the calculations that
hold it up, and that, for me, is making it.

"Do you want to go there?"

I do. I want it more than I've wanted anything since I woke up, and I know it
isn't today. I know that if I go near it today, with this body that hasn't
learned what a fever is yet, with this filter in my nose, with this borrowed hat,
I'll put my hand on its wall and I won't know how to leave.

"Another day," I say.

Ravi nods. He knows how to wait.

At the next table, a little girl is looking at me.

She's about seven, hair cut straight at chin length, and she's sitting with her
mother, who is talking to someone who isn't there, her eyes slightly unfocused,
the look of someone listening to a voice close to her ear. The girl isn't
talking to anyone. She's looking at my nose.

I'd forgotten the filter. I put my hand up to it.

"Are you from before?" she asks.

Her mother comes back from wherever she was, startled, and takes hold of her
daughter's arm, and apologizes to me with a gesture, and I make another gesture
saying it's fine.

"Before what?" I ask.

The girl thinks. She doesn't seem embarrassed. She seems to be looking for the
right word, with the seriousness of someone who's been corrected many times.

"Before everything," she says.

"I am."

She nods, satisfied, as if I'd confirmed something she already knew.

"My great-grandma is from before too. She came back."

The mother goes red. I don't know why, and I put that away to think about
later: that here, coming back is something you go red about in front of other
people. Ravi, beside me, drinks his coffee and says nothing, and I realize he's
letting it happen, that this is part of the job too.

"And what's she like?" I ask.

"She says words nobody understands."

"What words?"

The girl frowns, remembering.

"She says *flash drive*."

I laugh. I can't help it. The laugh comes out high, and the girl laughs too,
without knowing at what, and her mother laughs as well, relieved, and for a
moment the three of us are laughing at a word I hadn't heard in a hundred and
nine years and that should have died well before that, at the back of a drawer,
along with the cables.

"What about you?" the girl asks. "What word do you say?"

I think. I look at the covered walkway, the channels the size of rivers, the
street down below where nobody walks when it rains. I think of the kitchen in
2031, the window over the sink, and a six-year-old girl who laughed at me.

"Umbrella," I say.

The girl repeats it, slowly, trying it out: *um-brel-la*. She doesn't know what
it is. She has no reason to. In a city where the rain falls on the roofs and
nobody walks underneath it, nobody needs to carry a roof in their hand.

"It's pretty," she says at last, with the generosity of children.

The mother gets up, says something to her daughter, and the two of them leave
along the walkway. The girl looks back once, and I wave, and she waves, and then
she disappears among the people who don't look up.

---

We go back to the clinic early in the afternoon, before the rain.

In the transit, watching the windows go by, I do the arithmetic I've been
avoiding since the café. Not the building's. The other one.

The building stayed. Who stayed with it?

Clóvis was fifty in 2031. The boy with the flashlight was nineteen. Joana, from
the office, was thirty-two. My sister was forty-one, and her two sons nine and
twelve. The badly shaven doctor was maybe thirty. I make the whole list, the way
I used to make a checklist on site, one item at a time, skipping none. The
building manager where we had the kitchen with the window. The neighbor next
door, who had a dog that barked at the rain. Ciça's teacher, who was very young
and wore earrings that didn't match.

I add a hundred and nine to each one. Every one of them goes past a hundred and
twenty. Every one, without exception.

I knew that. I knew it the first time, lying down, with Ravi on the other side
of the glass. But knowing it in bed is one thing, and knowing it here, inside a
silent capsule going past the windows of kitchens where people I don't know are
making lunch, is another. The city is full. I saw more people this morning than
I used to see in a week in 2031. And not one of those people was alive when I
was.

Except one.

"Ravi. My daughter. When am I going to see my daughter?"

He takes a little while. It isn't the delay of someone hiding something. It's
the delay of someone choosing words.

"She asked for a few weeks."

"For what?"

"To get ready. To get the house ready." He hesitates. "She's a hundred and
fifteen, Helena. You're not the only one who's going to see someone for the
first time."

I look out the window. The first drop hits the glass of the capsule, thick,
heavy, and runs off diagonally. Then another. Then the whole rain comes, all at
once, as if someone had tipped over a bucket the size of the sky, and down below,
between the buildings, the streets start to fill, the way he said, on purpose.

"Does she live alone?" I ask.

"She does. She has a granddaughter. Irene. Sixty-eight."

I do the arithmetic. I can't help it; the arithmetic comes by itself.

My daughter's granddaughter. Sixty-eight years old.

"I have a great-granddaughter," I say. "A sixty-eight-year-old
great-granddaughter."

Ravi doesn't answer. There's nothing to answer. And I start laughing, the high
laugh of the new throat, and this time I don't cry at the end, because I'm too
tired to cry, and because it's funny, it really is funny, and because the only
person in the world who would find it as funny as I do is a lady of a hundred
and fifteen I haven't seen yet and whose diapers I changed.

Everyone I knew is dead. Except her.

The rain falls. The streets fill. The city walks up top.
