Noor's boy came on Thursdays.

His name was Davi and he was eleven, then twelve, and he came the first Thursday
with the guitar from 2019 under his arm, because his mother had taken the guitar
to him to change the strings, and he had changed them, by himself, watching how it
was done in a video from before, and he didn't want to give it back without
showing.

He sat on the rug, in front of the armchair, where Cecília's mother used to lie
down to count joints, and he played.

He played badly. He played the way twelve-year-old boys play, with too much force
in the right hand and too much fear in the left, and the new strings, steel, shone,
and buzzed on the frets where he didn't press properly. Cecília listened from the
armchair, with her eyes closed. Nobody had played a real instrument in that room
for sixty years.

When he stopped, she opened her eyes.

"The third finger," she said.

The boy looked at her, startled. He didn't know the old woman in the armchair was
a teacher. Nobody had told him.

"What?"

"The third finger of your left hand. On the second fret. You're laying it down.
Stand it up, as if it were a hammer. The tip. Just the tip."

He did. The chord rang out clean, whole, in the room, and stayed in the air
awhile, and the boy looked at his own hand like someone looking at a magic trick.

Cecília couldn't press a string anymore. Her fingers wouldn't close. But she could
see, and she could say, and the boy came back the next Thursday, and the one after,
and every Thursday of those three months, and Cecília taught him, from the
armchair, without playing, everything she could teach in three months to a boy who
wanted to know if it was true that the songs from before had a beginning and an
end.

"They did," she said. "They had a beginning, a middle and an end. And you knew when
it was over."

"How?"

"Because it stopped."

The boy thought that was the strangest thing he had ever heard. Cecília laughed,
and her mother, in the kitchen, laughed too, without having heard the question,
just because she heard her daughter laugh.

---

That Tuesday, Cecília woke up and asked for a bath.

"A real bath," she said.

Her mother was sitting on the floor, beside the armchair, where she slept almost
every night now, on a thin mattress Irene had brought from Harlo. She hadn't slept
in the room that didn't know anyone's name for two months. She said the living
room floor was better for her back. It wasn't. Cecília knew it wasn't, and didn't
say anything, because her mother knew too, and they both preferred it that way.

"What's a real bath?" her mother asked.

"With water. In a basin. With you."

The house knew how to give a bath. There was a chair in the bathroom that received
the body and washed it without anyone touching anything, with the water at the
exact temperature, the soap in the right amount, a warm steam at the end that dried
the skin without a towel. Cecília had used that chair for six years. It was
efficient and clean and touched no one.

Her mother looked at her for a while. Then she got up, without saying anything, and
went to the kitchen, and Cecília heard the drawer with the metal pans open, and the
water running.

She came back with a big aluminum basin, dented at one side, which Cecília hadn't
known was still in the house. Full of warm water. With a cloth and a bar of soap,
the kind nobody made anymore, which her mother must have bought at the shop in
Corvel without telling anyone.

"Right here?" her mother asked.

"Right here."

"The house is going to complain."

"Let it complain."

---

Her mother undressed her slowly, in the armchair, the way you undress a sleeping
child.

The gray dress, over the head, carefully so it wouldn't catch on her ears. The
mesh, which Cecília didn't wear anymore and which had stayed on her legs since
yesterday only because nobody remembered to take it off. Her underwear. Her mother
folded each piece and put it on the arm of the couch, one on top of the other, the
way she used to do with the school clothes, in 2031, on the bedroom chair.

And Cecília sat naked in the armchair, at a hundred and fifteen, in the morning
light, in front of her mother.

She wasn't ashamed. She had thought she would be and she wasn't. She looked at her
own body, the way her mother was looking at it, and saw what her mother saw: the
skin all folded, thin, spotted, clinging to the bones. The breasts shrunken. The
belly sunken, with an old white line crossing below the navel, from when Lívia was
born. The enormous knees on the thin legs. The scars: the one on her thumb, from the
can of peaches; one on her shoulder, from a fall on the stairs at forty; one on her
hip, from an operation at ninety; the marks of the old needles, in the crooks of
her arms, which had never quite gone. A hundred and fifteen years written, one by
one, on the skin.

Her mother wet the cloth in the basin, wrung it out, and began with the hands.

She washed one hand at a time, finger by finger, slowly, between the fingers, the
palm, the back, where the spots were darkest. She went around the ring without
trying to take it off. Then the arms. Then the shoulders, with the warm cloth
running down her back, and Cecília closed her eyes, and felt the water go down her
spine, over her ribs, one by one, the way her mother had felt her ribs at the
clinic, on the first day, before the floor.

Her mother was singing.

Quietly, a little off-key, the same song as always, which Cecília didn't know the
name of and now did: it was the song her mother sang at her bath, in 2031, in the
blue plastic tub that sat inside the shower, with the warm water, and the toys
floating, and her mother on her knees on the bathroom floor, singing. Cecília had
forgotten she remembered. She remembered it now, whole, the tune and the words, and
sang along, without a voice, only moving her lips.

Her mother saw. She didn't stop singing. She smiled, and sang louder, and washed
her neck, and behind her ears, and her face, with the tip of the cloth, the way you
wash the face of a child who doesn't want it, and Cecília wanted it.

Then the hair. Her mother tipped her head back, in the armchair, with a hand at the
nape of her neck, and poured the warm water with cupped hands, slowly, over her
forehead, and the water ran through the fine white hair and fell into the basin,
and her mother rubbed the bar of soap between her hands until it lathered, and
washed. With her fingertips. In circles. The way you wash a baby's head, careful of
the soft spot.

"Close your eyes, it stings," her mother said.

Cecília closed them.

It was the same sentence. *Close your eyes, it stings.* A hundred and ten years
later, in the same voice, hoarse now, from the new throat that was learning to be
the old one.

---

When her mother had finished and dried her with a real towel, rough, which must
also have come from the shop in Corvel, and put her in a clean nightgown, and sat
her back in the armchair, by the window, with the throw over her knees, Cecília
asked.

She hadn't planned it. The question came on its own, the way the memories came
now.

"And the scar?"

Her mother was kneeling on the rug, gathering up the basin. She stopped.

"What scar?"

"Yours. On your belly. From me."

Her mother stayed kneeling a while, with the basin in her hands. Then she put the
basin on the floor. She lifted the hem of the pale clothes, slowly, up to her
waist, and showed her.

Nothing. A smooth belly, new-skinned, no mark, no line, nothing. The belly of
someone who never.

Cecília looked. She had thought about this many times, since the first day, since
the image of the finished body Ravi had shown her and that she had asked him to
turn off. She knew there wouldn't be anything. She had known for two years. And she
looked anyway, and it hurt anyway, and it was a pain different from all the
others, because it wasn't hers: it was her mother's.

She held out her hand. The spotted, thin hand that shook. And she put it on her
mother's belly, where the line should have been. Low, just above the hair. Twelve
centimeters.

"I came out of here," she said.

"You did."

"And nothing stayed."

Her mother put her hand over her daughter's, on her own smooth belly, and pressed.

"It did," she said. "You stayed."

They stayed like that. The old woman in the armchair with her hand on the belly of
the thirty-eight-year-old woman kneeling on the rug. The daughter with her hand on
the place she had come from. Neither of them cried. They had cried everything, the
two of them, in the first weeks, on the nights in the living room. There was none
left.

"It used to itch before the rain," said Cecília.

"It did."

"You were magic."

"I wasn't. It was you." Her mother smiled. "It was you letting me know, from
inside, where you had been."

---

Her knee began to hurt at eleven.

Thin, inside, where bone meets bone. Cecília knew it. It was the rainy season, but
the rains came in the afternoon, at four, and the morning sky was clear, and the
forecast, on her mother's wrist, said zero percent until four.

"It's going to rain," said Cecília.

Her mother looked at the window. She looked at her daughter. She didn't doubt it.

"Then stay to see it," she said.

"I will."

Irene arrived at noon, the way she arrived every day now, with a bag of something
from Harlo that she thought her grandmother would eat and that her grandmother
didn't eat. She saw the aluminum basin on the rug, and her grandmother's hair still
damp, and the clean nightgown, and stopped in the doorway.

"What did you two do?"

"Bath," said her mother.

Irene looked at her great-grandmother and then at her grandmother, and something
crossed her face, quickly, that wasn't jealousy. Cecília saw it. It was something
else. It was Irene understanding that she had arrived at the end of something that
began before her.

Irene didn't say anything. She sat on the floor, on the other side of the
armchair, and took her grandmother's other hand.

Cecília stayed like that, between the two of them. Her mother on one side, her
granddaughter on the other. A hand of new, warm skin. An old hand, with the wide
mouth pressed tight above it. A hundred and fifteen years in between.

She thought of Lívia. She thought of Tomás. She thought of Aunt Marta watching the
clock in the ice cream shop. She thought of Noor's boy and the third finger
standing up, like a hammer. She thought of a house with a backyard that didn't exist
anymore and a little girl of a year and two months saying *Mom* with a spoon in her
hand. She thought of everything, one thing at a time, without hurrying, as if she
were tidying a room before going out.

She was tired. Very. With a tiredness that wasn't bad.

"Mom," she said.

"I'm here."

"Say the thing."

Her mother didn't ask what. She let go of her daughter's hand, slowly, and put her
hand on her forehead. Warm, with the calluses at the base of the fingers, not
shaking.

And she said it.

"Sleep, I'm here."

Cecília closed her eyes.

She heard, outside, the first drop hit the window. Then another. Then the rain,
fine, out of season, that nobody had predicted, not the city, not her mother's
wrist, only an old knee.

She thought: *I knew before*.

She felt the hand on her forehead. She felt Irene's hand in hers. She heard the
rain. She heard her mother start to sing, quietly, a little off-key, the bath song,
and she didn't open her eyes, and didn't need to, and didn't ask for anything.

Nobody had asked.

And she slept.
