I don't take my boots off.

I come into the hallway and stop, and the boots stay on my feet, full of mud from
Corvel, because it rained all afternoon and Tessel turned into a river under the
trees and I walked through it. I don't say *I'm home*. I haven't said it for seven
weeks, and the house has stopped waiting.

I walk into the living room in my boots.

Ciça is awake. She's in the armchair, with the throw over her knees, and the window
is dark, and the rain has stopped, and she looks at me when I come in as if she'd
been waiting for me, not today, but for seven weeks, every night, with her eyes
closed.

I take the contract out of my pocket.

It's been wet and dry and wet again, crumpled, the folds tearing, the ink smudged
in the corner where the rain in Harlo got in. I've been carrying it for seven
weeks. I've opened it so many times I don't need to open it to read it.

I put the contract on the little table, beside her photograph, my photograph, the
one on the site, me laughing in the sun.

Ciça looks at the sheet. She doesn't pick it up.

"Irene," she says.

"Irene."

She nods slowly, like someone hearing confirmation of something she already knew.

"When?"

"On a Sunday. In Harlo." I sit on the arm of the couch, facing her, in my boots.
"Before the rain started."

"Seven weeks."

"Seven weeks."

Ciça closes her eyes a moment. Opens them.

"And you didn't say anything."

"Neither did you."

---

We stay like that awhile. The house, at the neutral point in the middle of the
room, doesn't say anything. It must be measuring something. Someone's heart rate.
It must be deciding whether to call the clinic.

"Why?" I ask.

It isn't the first time I've asked that in the last seven weeks. It's the first
time I've asked it out loud. Inside myself I've asked it every possible way,
walking over the city, lying in the room that doesn't know my name, on the ninth
floor on Tessel, with Noor looking at me without asking.

"Why didn't you tell me?"

Ciça breathes. The air goes in slowly, with the wheeze at the end.

"Because you'd have looked after me," she says.

"I do look after you."

"I know." Her voice is thin, scratchy, steady. "But you look after me because you
wanted to. Because you got here and saw me and wanted to. If I'd said, on the
first day, *Mom, I brought you back to look after me until I die*, you'd have
looked after me just the same. I know you would. And I'd never have known whether
it was because you wanted to or because I'd asked." She stops. Breathes. "The hand
on the forehead only counts when nobody asked."

"Irene told me that."

"Irene tells everything." She almost smiles. "She takes after you."

I don't smile. I look at the contract on the little table.

"You put me in a house," I say, "to do a thing, and you didn't tell me what it
was. And you watched me do it. Every day. Knowing. The dish towel. The hospital
chair. The hand. You watched me do exactly what was written here, and me thinking
it was me."

"It was you."

"It was the contract."

"It was you!" She raises her voice, and her voice can't take being raised, and it
breaks in the middle, and turns into a cough. The house says something, quietly,
about breathing. Ciça makes a gesture with her hand, *shut up*, at the house, and
the house shuts up. "It was you, Mom. The contract doesn't put a dish towel to
anybody's chin. You did. The contract doesn't sleep with its mouth open in a
hospital chair. The contract doesn't know what an egg with a runny yolk is. You
were the one who knew."

---

"You said you'd come back," says Ciça.

She says it suddenly, in the middle of a silence, without looking at me. Looking at
the photograph.

I don't understand.

"What?"

"On the last day. In the bedroom. You called me in and I sat on the bed, and the
bed was high, and my feet were swinging. And I asked you if you'd come back." She
turns her face to me. Her eyes are dry. They shine. "And you said: *I'll come back
when you need me*."

The bedroom. The open curtain. The glass of water.

I don't remember.

I'm trying. I'm trying the way I tried on the first day, lying down, with Ravi on
the other side of the glass, and the way I've tried every night for the last seven
weeks, with my hand on her forehead. I go back to the kitchen, to the window over
the sink, to the silver cases in the hallway, to the shoe on its side. And the
sentence is there, at the edge, the size of a short sentence. And I can't reach
it.

But now it's here. Outside me. In the mouth of my daughter of a hundred and
fifteen, who kept it for me for a hundred and nine.

*I'll come back when you need me.*

It sounds like something I'd say. It is something I'd say. It's exactly the kind of
sentence I'd say to a six-year-old girl sitting on a high bed, with her feet
swinging, asking if her mother's coming back. A sentence that isn't yes and isn't
no. An engineer's sentence: with a margin. With a safety factor. *When you need
me.* Which could be never.

"I needed you," says Ciça. "I needed you, Mom. And I called you."

---

"I didn't choose to go so I could come back," I say.

I hear my voice saying it and I don't recognize it, because it's a voice I've
never used with her. Not when she was six, not now. It's the voice I used on site
when someone said something wrong about a structure and I needed them to stop.

Ciça looks at me.

"Aunt Marta said you chose to go so you could come back."

"Marta." I laugh, without any humor at all. "Marta couldn't accept it. She spent
my last three months telling me I was giving up. That I could try one more
treatment. That I owed it to you to stay until the last day. I told her that
because it was the only thing she could hear. *I'm going so I can come back.* She
needed a reason that had a future in it. I gave her one."

"And it wasn't true?"

"It wasn't the reason."

"Then what was?"

I get up from the arm of the couch. I can't stay sitting. I walk to the dark
window, in my boots, and leave marks of mud on the rug, in the place where I used
to lie down and count the joints.

"The disease was going to my head," I say, with my back to her, to the glass. "In
weeks. The doctors showed me. And when it got there, I was going to stop being me.
In pieces. I was going to forget things, and then people." I turn around. "I was
going to forget you, Ciça. I was going to look at you and not know who you were.
And you'd have remembered that. You'd have been six, seven, and the last thing
you'd remember of me would be a woman in a bed looking at you and asking your
name."

She doesn't say anything. Her face doesn't move.

"I didn't want that for you. I'd rather you remembered me doing your braids. Even
crooked. Even with my hand shaking. I'd rather go while I still knew your name."

"And the preservation?"

"That was the doctor." I shake my head. "He said that if I was going to choose the
day, I could donate my brain. For research. That my head was sound. That one day,
maybe, very far off... I signed. I signed it the way you sign an organ donor card.
So it would be of use for something. I never thought I'd wake up, Ciça. Never. Not
for a minute. I thought I'd end up as a slide in a lab. A statistic. The brain of
a thirty-eight-year-old woman that somebody would read in a hundred years to
understand what a brain from before was like."

"Then why did you tell me you'd come back?"

And I look at her, and she's sitting in the armchair that doesn't know her name, a
hundred and fifteen years old, with the throw over her knees, and her hands
together in her lap, squeezing one in the other, and I see the little girl on the
high bed, with her feet swinging.

"Because you asked," I say. "You were six and you asked if I'd come back. What did
you want me to say?"

---

It isn't the same story.

I know that while I'm talking, and she knows it while she's listening, and neither
of us can stop. They're two stories of the same day, the same high bed, the same
sentence, and they don't match. Like two sets of drawings for the same building
made by two people who never spoke to each other: the walls in the same place and
all the dimensions different.

In hers, I chose to go so I could come back. I traded the months I had with her
for a chance at a future, and I promised. And she spent her whole life with the
anger of one thing and the promise of the other, braided together.

In mine, I chose to go so she wouldn't see me stop being me. And the preservation
was a signature on a form, a donation, something I barely remembered doing
properly. And the sentence was what you say to a child.

And I don't remember the sentence. So not even my version is whole. I have the
reasons from before, from the weeks before, from the talks with the doctor, the
fights with Marta. I don't have the day. The day is hers. She's the one who kept
the day.

Maybe both are true. Maybe I chose to go for her sake and, at the time, sitting on
the bed, with her hand in mine, I wanted to come back. Maybe I believed, for a
minute, in my own sentence. I don't know. I have no way of knowing. The reading
doesn't choose. What was there is here, and what wasn't, isn't.

"You bought me," I say.

It comes out before I decide. It comes out of the new throat, which can't keep
things apart.

"You bought me to look after you. With Irene's money. And you wrote it on a piece
of paper. And you put me in a house without telling me. You didn't call me, Ciça.
You ordered me."

Her face closes.

She doesn't cry. She doesn't say anything. She sits looking at me from the
armchair, with her hands together, and I see that I've hit something in her I
didn't want to hit, something that had been there since the ice cream shop, and
that I, of all the people in the world, was the only one who couldn't have said
it.

I know it's unfair. I know before I finish the sentence. And I can't unsay it.

"I need some air," I say.

---

I go out in my boots.

I cross the hallway without looking back, and the door opens before I get there,
the way everything in this city opens before you get there, and I hate it, for the
first time I really hate it, a door that won't even let me slam it.

In the building corridor, I stop.

I lean against the wall. I hear, through the door that closed by itself behind
me, the house talking in there, low, in the calm voice. *Mrs. Cecília. Breathe with
me. One. Two.*

And I don't hear Ciça. Not her breathing. Not the wheeze at the end.

I almost go back. I put my hand on the door, and the door starts to open, because
it recognizes me, because it knows my name even if my room doesn't, and I take my
hand away, and the door stops, ajar, waiting, and then closes.

*Three. Four.*

The house looks after her. The house knows the exact number. The house calls the
clinic in four minutes. The house doesn't need me.

That's what she brought me back for, and the house was already doing it.

I go down the emergency stairs, because I don't want the transit, I don't want the
walkway, I don't want anything in this city that knows where I'm going before I
do. Twenty-two floors. I go down counting the steps, the way I used to count the
joints. On the tenth, my legs shake. On the fifth, I sit on a step and stay
there, in the dark, with my hands over my face, with the mud from my boots drying
on the concrete steps.

Nobody finds me. The stairwell is the only place in the building the house doesn't
reach.

On the ground floor, I push the door with both hands, and it's heavy and old and
doesn't open by itself, and I go out into the street in Ostrel, which is flooded
shin-deep from the afternoon rain, dark, empty, with the mouths of the cisterns
swallowing slowly.

I don't know where I'm going. I know it's away from that room.

I walk down the flooded street, under the lit-up walkways, through the water,
alone, the only person in the street, toward Corvel. Toward the only place in this
city that was made with my hands.

Behind me, on the twenty-second floor, in a lit window, my daughter is breathing
with a house.

Or isn't.

I don't look back to find out.
