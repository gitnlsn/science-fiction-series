I go alone.

It's the first time. I've been living in Ciça's house for ten weeks, and in those
ten weeks I've learned to take the transit, to cross the walkways without looking
up at every span, to order a coffee by touching my wrist to the counter. The
first time I did that alone, the counter hesitated half a second and then
showed, in small, polite letters, *dependent: Cecília*, and the coffee appeared.
I stood looking at those words until they faded. I drank the coffee standing
up, fast, as if someone were going to come and charge me for it.

Today I don't have coffee. Today I take the transit from the Ostrel platform at
seven ten and get off in Corvel at seven thirty-four, and I go down from the
walkway by a staircase, a real staircase, with steps, because Corvel is the old
center and still has staircases, and I step into the street.

The street. It's morning, it hasn't rained yet, and the sidewalks are dry, and
there are people walking on them, not many, but some. The round mouths of the
cisterns are open in the middle of the asphalt, each the size of a table, with
a fine grate over the top. The buildings in Corvel are lower, older, all
different heights, and the channels on them were added later, you can tell,
hung on the façades like patches. This is what's left of the city I knew. The
city I knew didn't turn into this one; it was covered over by it, like a drawing
under another drawing.

Tessel isn't an avenue anymore. It's a wide street with trees down the middle,
where there used to be four lanes of cars and a median with dry grass. The trees
are enormous. They must be seventy years old. Under them go two children in
uniform, an old man with a dog, a woman pushing something I first take for a
stroller and then see is a shopping cart that moves on its own, following her a
meter behind, like a dog that doesn't bark.

My building is in the middle of the block, on the left.

---

Up close, the cage isn't a cage.

It's a mesh. Thin, pale, made of a material that isn't metal and isn't plastic,
and that wraps the whole building from the ground to the roof, about thirty
centimeters out from the façade, held to it by rods that pass through the wall
at regular intervals, like pins through cloth. The strands of the mesh are the
thickness of a finger and cross in diamonds, and where they cross there are
thicker knots, and the rods come out of the knots. None of it was assembled. It
was grown, or woven, or some other word I don't have. The rods go into the wall
with no hole around them, no burr, no crack. As if the concrete had made way.

And in the middle of the mesh, crawling over it, there are bugs.

They aren't bugs. They're machines the size of a hand, with six legs,
copper-colored, that climb up and down the strands of the mesh in no hurry, and
stop at the knots, and stay still for a while, and then move on. There must be
about a hundred. They're all over the façade, and when the sun hits them they
shine, and the whole building looks like a trunk covered in beetles.

I put my hand on the wall. On my wall.

Under the mesh, the ceramic cladding we chose in 2027, gray with a thread of
green, is faded and cracked and missing in places, with the concrete showing
underneath. The concrete I watched arrive by truck. That I watched come down
the pump, and be spread, and vibrated by the boy with the flashlight, and cure
for twenty-eight days under a wet tarp that someone kept forgetting to wet and I
had to remember. It's cold. It's rough. It's there.

I stand with my hand on the wall and I don't know how to leave, exactly as I knew
would happen.

"Can I help you?"

---

It's a woman of about forty-five, in a hard hat. A real hard hat, white, like
mine in the photograph, only thinner and with no chin strap. She has black hair
pulled back in a bun and her arms crossed and the face of someone who has run a
lot of curious people off that sidewalk.

"This is a site," she says. "You can't touch the mesh."

"I'm not touching the mesh. I'm touching the building."

She looks at my hand, between two strands, on the wall. She looks at me.

"Not the building either."

I take my hand away. She uncrosses her arms, satisfied, and I see on her vest,
which is gray and not orange, a name: *Noor*. And underneath, smaller,
*structures*.

"You're an engineer," I say.

"I am." She frowns. "Where are you from?"

I don't know how to answer. So I tell the truth, because it's what I have.

"I calculated this building."

Noor laughs. It isn't a mean laugh; it's the laugh of someone who hears something
absurd at seven forty in the morning and doesn't have time for it. Then she stops
laughing, because she's looking at my face, and at my nose, where there hasn't
been a filter for a long time, and at the way I'm standing in front of the
building.

"You're from before," she says. She doesn't ask.

"I am."

She's quiet for a while. Then she takes off her hard hat, scratches her head, and
puts it back on.

"This building is from 2029," she says.

"It was finished in 2029. Work started in 2027. The foreman's name was Clóvis.
The cladding is ceramic, gray with a thread of green, because the architect
wanted gray and the owner wanted green and I was the only one with no opinion."
I hear my voice, which still isn't mine, saying these things fast. "The slabs on
the ninth and tenth floors gave us trouble."

Noor doesn't move.

"What trouble?" she asks, very slowly.

---

She takes me inside.

There's a door in the mesh, a section that opens when she touches her wrist to
it, and behind it the building's door, the original door, glass and aluminum,
which I recognize and which has no glass in it now, only the frame. The lobby is
empty. There's no floor anymore; there's the screed, bare, and marks on the
ground where the front desk used to be. The elevator has been ripped out. The
shaft is open and, looking up, I see light coming in at the top, fourteen floors
above, through the open roof.

We go up the stairs. The original fire stairs, concrete, with the iron handrail
I fought for myself so it would be iron and not aluminum. I count the floors. On
the fourth, I stop.

"Wait."

Noor waits. I crouch on the landing, by the wall, where the step meets the floor,
and run my hand over the concrete. I search. I don't know if it's still there. I
don't know if they covered it, or sanded it, or painted over it at some point in
the last hundred and eleven years.

It's there.

*R.S. 28*. Scratched into the wet concrete with the point of a nail, one night
in the rain, by the boy with the flashlight. Rafael. Nineteen. I saw it, at the
time, and I told him off, and he went red, and said *sorry, Doc*, and I said
there was no getting it out now, that the concrete was already setting, that he
was going to have to live with it forever. And he said: *okay then*.

I run my finger over the letters. They're shallow, worn, almost erased by the
feet of everyone who has gone up and down here for a century. But they're there.
It's the only mark in the world, as far as I know, of someone I knew.

"What is that?" asks Noor, behind me.

"A boy," I say. "He was nineteen."

I don't explain any more. She doesn't ask any more. I stand up and we keep
climbing.

---

On the ninth floor there are people. Three people and a table. The table is a
pale surface in the middle of the empty floor, with no legs, floating at waist
height, and on it, inside it, the whole building, drawn in light, transparent,
with the slabs and the columns and the beams in thin lines of different colors,
turning slowly.

I stop in front of the table and forget to breathe.

It's my building. It's the drawing of my building, in three dimensions, turning
in the air, with every bar of steel marked in red inside the gray concrete, and
I've never seen it like this. I saw it in plan, in section, on a flat screen, on
paper. I put it together in my head, piece by piece, for two years. I never saw
it whole, all at once, in the air, like a thing that exists.

"This is what we know about it," says Noor. "What the mesh has read." She points
at the copper beetles outside, through the window with no glass. "They read the
concrete from the inside. Where the steel is, how much, what state it's in. The
model puts it all together and says what the building can take and what the
mesh has to take for it."

"And the original design?"

An older man, with a short white beard, sitting on a crate near the table, gives
a dry laugh.

"The original design is a file that hasn't opened in anything since before I was
born," he says. "Like everything from that time. We work with what the mesh
reads."

The files that don't open. Ciça told me about the photos. I hadn't thought about
the designs. My drawings, my calculation reports, the spreadsheets I checked in
the middle of the night, all of it turned into something that doesn't open in
anything.

"Breno," says Noor, pointing at the man. "He owns this."

"The company," Breno corrects her. "The building belongs to the city." He looks
me up and down, without getting up from the crate. "Noor said on the channel that
you calculated this."

"I did."

"Then tell me why the mesh reads too much steel on nine and ten."

I look at the table. I turn the building with my hand—the light obeys, turns
slowly, where I tell it—and I look for the ninth floor, and find it, and there
it is: the ninth-floor slab, full of red, much more red than the slabs above and
below, with the steel dense, bent, bunched in strips that make no sense to
anyone who wasn't there.

"The concrete for nine came in weak," I say. "In 2028. The test gave eighteen
megapascals where it should have given thirty. The supplier swore it didn't, and
we ran another test, and it gave seventeen. We couldn't demolish, ten was
already shored up on top of it. So I reinforced it. Extra steel, in the column
strips, and an eight-centimeter topping of new concrete over it, with
connectors. That's why the ninth-floor slab is thicker than the others. Your
model probably thinks it's a reading error."

Nobody says anything. Breno gets up from the crate.

"And ten?"

"Ten has a cold joint." I turn the building a little more and point, with my
finger, to a diagonal that crosses the tenth-floor slab, from a corner to nearly
the center. "Here. More or less. The pour stopped halfway because it rained, and
the pump broke, and when we came back, the next day, yesterday's concrete had
already set. I had the joint scabbled and treated, but it's there. It doesn't
show in the drawing because it wasn't supposed to exist."

Breno looks at Noor. Noor touches two fingers to the table and says something
quietly that I don't understand, in a word I don't know. Outside, on the mesh, I
see the beetles move. About ten of them leave what they were doing and climb the
strands up to the tenth floor, and spread out at the level of the slab, and stop.

We wait. Nobody speaks. I hear the city down below, the trees on Tessel, the
shopping cart following the woman.

On the table, in the tenth-floor slab, a thin line appears in yellow, on a
diagonal, from a corner to nearly the center.

"Well, fuck me," says Breno.

---

I leave the building at noon with a contract.

It isn't quite a contract. Not yet. Breno explained, sitting on the crate, while
Noor looked at the yellow line like someone looking at a ghost: the city is full
of buildings from before. Hundreds. All of them in Corvel and the old
neighborhoods, all of them with files that don't open, all of them with things
inside that nobody drew. Cold joints, reinforcement done in a hurry, a column
that got moved one morning because of a pipe nobody mentioned. The mesh reads
the steel, but it doesn't read the why. And the why, Breno said, is what decides
whether a building stands another fifty years or comes down in the next rainy
season.

"We've never had anybody who was there," he said. "Never. Nobody from before
wants to work on a site. People who come back want to see their family, want to
see the sea, want to stay home. I don't know how much to pay you because the job
doesn't exist."

"Make it up," I said.

He laughed, the dry laugh.

"I need authorization from your sponsor," he said. "You're a dependent."

"I know what I am."

I called Ciça from the sidewalk on Tessel, under the trees. I still don't know
how to do it properly; I touched two fingers to my wrist, the way Noor does, and
said her name out loud, and waited, and after a while her voice arrived close to
my ear, thin, scratchy, worried.

"Mom? Did something happen?"

"I went to the building."

Silence on the other end. A silence I knew, which wasn't worry.

"Is it there?" she asked.

"It is. In a splint. But it is." I breathed. "Ciça, they've offered me work. They
need you to authorize it. Because I'm..." I didn't finish.

"I authorize it," said Ciça, before I'd finished. "I authorize whatever it is.
Tell them."

And then, more quietly, with something in her voice I hadn't heard since she was
six and showing me a drawing:

"Mom. You're going to make buildings again."

"I'm going to fix them."

"It's the same thing," she said. "For you, it's the same thing."

I hung up. I stood for a while under the trees on Tessel, looking up, at my
building in its splint, covered in copper beetles, with an invisible yellow line
crossing the tenth floor, exactly where I had said it was.

I'm thirty-eight years old in a body that's two. I have a daughter of a hundred
and fifteen and a great-granddaughter of sixty-eight. I have a room that doesn't
know my name, and a wrist that says *dependent* with every coffee.

And, starting Monday, I have work.

For the first time since I woke up, someone needs something only I know.
