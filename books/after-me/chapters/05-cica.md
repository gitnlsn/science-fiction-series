I change clothes three times.

I don't have clothes to change. I have the four sets the clinic gave me, all of
the same fabric that cools in the sun, in four shades of sand, and I swap one
for another in front of the glass that turns into a mirror, as if the color
could make some difference. I put on the darkest. I take it off. I put on the
lightest, because Ciça liked me in white, and then I remember that Ciça liked
me in white at six, and that that was a hundred and nine years ago, and that
the lady who is going to walk through that door at three in the afternoon may
not like white, may not even see colors properly, may not remember she once
liked it.

I put on the lightest anyway.

"You look nice," says Ravi, close to my ear.

"I didn't ask for an opinion."

"I know. I'm giving it away free."

I'm more nervous than I've ever been. Not at the presentation of my first
project, not in labor, not the morning the badly shaven doctor sat down on the
edge of my bed. Those times I knew what was going to happen. Today I don't even
know who's going to walk in.

I tried to imagine. I've spent the last three weeks trying, lying down, at
night, with the ceiling light turned all the way down. I took Ciça's face at
six, round, with the crooked bangs I used to cut myself, and I tried to age it.
I tried the way you'd do a creep calculation, which is how much concrete
deforms over time under its own weight: slowly, a little every year, never
stopping. I added ten years, twenty, fifty. At fifty it was still her, a woman
my age, with my crooked nose. After a hundred, the calculation doesn't close.
After a hundred, I don't know what happens to a face. Nobody I knew got there.

---

The meeting room isn't my room.

Ravi takes me there half an hour early, down a corridor I didn't know yet, and
leaves me alone inside, and stays outside, in the corridor, where I can't see
him. The room is small and bright. It has a real window—real, I think, whatever
that means here—wide, letting in the view of the green roofs and, far off, the
pale mesh of the cage around my building, on Tessel. It has two armchairs, one
facing the other, and a low table with a jug of water and two glasses.

I look at the ceiling. It's a single piece, like the one in my room. But here
there's something that makes me smile: in one corner, near the window, a faint
damp stain, the size of a hand. A leak. Not even the city that thinks about
every drop can think about all of them.

I don't sit. I stand by the window, hands together, squeezing one in the other,
and I run my right thumb, again and again, along the side of the index finger
where the scar isn't.

At three o'clock the door opens.

---

First I hear the sound. A low hum, thin, almost an insect, that I don't know and
that comes from the floor, near the door.

Then I see.

A very old lady is standing in the doorway.

She's small. Smaller than me, much smaller, and very thin, and she stands the way
very light things stand, as if a stronger wind would carry her off. Her hair is
white and fine, short, combed back. The skin of her face is all folded, in a
thousand lines, and almost transparent at the temples, where you can see the
veins. She's wearing a gray dress, plain, to the knee, and under it, on her
legs, something silver that shines in the light from the window, tight as a
stocking, and that's where the hum is coming from.

She takes a step into the room. Another. The steps are short, all the same
length, as if someone were measuring them for her. On the third, she stops.

And she stays there, standing, in the middle of the room, three steps from the
door and three from me, looking.

I look back. I search for Ciça.

I search in the nose, and the nose isn't my crooked nose; it's thin and long,
and it drops a little at the tip, the way her father's did. I search in the
mouth, and the mouth is narrow, sunken, and I don't know what it was like
before. I search in the hands, which are together in front of the dress,
squeezing one in the other the way mine are, and the hands are spotted and thin
and have a wedding ring that's too big. I search in the eyes. The eyes are pale,
a brown that age has made paler, wet, very wide, and they're looking at me with
something I don't know how to read.

I can't find her.

It's the worst thing that's happened to me since I woke up, and a lot has
happened to me. Worse than the smooth belly. Worse than the arithmetic of the
hundred and nine years. I'm three steps from my daughter and I don't know where
she is.

The lady doesn't move either. The two of us stay like that, standing, squeezing
our own hands, and the hum is the only sound in the room.

And then she does something.

It's small. Maybe she doesn't even know she's done it. She lifts her right foot
off the floor and sets it on top of her left foot, instep on instep, and stays
balanced like that, on one leg, her body slightly crooked, the way children
stand when they're shy in front of someone they don't know.

The silver thing on her legs protests. The hum rises in pitch, goes shrill, and
I see the lines of the mesh stiffen to force the foot back to the floor, and
the foot doesn't go back. She stays there, standing on her own foot, at a
hundred and fifteen, fighting the machine that holds her up, without knowing
she's fighting.

Ciça used to do that. At the school door, on the first day. At her cousin's
birthday party. In front of the doctor. Whenever she was shy, or scared, or
both, she'd put one foot on top of the other and stand like that, crooked,
waiting for the world to decide what it was going to do with her. I'd say:
*both feet on the floor, sweetheart, or you'll fall*. And she never fell.

"Ciça," I say.

The lady opens her mouth. Nothing comes out.

Her eyes fill, all at once, and the water runs down the thousand lines of her
face without finding a straight path, spreading out, like a city that didn't
think about every drop. Her foot slides off the other one. The mesh screams.
And her knees give way inside the silver thing, and I see that she's going to
fall, and the floor in this room isn't like the one in mine, or it is and I
don't trust it, and I cross the three steps between us faster than I've crossed
anything in my life.

I catch my daughter before the floor does.

---

She weighs nothing.

That's what I feel first, before anything else: that she doesn't weigh
anything. I hold her by the waist and the shoulders, and she fits in my arms the
way she fit at six, when she fell asleep in the car and I carried her upstairs.
Lighter than at six. All her bones are showing under the dress, like a structure
without cladding, and I feel her ribs in my palm, one by one, and her heart
beating fast behind them, too fast, like a little bird.

She cries without a sound. I don't know if I'm crying. I take her to the nearest
armchair, slowly, and sit her down, and kneel in front of her, and keep my hands
on her knees, where the silver thing hums softly, tired.

She looks down at me. For the first time since she was born, I'm the one looking
up at her.

"You looked at the ceiling," she says.

The voice is thin, scratchy, very quiet. It isn't Ciça's voice. It's the voice of
a lady of a hundred and fifteen. But the words are hers, and the way of saying
them, half accusing, half laughing, is hers, entirely hers, and I laugh, and out
comes the high laugh of the new throat, and she laughs too, a laugh that's
almost only air.

"There's a leak there," I say, and point to the corner, without taking my eyes
off her.

"I know. I complained."

"When?"

"Last week. When I came to see the room."

She came to see the room. She came, in the mesh, on legs that can't hold her, to
see the room where she was going to meet her mother, and she complained about
the leak. Because she knew I'd look.

I don't say anything. I have nothing to say. I stay on my knees, with my hands on
her knees, and she puts her hand on top of mine. It's the lightest hand, cold,
its skin thin as tissue paper, shaking a little. She looks at my hand under
hers. She looks at the side of my index finger.

"It's gone," she says.

"What is?"

"The line. From the rebar."

I never told her about the rebar. She was six. I don't know how she knows.
Maybe I did tell her, some night or other, and I don't remember; maybe someone
told her later, my sister, years later. Or maybe she looked at that line on my
hand so many times that she ended up knowing what it was, the way children know
things.

"It's all gone," I say. "This body is two years old."

She nods slowly, like someone who already knew and needed to hear it.

"And I'm a hundred and fifteen."

"I know. I did the arithmetic."

"Twice?"

I laugh again. I cry again. There's no difference, the new throat can't tell the
two apart.

"Twice."

---

We leave together at five, before the rain.

She goes in a chair that moves on its own, that slides down the clinic corridor
without a sound and keeps to the speed of my steps, and I walk beside it, with
my hand on her shoulder, which is bone. Ravi goes with us as far as the transit
platform and stops there. He doesn't get in. He takes a step back when the
capsule opens its doors, and I understand that this is where his job ends, and I
turn to say something, and I don't know what.

"Thank you," I say.

He nods. He looks at Ciça, and then at me, and there's that thing on his face
again that I don't know how to read, the same as on the first day, on the other
side of the glass.

"Look after her," he says.

And the door closes before I can ask whether he said *look after her* to me or
to her.

---

In the transit, her chair locks itself to the floor, and I sit on the bench
beside it, and the capsule moves at the height of the building windows, and for
a while neither of us says anything.

I have a hundred and nine years of questions. I don't know which to start with.
They all seem too big for a capsule.

"Did your aunt look after you?" I ask, finally.

"She did."

"Was she good to you?"

Ciça takes a while. She looks out the window, where a kitchen goes by with a
family having dinner, a little boy standing on his chair.

"She was. She didn't know how to do braids. But she was."

I did braids. Two, really tight, every morning before school, with Ciça
complaining that it pulled. I think of my sister, who had two boys and never
needed to learn, trying to braid the hair of a six-year-old girl who cried
because it wasn't the same.

"And after that? Did you study? Get married? Do what?"

"I'll tell you later."

"Tell me a piece."

She turns her face to me. The pale eyes are tired, and there's something in
them I can't read, and that bothers me, because she's the second person today
whose face I can't read, and the first was a stranger.

"I studied music," she says. "I taught for a long time. I married a good man
named Tomás, who died forty years ago. I had a daughter."

"A daughter."

"Lívia."

I repeat the name, quietly. Lívia. A granddaughter. My granddaughter. A girl who
was born, grew up, had her whole life, and whom I never saw, and her name reaches
me now, in a capsule, a hundred-and-some years later, like a letter that got
stuck in the mail.

"Is she...?"

"She died nine years ago."

Ciça says this looking straight ahead, her voice flat, and I understand, from
the flat voice, that it was the worst thing. That of everything that happened to
her in a hundred and nine years, without me, that was the worst. I don't ask
what of. I put my hand on her shoulder, which is bone, and she lets me.

"Irene is her daughter," says Ciça. "You'll meet her. She's going to think you're
very young."

"I am very young."

"I know." She almost smiles. "I think so too."

We don't talk for the rest of the ride. She closes her eyes and I keep looking at
her face, up close, with the light from the windows going by, and I search for
the little girl again, and now I find pieces. The curve of the ear. The way she
presses her lips together when she sleeps. A small mole, nearly faded, at the
corner of her left eye, which I'd forgotten she had and which I used to kiss
when she cried.

I don't ask anything else. I have the feeling she's keeping something back, and
that if I ask one more time, she'll have to choose between telling and lying.
And I don't want her to have to choose anything today.

---

The apartment is in Ostrel, on the twenty-second floor, and it has a whole wall
of window, and the rain is already falling when we arrive, in weight, straight
down, onto the city that walks up top.

Ciça goes straight to an armchair by the window, an old armchair, worn on the
arms, that doesn't move when she sits, and closes her eyes, and I see that she's
at the end of her strength. A calm voice, a woman's, coming from a point in the
middle of the room, says *good afternoon, Mrs. Cecília*, and then *good
afternoon, Ms. Helena*, and I jump and look for where it's coming from, and Ciça,
without opening her eyes, says:

"It's the house. Don't mind it."

"The house knows my name."

"The house knows everybody's name." She opens one eye only, and it's so much
her, so much the six-year-old Ciça pretending to be asleep, that I have to sit
down on the arm of the chair. "Your room doesn't."

My room is the door on the left.

I go in alone. Ciça stayed in the armchair; she said she didn't need to show me,
that I'd understand. It's a small room, with a low wooden bed, the kind that
doesn't do anything. A white sheet, which I touch, and it's cotton. A window that
is only a window, with the rain outside. A chair. A wardrobe with its doors open
and nothing inside, waiting.

I speak out loud, to test it.

"Good night."

Nobody answers. The room doesn't answer. The room doesn't know who I am.

It's the first place since I woke up that doesn't know my name, and I sit on the
edge of the wooden bed and cry with relief, quietly, so she won't hear on the
other side of the wall.

Then I lie down. The sheet is rough and cold and smells of storage. I look at
the ceiling, because I'm an engineer and the ceiling is what I look at when I
walk into a place.

It's an old ceiling, from before the city thought about every drop. It's made of
panels. It has joints.

I count the joints until I fall asleep.

On the other side of the wall, my daughter breathes. I hear, through the thin
wall, her breathing, short, tired, with a slight wheeze at the end of each
breath, the breathing of someone very old who is asleep sitting up in an
armchair. And I think, before I fall asleep, something I hadn't thought until
now, something simple, that I don't know where it comes from and that seems to me
the most natural thing in the world:

She needs someone.

I'm going to look after her.
