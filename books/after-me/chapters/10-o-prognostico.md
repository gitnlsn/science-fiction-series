Cecília woke at three in the morning and her mother was holding her hand.

Not awake. Her mother was asleep sitting in a chair beside the bed, her head
fallen to one side, against the chair back, her mouth slightly open, and her arm
stretched over the rail, and her hand closed around Cecília's. It wasn't
squeezing. It was just there, around it, the way a hand stays after the person
has fallen asleep holding on.

Cecília didn't move.

She was lying on her back in a bed that wasn't hers, in a room that wasn't hers,
with a low light coming from the walls and a smell of nothing, the too-clean smell
of hospitals, which had changed little in a hundred years. Her chest felt heavy,
as if someone had left a big book on it. She felt a thread of something going
into her left arm, without a needle, through the skin. Her throat was dry.

And she felt her mother's hand.

It was a hand of new skin, unmarked, soft in the palm in a way no adult hand
should be, with calluses only beginning to show, after three months on sites, at
the base of the fingers. It was warm. Cecília lay in the dark, not moving,
feeling that warm hand around hers, and thought that she had imagined this many
times, on the nights in the armchair, and had imagined it wrong. She had imagined
it would be like being a child again. It wasn't. It was something else, which
she didn't know. It was being old and being held.

She stayed that way until it got light, afraid to move her hand in case her
mother woke up and let go.

---

She remembered Lívia.

She didn't want to. The memory came on its own, the way they came now, without
asking, so sharp that sometimes Cecília didn't know what year she was in. A room
like this one, nine years before. A light like this one. Lívia in the bed, at
eighty-one, her face shrunken, the wide mouth still wide, and Cecília in the
chair beside her, at a hundred and six, holding her daughter's hand. Not
sleeping. Cecília had never been able to sleep in those chairs. She stayed awake
the whole night, watching, and counting her daughter's breaths, as if counting
would stop them from stopping.

They had stopped anyway. In the small hours, like now, a little after four,
without a sound, between one count and the next.

And now it was Cecília in the bed, and in the chair was her mother, who was
thirty-eight and slept with her mouth open, and the order of things was so turned
around that there was no word for it. Cecília thought that maybe that was why she
had brought her mother back: not only to be looked after. So that there would be
someone in the chair who wasn't her. So that, at least once, before the end, the
person awake all night counting someone's breaths would be somebody else.

But her mother wasn't awake counting. Her mother was asleep. She had fallen
asleep holding her hand, the way a child falls asleep holding the hand of the
person telling the story.

Cecília smiled in the dark. She thought it was better that way.

---

Dr. Sabino arrived at seven.

He was Irene's age and had looked after Cecília for thirty years, and he came
into the room without making a sound, and stopped in the doorway when he saw her
mother asleep in the chair. He looked at her for a while. Then he looked at
Cecília, who was awake, and Cecília saw in his eyes the same thing she saw in the
eyes of everyone who saw her mother for the first time: the arithmetic.
Thirty-eight and a hundred and fifteen. The mother and the daughter. The sum that
doesn't close.

"She spent the night there?" he asked, quietly.

"She did."

"She came in the ambulance with you. She wouldn't let go." He smiled, tired.
"The nurses gave up asking."

He sat on the other side of the bed, away from her mother, on the edge of the
chair, and spoke quietly so as not to wake her.

He didn't use technical names. He never did. He said the cascade had sped up.
That the things that used to stop responding one at a time, over months, were
now stopping together, over weeks. That her heart had held out that night because
the house was quick and the team got there in four minutes, and that next time it
might not hold, and that there would be a next time.

"How long?" asked Cecília.

It was the same question as two years before. She noticed that as she asked it
and almost laughed.

"Six months," said Sabino. "Maybe less. Your head clear until near the end, as I
said last time. That hasn't changed." He hesitated. "The body is going faster
than we expected."

"The body's already gone, Doctor."

"You said that last time too."

"It's still true."

He didn't laugh. She liked him for that, and she was quiet for a while, looking
at the hospital room ceiling, which was smooth, without joints, a single piece,
with the light inside it. She thought her mother would hate that ceiling.

Six months.

She did the arithmetic, because she had learned from her mother to do it. Six
months was the end of the rainy season that hadn't started yet. It was her mother
in the middle of the first year of the five in Corvel. It was her mother with
three hundred and eleven buildings ahead of her.

"Doctor."

"Yes."

"She can't know."

Sabino looked at her mother, who was asleep. Then he looked at Cecília.

"I can't tell her, Mrs. Cecília. Even if I wanted to. She's your dependent. She
isn't a responsible party." He said the word *dependent* carefully, as if it
weighed something. "The law doesn't let me discuss your condition with her
without your authorization."

"I know. I'm asking you not to authorize anything I don't authorize. That nobody
here tell her. No nurse, no doctor on a wall. That nobody answer if she asks."

"She'll ask."

"I know."

"And what will you answer?"

Cecília looked at her mother's hand, closed around hers.

"Something," she said.

Sabino was quiet. Then he nodded, slowly, and stood, and at the door he stopped,
with his hand on the frame.

"Irene already knows," he said. "She's the registered responsible party. She got
everything last night. And she asked the clinic for a copy of the restitution
contract. The hospital needed the sponsor's document and she asked for all of it
together."

Cecília felt her chest get heavier, as if someone had put another book on top of
the first.

"All of it together," she repeated.

"All of it together." Sabino looked at her with something like pity. "I thought
you should know."

---

Her mother woke at eight, suddenly, the way she woke now, with a start, as if she
had fallen asleep on duty.

She let go of the hand. Cecília felt the lack of it right away, as if someone had
taken off a blanket.

"You're awake," her mother said. "How long have you been awake?"

"A little while."

"Why didn't you call me?"

"You were asleep."

Her mother rubbed her face with both hands. She had marks from the chair on her
cheek, red lines, the first marks Cecília had seen on that face, and they would be
gone in an hour. Her eyes were puffy. There was still concrete dust in her hair,
from yesterday, from Tessel, from before the guitar.

"Did the doctor come?" she asked.

"He did."

"And what did he say?"

Cecília had been thinking about it for an hour. She had thought of several
sentences, and all of them were lies, and she didn't like any of them. In the
end, she chose the one that was least a lie.

"That I'm old, Mom. That that's what I've got."

Her mother looked at her. Cecília saw, in her face, the engineer: the woman who
read the faces of foremen hiding problems. She saw her mother read hers. And she
saw, with a pang, that her mother couldn't read it. That too much time had
passed. That Cecília's face, at a hundred and fifteen, was a building full of
files that wouldn't open.

"Has this happened before?" her mother asked. "Yesterday. Had that happened
before?"

"A few times."

"How many?"

"A few."

"Ciça."

"Mom." Cecília put her hand over her mother's, on the bed rail. "I'm a hundred and
fifteen. Things happen to your body at a hundred and fifteen. They happen every
day. I'm not going to tell you all of them, because then you won't do anything
else, and I want you to do things."

"I want to stay here."

"I know. And I want you to go."

"Go where?"

"To Corvel. To Tessel. To the three hundred and twelve."

Her mother laughed, a laugh with no fun in it at all.

"You nearly died yesterday."

"Nearly." Cecília squeezed her hand, with what little strength she had. "Mom.
Listen to me. You didn't come back to sit in a hospital chair."

She said it and heard what she'd said, and felt her face go hot, because it was
exactly the opposite of the truth. Her mother had come back for exactly that. For
that chair, for that hand, for that night. Cecília had brought her back for
that, and had written it in the contract, and now she was sending her mother away
from the chair, and she didn't know why.

Or she did. She had known since the night in the armchair: *the hand on the
forehead only counts when nobody asked*. And she didn't want her mother to stay in
the chair because the doctor had said six months. She wanted her mother to stay
because she wanted to. And if her mother didn't know about the six months, and
stayed anyway, then it was because she wanted to.

It was a way of lying so complicated that she lost herself in it.

Her mother looked at her for a while. Then she stood up, and bent down, and kissed
her daughter on the forehead. Quickly. Without asking.

"I'll go to Tessel in the afternoon," she said. "In the morning I stay. That's
not negotiable."

"Okay."

"And at night I come back here."

"Okay."

"And you call me. If anything happens. Anything, Ciça."

"Okay."

Her mother looked down at her, suspicious, with the marks of the chair on her
face.

"You're saying *okay* to everything."

"I'm tired," said Cecília. And it was true.

---

Irene arrived at noon, when her mother had gone down to get food, and closed the
door of the room behind her, and stood leaning against it.

She didn't say hello. She had the face of someone who hadn't slept, and
yesterday's clothes, and something in her hands that she held the way she had
held the succulent in the living room doorway, in front of her body: a sheet. A
sheet of paper. Printed.

"I asked for it to be printed," Irene said. "I don't know why. I think I wanted
to hold it."

Cecília didn't need to ask what it was.

"You read it."

"I read it." Irene took two steps, stopped at the foot of the bed.
"*End-of-life companionship*. That's all. You didn't tick anything else." Her
voice shook, and she steadied it. "Grandma. I thought it was vanity. I thought it
was... I don't know. Madness. Something very old people do. I shouted at you in
the kitchen. I said *doll*."

"I remember."

"You brought her back to look after you until you die."

It wasn't a question. Cecília didn't answer. She looked at the sheet in her
granddaughter's hand, the white paper, the small print, the clause she had chosen
listening to a woman's voice read it aloud, six months before.

"And she doesn't know," said Irene.

"No."

"Grandma, she slept in that chair all night." Irene pointed at the empty chair,
with the sheet. "The nurses told me. She wouldn't let go of your hand in the
ambulance. She shouted at the house. She doesn't know anything and she's doing
exactly what you bought."

"I know."

"She deserves to know."

"She will know."

"When?"

Cecília closed her eyes. Her chest was heavy. The thread of something went into
her arm, without a needle, and she could taste it in her mouth, metallic,
muffled.

"When I have the courage," she said.

She heard Irene breathe, at the foot of the bed. A long time. Then she heard the
paper being folded, once, twice.

"I'm not promising anything, Grandma," said Irene.

Cecília opened her eyes. Her granddaughter was putting the folded sheet in her
coat pocket. She had Lívia's creased forehead, and her great-grandmother's wide
mouth, and something new, which Cecília had never seen in her and which looked a
lot like what her mother had on her face when she was deciding on a structure:
the face of someone who is going to do what she thinks is right, and pay for it
later.

"I know you're not," said Cecília.

The door opened. Her mother came in with two trays and her dusty hair and stopped
when she saw Irene.

"Hi," her mother said. "I brought food for two. Now it'll have to stretch to
three."

"I already ate," said Irene, who hadn't.

And she left, with the folded sheet in her pocket, without looking at her
grandmother.
