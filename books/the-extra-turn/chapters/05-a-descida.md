Going down is worse than going up.

Every technician knows it and nobody says it. Going up, you get lighter. Every
hour takes something off you: a thousand kilometers, and you weigh a quarter less;
five thousand, and you weigh a third of what you weighed; ten thousand, and the body starts to
forget it ever had weight. It's a good thing, going up. It's like putting down one backpack at a
time.

Going down, the backpacks come back. One at a time. And you know exactly when,
because the panel shows it, in the corner, in small type: *apparent mass*.

At seven thirty on Monday night, the maintenance car lets go of the Post's collar
and starts down, and the panel says *2.9 kg*. Otávio is behind me, in the
cargo compartment, inside a gray thermal bag that Inês brought up from
below and that I zipped shut by myself, because she offered to help and I said no.
He weighs three kilos. I weigh three kilos. We're two light packages in a box falling
slowly down the ribbon toward the sea.

Álvaro stayed at the Post's kitchen window when the car let go. I watched his
face go up and out of sight. He raised his hand. I raised mine.

---

For the first few hours, I don't feel anything.

That's normal. Between twenty thousand and fifteen thousand kilometers the weight hardly changes for
someone inside: from four percent to six, to eight. The body doesn't notice.
The head notices only because it knows. I sit in the front seat, my hands
in my lap, watching the ribbon come out from under the car and vanish up above, and then
watching the Earth, which grows in the window so slowly that I can only tell it has grown
if I stop looking for an hour and look again.

I try the file again. *Otávio Reis — suit 4471. Delivered late. File
damaged.* Forty-one seconds. *Unable to open.* The wrist doesn't know how to
fix it. The Post didn't know. Maybe the Station knows, up there, where they have the
comms lab, the machines that recover what comes in broken from the
satellites. But I'm going down, and the Station is sixteen thousand kilometers
up, and my access is suspended.

I keep the file. I don't delete it. I'm never going to delete it.

And then I remember there's something else on his wrist, on my wrist, that I never
opened. Not the file. The call.

---

Monday, the third, two weeks ago. Twenty-three fourteen. A
missed call, *Otávio*, and underneath, small, an icon I had seen at the time
and ignored, because I was dead tired and because he left a message every time
I didn't answer, and his messages were always long, always about something
on the cable, always ending with *call me when you can*.

A message. Nine seconds.

Nine seconds. He never left a nine-second message in his life.

I look at the icon for a long time. Outside, the ribbon comes out from under the car and rises. The
panel says *3.8 kg*.

I touch it.

First, wind. A strong wind, beating on the microphone and making that sound of
crumpled paper. Then, under the wind, the sea: the sound of the waves hitting the
hulls of the Anchor, which I know the way I know my own breathing,
because I've slept with it my whole life. He was on deck. On the outer edge, where
it's windy.

And then, in the middle of the wind, his voice. Close. Low.

"Iara."

That's all. My name. And then a breath, just one, like someone who was going to say something
more and changed his mind, or someone who was choosing how to say it.

And the wind. And the sea. And it ends.

I play it again. *Iara.* The breath. The wind.

Again. *Iara.*

I keep listening to my name in his voice until I lose count. I don't know what he was going to
say. I'm not going to know. I know he was on the outer deck, close to midnight,
on a Monday, with wind, and that he thought of me, and that he called, and that I saw his
name and thought *tomorrow*, and turned over and slept, and that he said my
name into a device, once, and gave up.

---

On the first night, the car passes sixteen thousand kilometers.

I don't sleep. I stay in the seat with my head against the cold window, watching the Earth,
which is now a little bigger than an open hand, and almost entirely in darkness,
with the Sun on the other side. You can see the lights. A few, yellow, in patches, along
coasts I've never visited. None on the sea. The Anchor is there, somewhere
in that darkness, without a single light I could see from here, with nine thousand people
sleeping and Bia on the cargo deck on the night shift and my bed empty.

I think about what he used to say about the Station.

The first time I went all the way up there, to geostationary, I was twenty-four,
and I floated the whole first day from one side of the central module to the other,
laughing like a child, bumping into the walls, because there's no weight at all there, not
four percent, not one, nothing. And Otávio watched me, leaning on a
handhold, with his usual apple, until I got tired. Then he pulled me by the sleeve to the
big window, the one that looks down, at the whole Earth, round, hanging in the
black.

"You think we're standing still here," he said.

"We are standing still. It's geostationary. We stay over the same place."

"We're falling."

I laughed.

"I'm serious." He took a bite of the apple. "Everything that's in orbit
is falling. The Station, the satellites, the Moon. The Earth pulls, and we fall
toward it. Only we're moving sideways so fast that, while we
fall, the Earth keeps curving away underneath, and we miss. Fall, and miss. Fall, and miss. All the
time. Forever." He pointed at the Earth with the apple. "*An orbit is a
fall that never ends*, kid. That's all it is. There's nothing up there that's
standing still. Everything is falling and missing the ground."

"Then why don't we feel it?"

"Because we're falling along with it." He smiled. "When everything falls along with you,
falling feels like floating."

I thought it was beautiful. I wrote it down in my notebook, the way I wrote down everything he said back
then, in the round handwriting of someone who still writes things down. I didn't think about it again.

Now I do. With my head against a cold window, at sixteen thousand kilometers,
in a car that isn't in any orbit, that isn't falling and missing, that's
only coming down, fastened to the ribbon, slowly, toward the ground it isn't going to miss.
With a three-kilo man in a bag behind me, who isn't falling
along with anyone anymore either.

The panel says *4.6 kg*.

---

In the small hours of Wednesday, ten thousand kilometers. Fourteen percent. Nine
point three kilos.

This is where the body starts to know. I get up from the seat to go to the cargo
compartment and, for the first time since the Anchor, my feet stay on the floor by themselves, without my
thinking about them. The cup of water I let go of doesn't hang in the air thinking anymore: it falls, still
slowly, but it really falls, and it makes a noise when it hits.

I open the cargo compartment. The gray bag is held with two straps to the floor,
the way I strapped it. I put my hand on it. I don't open it.

"We're nearly there," I say.

It's a lie. There's more than a day to go. But it's what you say.

I go back to the seat. My legs are a little heavier. I notice. I don't want to notice.

---

On the second night, five thousand. Thirty percent. Twenty kilos.

I try to sleep in the reclined seat and can't, because now the body weighs enough
to hurt in the places where it rests. The shoulders. The hips. The back of the neck. Eleven days of
almost nothing and the body has forgotten how to carry itself.

And grief comes back that way too. I didn't know that. Nobody told me it was like that,
that it would come back along with the weight, kilo by kilo. Up there, at the Post, I
felt everything and felt nothing, because everything weighed three kilos, even that. I'd sit
next to him in the cold room and not cry. I thought it was strength. It wasn't. It was the
height.

Here, at five thousand kilometers, at twenty kilos, I cry. For the first time.
Curled up on the seat, with my hands over my face, making a noise I didn't know
I made, until my nose is blocked and my throat hurts. The panel says *20.4 kg* and I cry
the whole twenty kilos.

---

On Thursday morning, a thousand kilometers. Seventy-four percent.

Now it hurts to stand. My knees complain. My head is heavy on my neck, like
something someone put there and forgot. The sky outside has stopped being black: it's
turning dark blue at the edges, because we're entering the end of the air, where
there's no air yet but already the beginning of the color. Through the lower window I see the sea.
Not a color: the sea. With clouds over it. With the shadows of the clouds on the water.

And in the middle of the sea, small, very small, a pale spot, round, with a
thin line coming out of the center and rising toward me.

The Anchor. The ribbon coming out of the middle of it, like a thread coming out of the center of a coin.

The panel says *49 kg*.

---

The car enters the Well at ten past two on Thursday afternoon.

The Well is the hole in the center of the Anchor that the ribbon rises from, a deep cavity,
round, surrounded by decks in rings, where the cars arrive and leave. It smells
of sea and oil and hot metal. When the hatch opens, the heat comes in all at
once, the heat of the Anchor, humid, heavy, that sticks to the skin like a wet cloth, and I
hadn't felt that heat in eleven days.

The panel says *66 kg*.

I try to get up from the seat.

I can't. I mean, I can, but my legs don't obey the way they
should, as if they'd gone numb, and I stand gripping the back of the seat,
shaking, with my whole body weighing sixty-six kilos all at once, after
eleven days of weighing almost nothing. The helmet, which I'm carrying under my arm, weighs like
a stone. The air weighs. The light weighs.

At the edge of the Well, on the arrivals deck, there are people waiting. Two men from the
agency, in gray, with a stretcher for the bag. An Operator doctor, whom I
know by sight. And, a little apart from the others, in the orange coveralls of the cargo
deck, her hair tied back any old way and her hands stuffed in her pockets,
Bia.

She sees me in the hatch and takes her hands out of her pockets.

I take a step out of the car, onto the deck, and my left knee gives, and I
only don't fall because I grab the edge of the hatch, and I stay there, half hanging,
with the helmet banging on the floor.

Bia runs across the deck. She doesn't hold me up: she kneels in front of me and
grabs the helmet, which was about to roll into the Well, and then looks up at me from below, with
a face I can't read, the face of someone who wants to say something and can't.

"You weigh," she says. I don't know if it's a question.

"Sixty-six," I say.

Behind me, the two men in gray take the gray bag out of the cargo
compartment. It takes both of them, now. One on each side. Seventy kilos.

He's gone back to weighing what he weighed.

I stand on the deck of the Well, with Bia's hand on my arm and the heat sticking
to my skin, and I feel every kilo, mine and his, and I can't walk, and I don't want
anyone to carry me, and I don't know how I'm going to carry all of this alone
down here, at one hundred percent, where nothing falls slowly.
