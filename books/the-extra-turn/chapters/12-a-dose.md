At four forty-one on Tuesday morning, C-114's wheel ring passes over
him.

Not him. The two turns of rope. I know that's what it is before the screen confirms it,
because I'm awake and waiting, with my hand open on the wall of the niche, at
four percent, weighing two point nine kilos, and I feel it: a jolt. Small.
Half a second. The ring gripping the ribbon from both sides and finding, in the middle of the
smooth face, something that shouldn't be there, a thickness of rope flattened by
two months of wheels going over it, and climbing over it, and coming down the
other side.

The screen says *20,000 km. Post 20: 180 m above.*

I close my hand against the wall.

He's a hundred and eighty meters below an empty Post. Not him: the place. The two
turns of rope, which I left when I took the knot. The place he marked with what he
had. And C-114, with thirteen tons of disks in its cargo body and a woman
hidden in a niche the size of a wardrobe, has just passed over it, and I
felt it, and no one else in the world did.

The Post goes by. A hundred and eighty meters. Two seconds. The position lights,
red and green, which I can't see. The bottom bunk, on the left. The empty cold
room. The paper taped up with its pencil words: *tension, not weight*.

And then it's just ribbon again.

---

Bia sent the cable's song at two in the morning, and I spent the next two and a half hours
with it on the screen.

Not the whole song. One night's. The night from Thursday to Friday, from the sixth to the
seventh, on the stretch between nineteen thousand nine hundred and twenty thousand one hundred. What the
robots kept and nobody listened to.

"They record almost everything. An hour here, another there," Otávio told me, at the anchor ring, with his hand on the warm
metal. "But they only warn about what someone told them to warn about."

It was stored. Bia found it through the deck access, in the same archive where she found the
report, in a folder nobody has opened since it was created. She sent it in pieces, at the
ends of the lines of numbers, over two hours, and I put it together.

It's a line. A thin line that rises and falls on the screen, with the time underneath, like the
trace of a heart in a hospital. When the ribbon is quiet, the line is almost
straight, with a tiny tremor, the wind down below, the breathing of the sea on the hulls of the
Anchor, twenty thousand kilometers down, arriving up here through the ribbon the way the voice
of someone talking on the other side of a wall arrives. When something touches the ribbon, the
line changes.

And I learned to read it. At twenty-two, with my eyes closed, with my hand on the ring.
He taught me.

---

At twenty-two oh-four on Thursday, the line changes for the first time.

Not much. A new tremor, finer, higher, that starts on top of the usual tremor and
stays. I know it. It's the song of a person on the ribbon. A technician
outside, held by a lifeline, moving hand over hand. It's the lightest sound
the cable knows how to make. Seventy kilos at four percent, hanging from a thread. It's
almost nothing.

At twenty-two oh-four his dosimeter reared up. At twenty-two oh-four the ribbon
began to sing Otávio.

I spend a long time on that fine tremor. It's the last thing I have
of him that isn't ice, or paper, or wind. Him moving. Him working. A hundred and
eighty meters below the Post, measuring, with the handheld reader, his glove on the edge.

The fine tremor continues. Twenty-three hundred. Twenty-three fifteen. Twenty-three
thirty.

At twenty-three thirty-seven, there's a spike.

---

It's a straight stroke on the screen. It goes up and down in a fraction of a second, far above everything
that comes before and after, and leaves behind it a tremor that dies slowly,
in smaller and smaller waves, like a bell someone struck once and let go.

I know that too. Every technician knows it. It's the song of an impact.

Something hit the ribbon, or near it, hard, all at once. It could have been
dust: a grain of something, out of the millions that cross the cable every day, bigger
than the others, faster, and the ribbon sang the impact. It happens. I've seen that straight
stroke in the logs a thousand times. It could have been a tool falling from a hand
and hitting the edge. It could have been the handheld reader itself slipping and bouncing back on its
strap. It could have been the lifeline going taut all at once, if a seventy-kilo man
at four percent lost his grip and fell the length of the line and was
stopped by it at the end.

The line doesn't say what. The line says when.

Twenty-three thirty-seven.

After the spike, the fine tremor comes back. Weaker. Different, in a way I can't
say: him moving less, or some other way, or me wanting it to be different. It comes back, and continues, weaker.

Until twenty-three fifty-two. After that, the robots on that stretch stop
recording.

---

An hour here, another there. The routine robots don't record all the time on every
stretch: there isn't the battery for it, nor the bandwidth to send twenty years of
song down through the belt, which eats the buffers. That night, on that stretch, the round ended at
twenty-three fifty-two, and the next one started at two in the morning.

Two hours and eight minutes of silence. Not from the ribbon. From the log.

And at two in the morning, when the recording comes back, the fine tremor isn't there anymore.

There's something else in its place. Something I recognize with my eyes closed, because
Otávio put my hand on the warm metal and said *feel it now*. The low song. The one that comes
and goes, comes and goes, like slow breathing. A heavy cargo climber's,
going by, with its ring gripping the ribbon and pulling sideways, late, like every climber
going up.

Not going by. Gone by. What the line shows at two in the morning is the end of the song, the
low note moving upward, moving off, going away toward the Station. The
climber passed through the stretch at some point in the two hours and eight minutes when
nobody was recording. And at two, when the recording comes back, it's already far away, and the man
isn't singing anymore.

---

I do the math, because it's what I know how to do.

C-X9 left the Well at six forty on Tuesday. The extra. The one Bia loaded on
Monday night. Three hundred kilometers an hour, like all of them, unless it
stopped, or slowed down, and an off-schedule extra doesn't tell anyone if it stopped. At
three hundred an hour, to twenty thousand, sixty-six hours and forty minutes.
It arrives at one twenty on Friday.

One twenty. Inside the two hours of silence.

And car M-3, with my code, left at twelve past one, five and a half hours earlier, and
reached the Post on Thursday night. Otávio went up first. The extra went up behind,
on the same ribbon, in the same direction. With distance. Without knowing he was there, because
an extra isn't on the schedule and doesn't look at the schedule, and because Otávio, with my code
on an emergency departure, wasn't on anybody's schedule either.

At one twenty, a cargo climber full of disks, which didn't exist, passed through the
stretch just below the Post, where, at twenty-three fifty-two, a man was still
singing.

I don't know if he was still alive at one twenty. The dosimeter doesn't know whether the person is
alive. The line didn't record it. The suit's reserve gave minutes, and the tear could have been at
twenty-three thirty-seven, with the spike, or after, or before.

What it was, I don't know. C-X9's wheel ring, which may have found his lifeline on the
face of the ribbon and rubbed it until it frayed, the way it found the two turns of rope
today and went over them. The dust, at twenty-three thirty-seven. The edge of the
ribbon, roughened, with its fibers loose like the hair on an arm. The heart of
a sixty-four-year-old man with thirty-one years of dose in a trunk of crystal
strip.

I know there was someone else on the cable.

And that nobody knows when.

---

*The code*, I write to Bia, at four fifty, when C-114 is already at
twenty thousand and forty-five and I already weigh less than Otávio weighed here.

The answer takes a while.

*M-3 was him. His fingerprint is on the car's panel, in the touch log. The
agency already knows. Inês asked me yesterday if I knew anything about C-X9.*

*And you?*

*I said yes.* A line of numbers. Another. *I told her everything. The extras, the man
who pays, the three nights. She wrote it down without changing her face. She said I did the right thing. I'm going to
lose my job, Iara.*

I keep looking at the screen.

*My mother's going to lose her cooler*, Bia writes.

And then:

*But he tied himself on for you to find. You did. I wasn't going to be the person who hid the
rest.*

The next line isn't Bia's.

*This is Inês Garrido. Bia gave me the channel. You're in C-114's niche, and I'm not
going to have the climber stopped.*

I take my hand off the keyboard. Put it back.

*And the man who pays for the extras?*

*Gone. The account closed yesterday, and there's no one at the address.* A line of
numbers. *When you get up there, tell me what you know. What you think, keep for
later.*

---

Hugo didn't know. I think about that at five in the morning, at twenty thousand and ninety-five, with the
notebook on my knees and the line of the cable's song still lit on the screen.

He didn't know Otávio was there. Nobody knew. Hugo sent a fourth
climber a day, in the small hours, off-schedule, because the program was running late
and the heat doesn't wait and five old people were going to die in the still week anyway, and
because nobody had ever been outside on the ribbon at one twenty in the morning without
being on the schedule. Until that night.

Hugo may have killed Otávio without knowing Otávio was there. Or he may have had
nothing to do with anything, and the extra passed over an empty stretch, with a man already dead
tied on with an extra turn, singing nothing.

The line didn't record it.

I put my hand on the wall of the niche and feel the tremor of
C-114's ring in the bones of my hand, the way I felt it in the warm metal at twenty-two. And I think that
I have the cable's whole song for that night, every tremor, every spike, every low
note, recorded to the thousandth of a second, stored in a folder nobody
opened. All of it, except two hours and eight minutes.

He tied himself on so I'd find the place. He didn't leave word of how he was going to die. Maybe he didn't
know. Maybe, at twenty-three thirty-seven, he too only heard a bell
ring once and go away.

The screen says *20,600 km. 4.1%.* The niche climbs. The ribbon sings C-114 for whoever is
listening, and no one is, except me.
