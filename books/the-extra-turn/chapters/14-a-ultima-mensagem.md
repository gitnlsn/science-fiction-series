At the Station, nothing falls.

I've known that since I was twenty-four and I forget it every time, and I remember it the instant
the transfer yard's arm opens the lid of the niche and I try to get out
the way you get out of a place, pushing down with my hands, and there is no down. The
push carries me upward, on my back, slowly, out of the niche, and I hang
still in the air of the yard, turning a little, with the notebook pressed to my chest, and the
niche's screen, down below, which isn't below, says *0.0 kg*.

The transfer yard is the largest enclosed space there is above the ground. A
cylinder a hundred meters long, with the ribbon running through the middle from end to
end, and the cargo climbers docked against it, in a row, and the yard's arms, which
are long and white and have ten joints, taking the columns of disks out of the
cargo bodies and passing them to the belt that leads to the upper ribbon, the one that goes on
to the tip. All of it without any sound you can hear. All of it floating, fastened to something.
Except me.

A man sees me. He's twenty meters away, held by one foot in a strap on the wall,
checking a column with a reader. He stops. He stares at me with his mouth open, the
woman in a technician's suit coming out spinning from inside a niche nobody has opened
in twenty years. Then he says something into his wrist, quickly.

I don't move. I have nothing to push off from. I keep turning, slowly, with the notebook on my
chest, until someone comes.

---

The one who comes is the yard chief, whose name is Moisés and who has white eyebrows and
doesn't know what to do with me. He holds me by the sleeve with one hand, without force, the way you
hold a balloon, and with the other he talks into his wrist with someone I can't hear. He says *yes*
three times and *I don't know* twice. Then he looks at me.

"You're Iara. Otávio's."

Nobody had ever called me that before. *Otávio's.* I nod.

"The agency down below sent word you might turn up," he says. "The
investigator. She said, if you turned up, not to let you leave the Station and
to give you water." He hesitates. "And she said to let you do what you came to
do, as long as you didn't leave the Station. I didn't understand that part."

"I do."

"And Álvaro's here," he says. "He came up last week, for the program
review, with Hugo. They're in the meeting module. Want me to call them?"

"Not yet." I hold the notebook tighter. "Where's the comms lab?"

---

The comms lab is on the Station's outer ring, in a small room
full of screens and black boxes held to the walls with elastic straps, and it smells of hot
dust, which is the smell of every place with too many machines and too little air. It's the place
where broken things end up. Satellite messages that arrived in pieces.
Climber logs corrupted by radiation. Everything the belt ruins as it
passes through, and that someone needs to read.

The one in charge is a man of about seventy named Tomé who doesn't seem
surprised to see me, as if women came out of niches every week. He's floating
in front of three screens at once, his feet hooked under a bar, in
glasses, with a coffee cup held by an elastic strap to his wrist.

"Suit?" he asks, when I show him my wrist.

"Suit. Otávio Reis. Four four seven one. Delivered late. Damaged."

He looks at me over his glasses. Then he holds out his hand. I touch my wrist
to his.

"Forty-one seconds," he says. "Suit buffer. Spent six days in the belt,
waiting for a network." He makes a sound with his mouth, someone who's seen this before. "The belt
eats buffers. Keeps taking pieces. But it doesn't take everything. It never takes everything."

"Can you open it?"

"I don't know." He pulls the file up on one of the screens, which fills with lines of color,
blocks, black holes in the middle. "Give me an hour. Sit down." And then, realizing:
"I mean. Hook yourself onto something."

I hook my foot under a bar, the way he has, and stay there. An hour. He
works in silence, moving the blocks of color with his fingers, drinking coffee through the
straw in the cup, and sometimes he says *ah* to the screen, quietly, and sometimes he says *no*. I
watch the blocks. Some turn green. Others stay black.

I don't think about anything. I can't. I think about the nine seconds of wind and my name. I think
that in a little while I'm going to hear his voice again, or I'm not, and that I don't know which
of the two I can bear.

"Eighty-two percent," Tomé says, after an hour and ten. "The holes
stay as silence. There's nothing to be done with them." He takes off his glasses. "Want to listen
alone?"

I nod.

He unhooks his feet from the bar and floats out through the door, with the cup on his wrist, and
closes the door behind him without a sound.

---

I touch it.

First, his breathing. Not the breath from the wind message. Another one. Short, fast, loud,
inside a helmet, with the muffled sound a voice makes when it's trapped behind glass
close to the mouth. And, underneath, a thin beep, intermittent, that I know, that every
technician knows and prays never to hear in their own suit: the reserve alarm. *Beep.
Beep. Beep.*

And then he speaks.

"Iara."

My name. The same as in the wind, and different. With no wind at all. Close.

"It's Otávio." A breath. "You know it's Otávio. I know you know.
I'm saying it for me."

I press my foot against the bar until it hurts.

"Sorry about your code. I called you to ask." Silence: not his, the
file's, a hole, half a second. "...tired, and that's fine. It was supposed to be quick. I'd
go up, measure, come down, you'd never even know. Monday I'd give you the notebook." A
longer breath. "Didn't work out."

*Beep. Beep. Beep.*

"The stretch. A hundred and eighty. It's in the notebook, Bia has it. You'll see. You always
see, you just don't believe you saw. Believe it."

A hole. Long. Two seconds of nothing, without even the beep.

"...nobody's fault, kid. Don't go looking. It's the ribbon. It's tired.
We wore it out, everybody a little bit." He laughs, or tries to, and the laugh turns into a cough.
"Me included. Three thousand nights."

*Beep. Beep.*

"I tied on here. With the turn. You'll know. Tie it like you're coming back, remember?
I'm not coming back. But you're coming. It's the same thing."

The breathing gets slower. Not calmer. Slower.

"You're going to think it's your fault. Because you didn't answer." A pause. "It isn't. I didn't
want to wake you. I chose. Leave that with me."

A hole.

"...from here the Earth is..."

Another hole. The beep comes back on its own, with no voice. *Beep. Beep. Beep.*

"...the open hand. You remember? The first time. You did the math and showed it to me, so
proud, and I said..." The voice fades and comes back, lower. "...sure, but look."

A breath. One.

"Look, Iara."

And it ends. Not a hole. The end. Forty-one seconds. Eighty-two percent.

---

I don't say anything for a long time.

I stay hooked to the bar by my foot, floating in front of the screen, with the file stopped at the
end, and the lab humming around me, the black boxes, the straps, the hot
dust. I don't cry. I don't know why. I cried at five thousand kilometers, in Bia's
kitchen, on the floor of hull seven. Not here. Here, without weight, tears don't fall: they stay
on the eye, a bubble of water that keeps growing and blurs everything, and that you have to
wipe off with your hand.

I wipe it off with my hand.

He didn't say how. I listen again, and again, and the third time I pay attention
only to that, the way you pay attention to the cable's song with your hand on the warm metal, and he doesn't
say. He doesn't say *a stone hit me*. He doesn't say *a climber went by*. He doesn't say *I stopped
trying to get back*. He says *nobody's fault* and *don't go looking* and *it's the ribbon*.

Maybe he didn't know. Maybe it was too fast, or too slow, or in the
dark. Maybe he knew and chose not to say, the same way he chose not to
wake me. *Leave that with me.*

The holes in the file stay as silence. There's nothing to be done with them.

I play the last part again. *Look, Iara.*

And I look. I let go of the bar with my foot and push myself slowly to the lab's window, which
is small and round and faces down, and I stay with my forehead almost touching the glass.

The Earth is there. Whole. Round. Smaller than from the Post: from here, from
geostationary, it isn't an open hand anymore, it's a closed fist at arm's length,
a little more, blue and white, with its edge lit on one side and the rest in darkness, hanging in the black with
nothing holding it up. And, coming out of the middle of it, where there's a sea I can't see, a
line you can't see from here either, but that I know is there, rising straight to
this window, passing through a stretch just below an empty Post, with forty
tired centimeters and two flattened turns of rope.

*Sure, but look.*

"I'm looking," I say, to the glass.

---

Someone knocks on the door. It isn't Tomé.

The door opens, and the one in the corridor, holding the frame with a big mechanic's
hand, with his short gray hair and his red eyes, is Álvaro.

He looks at me. He looks at the screen, stopped at the end of the file. He looks at the black-covered notebook,
floating beside me, held by an elastic strap to my wrist.

"Moisés told me," he says. His voice is low. "That you came out of a niche."

"I did."

"From C-114."

"Yes."

"Five days." He closes his eyes. "Kid."

He doesn't say anything else. He doesn't need to. He stays at the door, big, holding the frame so as not
to float in, as if he were afraid of going into a place that's now mine and
his.

"Do you want to hear it?" I ask.

Álvaro opens his eyes.

I let the file go into the air, between the two of us. The screen shows *Otávio Reis — suit 4471 —
41 s — 82%*.

He comes in.
