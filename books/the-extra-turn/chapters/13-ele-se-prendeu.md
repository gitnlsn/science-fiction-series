The niche's screen says *0.7 kg*, and I let go of the pen from the emergency kit in midair and it
stays. It doesn't fall. I mean, it does: at one percent, everything still falls. But it falls so slowly
that I have time to think a whole sentence before it reaches the floor of the niche,
a meter below. I think the sentence. The pen arrives. I pick it up and let go again.

I've been in here four days. My body stopped complaining yesterday, when the
weight got too small to hurt. My head doesn't stop.

For the first time since the twelfth, I have nothing left to find out before
getting somewhere. The Station is six thousand kilometers away, nineteen hours. The notebook
is read. The report is found. The cable's song is on the screen, with the spike at twenty-three
thirty-seven and the hole of two hours and eight minutes. Bia told Inês.
There are no more folders to open.

So I do what Otávio taught me to do when the measurements run out. I put everything
on the bench and separate it.

---

Inês does that too. *What you know, and what you think.* I thought it was agency
method. It was technician's method. Otávio did it with old ribbon on the workshop bench
on the Anchor: on one side, what the reader measured; on the other, what we imagine
the reader measured. Never mix them.

I write on the back of the last page of the notebook, behind the drawing of the knot, with the pen
that doesn't fall, in two columns.

On the left side, what I know.

*He got the letter. He wrote "still." He did the math, wrote the report. Nobody read it.*

*He called me at 23:14 and I didn't answer. He went up at 01:12 with my code.*

*He left the Post at 22:04 on Thursday. He measured. At 23:37, an impact. At 23:52, he was still moving.*

*At 01:20, more or less, C-X9 passed through the stretch.*

*The suit tore from outside. Ten, fifteen minutes of reserve. The lifeline frayed.*

*He tied himself on at the edge, with the extra turn. He stayed there six days. I found him.*

*The suit kept forty-one seconds. The Post's network only reaches the hull and the
collars; it delivered when I carried him inside.*

On the right side, what I think.

I hold the pen still over the right side for a long time.

---

The first thing I think is the most likely, and it's Inês's.

At twenty-three thirty-seven, a pebble, out of the millions that cross the cable every
day, hits his suit, or the ribbon beside him, and the suit opens a hole two
centimeters long in the left thigh, with the edge turned in. The reserve opens. He has
minutes. He tries to get back to the Post, a hundred and eighty meters up, hand over hand;
the lifeline, rubbing on the roughened edge of the ribbon, gives way on the way, a hand's width from the
carabiner. He understands he isn't going to make it: without a line, one slip at the edge is a
fall that burns, and the message goes with him. And he uses the minutes he has left to tie himself
there, at the place he came to measure. The song gets weaker after the spike because he's
hurt, or cold, or out of air. At twenty-three fifty-two, he's still
moving. After that, not.

When C-X9 goes by, at one twenty, he's already dead, tied on with the extra turn, at the
edge, outside the wheel track. The ring goes over the two turns of rope and past him, with
a small jolt, like C-114's in the small hours yesterday, and climbs on.

It's a story that closes. I write beside it: *dust*.

---

The second thing I think is the one I didn't want to think, and I think it.

At midnight he's still alive. Working. Maybe the spike at twenty-three
thirty-seven was only the handheld reader slipping and bouncing back on its strap, or the
line going taut when he changed position. He keeps measuring. He doesn't know about any
extra. Nobody knows. One twenty in the morning, a hundred and eighty meters below an empty
Post, a man at the edge of the ribbon, held by his lifeline, his back to the ground, looking at
the reader. And a cargo climber that doesn't exist, full of disks, coming up from below
him at three hundred an hour, with no window, with no one inside, not knowing.

The ring grips the ribbon from both sides. It finds the lifeline stretched across the face.
It rubs, or pulls, or drags. The line gives. Something on the ring, a corner, a
clip, a sliver, scrapes the outside of the suit's left thigh. The climber goes by. He
stays, without a line, with a small hole and minutes of air, and the low song of C-X9
moving off upward.

And he ties himself on. With the extra turn.

It's a story that closes too. I write: *the extra*.

And I write underneath, smaller, because it's true and it hurts: *Hugo didn't know. Bia loaded it.
The extra was behind him because he went up with my code, off-schedule, where
nobody could see. And I didn't answer.*

---

The third thing I think, I take a long time to write.

*Still.*

The word in the margin of the letter from the medical service, in pencil, with the crooked dot on the i. Thirty-one
years on the cable. Three thousand nights at the Post. The last authorized climb by the tenth, and
after that, never again past a thousand kilometers. Never again the glove on the edge of the ribbon.
Never again the Earth the size of a hand.

He went up before the tenth. He knew it was the last one.

He was sixty-four with a trunk of dose too thick, and a sixty-four-year-old
heart, and he worked outside for almost two hours, alone, in the
belt, with a handheld reader. Hearts stop. Sometimes the suit tears afterward,
on anything, and nobody ever knows which came first.

And there's the other thing inside *still*, which I don't write in the column. Which I can't
write. That a man who knows it's the last climb of his life, and that nobody is going to read the
report, and that the ribbon he built with his own hands has four months, and
that he's going to spend the rest of his days on the decks of the Anchor watching it rise from the middle of the Well,
maybe, a hundred and eighty meters from the Post, with the reserve opening, didn't try to get
back as hard as he could have.

Maybe he spent the minutes on the knot instead of on the way back.

I don't write that. I write only: *himself*. And I cross it out. And I write it again, under the
line, because Otávio used to say that what you cross out is still something you
thought.

---

The fourth thing isn't a story. It's what's left over.

It's the weaker song after twenty-three thirty-seven, which I can't read. It's the
hole of two hours and eight minutes. It's the roughened edge of the ribbon, which could be cause
or consequence. It's the straight stroke of the spike, which says when and doesn't say what. It's everything
the line suggests and doesn't confirm. It could be a fifth thing I haven't thought of. It could be all
three together. It could be dust at twenty-three thirty-seven and the extra at one twenty
passing by a man who was already gone.

I write: *what the line didn't record*.

And I keep looking at the four entries on the right side. *Dust. The extra. Himself. What
the line didn't record.*

The evidence supports all four. Each one closes on its own. None closes better than the
others. I spend an hour trying to find the measurement that breaks the tie, the number that takes
one off the bench, and there isn't one. The suit tore from outside: all
four. The line frayed: all four. The reserve at zero: all four. The knot with the extra turn: all four.

I'm never going to know how he died.

I write that too, in a single line, under the two columns, across the page.

---

*Up there, nobody falls alone.*

Álvaro said it in the Post's kitchen: *every rookie heard it from Otávio on the first day,
and everyone thought it was about company.* And he went up alone. And I thought, until
now, that the saying had turned into an accusation: if nobody falls alone, who was there?

C-X9 was there, maybe, with no one inside. The dust was there. The edge of the ribbon was there,
tired. His heart was there. Or nothing was there, and it was only him, a hundred and eighty
meters from an empty Post.

The saying from the first day turns into a question, and the question fits in neither column.

---

The pen floats in front of me, falling. I don't catch it. I let it fall.

Outside, the Earth must be the size of an open hand with the fingers together. The
Station, eighteen hours away. The niche is cold; the heating was made for seven days
and I'm on the fifth, and it's saving itself.

I open the notebook to the last page he wrote. *Iara. Start with the stretch just
below the Post. A hundred and eighty meters. You'll see.* And the drawing of the knot, in pencil,
small, with the turn on the inside of the loop.

He didn't write *you'll know how I died*. He didn't write *it was the dust*, or *it was
the extra*, or *it was me*. He wrote *you'll see*, and tied the place with his own body,
and left the notebook with Bia for her to give me. He didn't know if Bia would give me the
notebook, or if the suit would keep anything in the belt. The body was the only message that
couldn't be lost. Everything he left points to the ribbon. Nothing points to him.

The screen says *30,400 km, 1.0%*. I close the notebook with the two columns inside and hold
it against my chest, and stay like that, climbing, with almost no weight at all, on my way to
a place where nothing falls.

The pen reaches the floor of the niche without a sound.
