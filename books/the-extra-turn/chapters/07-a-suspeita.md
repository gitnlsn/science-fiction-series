The robots don't find anything.

Álvaro sends me the message on the fifth day after the descent, from up there, from the
Post, where he and Inês stayed on rereading the stretch. He doesn't call. He writes. Álvaro
never writes, and he writes, and that's how I know he didn't want to hear my
voice when I read it.

*Kid. The robots reread the whole stretch, three times, with the fine reader. Eight
tenths on the modulus, forty centimeters, same as you saw. Within
tolerance. It hasn't grown. No crack, no flaw in the weave. The edge has
loose fiber, but the edge has loose fiber in three hundred places. I don't know what
he saw. Sorry. Coming down tomorrow.*

I read it three times. By the third, the word *sorry* doesn't mean anything anymore.

Within tolerance. It hasn't grown. He doesn't know what Otávio saw.

Neither do I.

---

Thirteen days after the descent, Inês sends for me.

She sends for me the way the agency does: a message on my wrist, at seven in the morning on a
Wednesday, with a time and a place and no question. *Nine o'clock. Deck one,
hull two, room 14.* Hull two is the Operator's administration hull, near the
Well, where the offices are that have real coolers. Room 14 is a room
the agency borrowed, small, with a table, two chairs and a round window
facing inward, toward the Well, from which you can see the ribbon rising.

Inês is sitting by the window. The gray coveralls, the sleeves rolled twice.
The device the size of a pack of cards on the table, switched off, or
looking switched off.

"Sit down, Iara."

I sit. The room's cooler is at twenty-five degrees, and after thirteen days at
twenty-nine in hull seven, the cold raises goosebumps on my arms.

"Two things," she says. "And then a question."

---

The first thing is a sheet. She turns the device's screen toward me, and the screen
grows in the air above the table, the size of a sheet of paper, and it's the boarding list for the supply
ship. The Thursday one. The seven a.m. sailing on the sixth.

A hundred and twelve passengers. The names in order. I read without meaning to, looking.

*Reis, Otávio — reserved — did not board.*

"He made the reservation three weeks before he died," Inês says. "The same day he got the letter
from the medical service. He reserved, and didn't board, and nobody noticed, because a passenger
who doesn't show up for the ship isn't anybody's problem. The ship sails anyway."

Three weeks. The same day as the letter. He got the letter, tore the edge crooked,
wrote *still* in the margin in pencil, and booked a passage he was never going to use, so
everyone would think he was at sea.

"Did you know about the letter?" Inês asks.

"I found it in his apartment."

"You found it after you broke the seal." Her face doesn't change.

"I did."

"Don't do it again."

It isn't a threat. It's how she says everything. I nod.

---

The second thing is another sheet.

The screen grows in the air again, and it's something I know by heart, because I've seen it
every day for twelve years: the Well's departure log. The lines for the cars and the
climbers, one under the other, with the time, the destination, the type, the code of whoever
authorized it.

Inês puts her finger on a line.

*4th, 01:12 — maintenance car M-3 — destination: Post 20 — emergency departure,
off-schedule — authorization: technician I.*

And my code. The nine characters of my access code, the ones I type
every time I need to open a hatch, release a car, enter the Well in the
middle of the night. Mine. There.

I keep looking.

"Tuesday, the fourth," Inês says. "Twelve past one in the morning. A maintenance
car left the Well off-schedule, as an emergency departure, which is the only
way to leave off-schedule, and which only a technician at your level can authorize.
With your code. It reached the Post on Thursday night. The Post logged the arrival.
It didn't log who was inside, because the Post doesn't ask."

"It wasn't me."

"What I know is that it wasn't you who went up." She says it quickly, and it's the
first time I've seen her say anything quickly. The screen changes in the air: the
turnstile on the cargo deck. "Your badge went through here at six in the morning that
Tuesday, and at eleven, and at two, and at eight at night. You worked fourteen hours. At
twelve past one you were asleep, or should have been."

She looks at my line. I read the one above it, without meaning to, the way I read the ship's.

*Monday, 23:58 — entry — technician B.* And no exit until five thirty.

At twelve past one, when the car left the Well with my code, Bia was on the
cargo deck, right up against the Well. I don't say anything.

"Then it was him."

"That's what I think." She turns off the screen. The sheet vanishes from the air. "Now the question."

---

"Did you give your code to Otávio?"

"No."

"Did he know your code?"

I think. I don't take long to answer, but I think, and she sees that I think.

"He knew it. He knew the code of everyone he trained. He said a trainer has
to know it, in case the trainee passes out outside and someone needs to open
the hatch for them. Nobody ever changed their code because of that. Nobody thought
they needed to."

"So he used it without asking."

I open my mouth.

And I hear the wind. The wind on the microphone, like crumpled paper. The sea hitting the
hulls. The outer edge of the deck, where it's windy. Monday, the third, twenty-three
fourteen. Two hours before the car left the Well with my
code. He called from the deck, and I didn't answer, and two hours later he went up.

*Iara.*

A breath. Of someone who was going to say something more, and was choosing how.

"Iara?" Inês says.

"He called me," I say. My voice comes out strange. "The night before. At eleven at night. I didn't
answer."

Inês stays silent. A long time. I see something cross her
face, quickly, that she hides right away, but that I see: it isn't distrust. It's
pity.

"Did he leave a message?"

"Nine seconds. My name. And the wind."

"You think he was going to ask."

"I don't know what he was going to say."

It's the truth. I don't know. But I know, sitting in a room at twenty-five degrees with
goosebumps on my arms, what I think, and Inês taught me to separate the two things, and
I separate them, and even separated they both hurt the same.

---

"I'm going to be honest with you," Inês says. "Because I think you deserve it."

She puts her hands together on the table, the sleeves rolled twice.

"For the agency, Otávio died in an accident. So far, that's what the evidence says.
But the Operator doesn't only want to know how he died. It wants to know how a car
left the Well off-schedule, in the middle of the night, with no emergency at all. And the
answer is: with your code. On an emergency departure you never asked for, but that's in your
name." She stops. "The Operator's legal people think you made it easy. That you gave him the
code, or let him know the code knowing he could use it. That a man six days from never
going up again went out alone, off-schedule, because you let him. And that he died up there."

"I didn't know he was going up."

"I believe you. Legal, not yet." She looks at the ribbon through the window. "Your
suspension is going to continue. Not because of the seal, or cutting the rope. Because of this.
Until they decide whether you're responsible."

"Responsible for his death."

"For his climb. It's not the same thing. But to someone on the outside, it looks like it."

I don't say anything. There's nothing to say. Outside, in the window, a cargo climber
goes up the ribbon, the second of the morning, and I hear, even through the glass, the rumble of the
wheels rising in pitch until it becomes a hum and then nothing.

"One last thing," Inês says, when I'm already on my feet. "The notebook."

"The 2110 one."

"It wasn't in the apartment when we sealed it. On the fourteenth. I had them
search. The team listed twenty-one notebooks on the shelf, from 2089 to 2109. There
was no 2110."

"So someone took it before."

"Or he took it. Or it was never there." She opens the door for me. "What you know,
Iara, is that it wasn't there. The rest is what you think."

---

I go down to the cargo deck to look for Bia.

I tell myself it's because it's what I do when I don't know what to do: I go to
Bia. I don't tell myself about the line at eleven fifty-eight.
It's been that way for nine years. Bia came to the cable three years after me, and Otávio
trained us both, and we shared a car on thirty climbs and a bunk room for a hundred nights
at the Post, and she's the only person on the Anchor who has seen me throw up and laugh on the same day.

The cargo deck is on the north side of the Well, on the lower ring, and at two in the
afternoon it's one single noise: the belts carrying the columns of disks into the
cargo bodies, the loading arms swinging, people in orange coveralls shouting
numbers at each other over the rumble. The heat there is worse than anywhere
else, because of the machines. Thirty-eight, thirty-nine.

Bia is at belt three, checking a column of disks with the reader. She sees me
coming across the deck. I see her see me.

And I see her turn her back.

It isn't a big movement. She just turns her body half a step, to the other side of the
belt, and goes on checking the column, and shouts a number to someone up
ahead, and doesn't look at me again.

I stop in the middle of the deck. The people in orange go past me, swerving.

I wait. She doesn't turn.

I send a message from my wrist, right there, ten meters from her. *Bia. I need to talk
to you.*

I see her feel her wrist buzz. I see her look. I see her type, without turning.

*Not now.*

And then, a second later, another:

*Sorry.*

---

I walk back to hull seven, along deck two, counting the steps on every
stairway, sixty-six kilos on each one. Outside it's thirty-seven, and the afternoon
rain starts when I'm halfway there, and falls hard on the slanted
panels of the roofs, and runs down the gutters to the cisterns, and the covered
corridor fills with a noise of water that doesn't wet anyone.

I think: my code. My unanswered call. My friend with her back turned,
who was on the deck at twelve past one.

I think: it's me. For the Operator, for legal, for anyone on the outside, the
person whose code took out of the Well, off-schedule, the car of a man who died up
there, is me.

And I think, with the part of my head that nobody switched off and that keeps doing
sums by itself, something worse, that I can't switch off:

if I had answered, he would have asked. And I would have said yes.
