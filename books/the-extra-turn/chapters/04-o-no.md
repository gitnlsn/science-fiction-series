At three in the morning, I read the knot.

It isn't that I decide to read it. I'm lying in the top bunk, on the right, with the
curtain closed and the reading light turned to the wall, the way he turned it,
looking at the plastic ceiling thirty centimeters from my face. The Post makes the
noise it makes in the small hours, the low hum of the ventilation, the occasional
tick of the structure cooling on the shadow side. Across the corridor,
Inês is breathing slowly, sleeping like someone who sleeps anywhere. Álvaro isn't
sleeping; I hear him moving in the bottom bunk on the right, the only one he would take.

And I'm thinking about my first year, without meaning to, about some exit or other, and
suddenly the knot is there, read, like a word you couldn't remember
that turns up whole when you stop looking for it.

---

It was in the eighth month. I wasn't throwing up anymore. I could tie the bowline without looking,
with the glove on, in the dark. We were outside, the two of us, at twelve thousand
kilometers, on a stretch the car's reader had marked as normal, and
Otávio stopped climbing the ribbon and stood looking at something I couldn't see.

"Look at this," he said.

I looked. It was the ribbon. Dark, matte, the same.

"I don't see anything."

"I know. Neither does the reader." He ran his glove slowly along the edge of the ribbon, and then
along the face, and then along the edge again. "But there's something. Feel it."

I ran my glove over it. I didn't feel anything. I felt the ribbon, hard, stretched. I said I
didn't feel anything, and he didn't insist. He took from the leg of his suit a short length of
emergency rope, half a meter, and went twice around the ribbon, good and tight,
and closed it with a bowline.

With the extra turn.

"Why the turn?" I asked. It was the first time I'd asked.

"Because this isn't for holding anything," he said. "This is for marking. When
I find something on the ribbon that the reader doesn't see, I tie this. Two turns and the
knot. Whoever comes after me, on the next exit, will find the rope and will know two
things." He raised one gloved finger. "That it was Otávio. Because of the turn,
which nobody puts in." He raised another finger. "And that it's here."

"Here what?"

"Here. That's all. Here. Look here. Come back here." He tugged the rope to see if it
was firm. "*Tie it like you're coming back*, kid. It's not for you. It's for
whoever comes."

Two weeks later, on the next exit, maintenance replaced a meter and a half of
ribbon on that stretch. The handheld reader, which is finer than the car's, had
found a flaw in the weave of fibers, small, that would have grown. Nobody could
say how Otávio had seen it. He said he hadn't seen it. He'd felt it.

I never thought about that rope again. Until now, at three in the morning, thirty
centimeters from a plastic ceiling.

---

He didn't tie himself on so he wouldn't fall.

I mean, he did tie himself on. But that wasn't why. A man who's out of air and who
only wants not to fall clips the line, or what's left of it, or hugs the ribbon. He doesn't take
the rope off his leg, doesn't go around twice, doesn't tie a bowline with an extra
turn that holds nothing and that costs, in gloves, without air, some thirty seconds
more.

He spent those thirty seconds. Out of a reserve that was running out.

He tied himself on the way you tie a mark. Two turns and the knot. It was Otávio. And it's
here.

He marked the place. With the only rope he had. With his own body.

I sit up in the bunk and hit my head on the plastic ceiling, and don't feel it.

---

I put the suit on at three forty, without turning on the light.

I know I shouldn't. I know the whole list of why I shouldn't, by heart, in the order of the
manual: exit off-schedule, exit without a partner, exit without telling control, exit in the
small hours, exit with an agency investigator asleep four meters away and the
head of operations awake three meters away. I know Otávio did all of that too, on a
Thursday at ten at night, and that he's in a cold room.

I put it on anyway. I take the handheld reader from the workshop, the good one, the fine one, the one we
take along to confirm what the car finds. I strap it to my thigh. I go into the
airlock. I close the inner door, slowly, so the latch won't click.

The air leaves in a breath that I feel and don't hear.

I go out.

---

A hundred and eighty meters down. Hand over hand, down the ribbon, weightless. It isn't
dark. Down below, on the Anchor, it's four in the morning and deep night; up
here, night almost never comes. The Post is too high for the Earth's
shadow, which only reaches it near midnight, for an hour, at this time of year. The
rest of the time it's the Sun, always, hard, from the side, with no air to soften it. The ribbon
shines dully on the side it comes from and is black on the other. The Post's position lights,
red and green, keep getting smaller up above.

The dosimeter on my chest starts counting faster. Green.

A hundred and eighty meters. I stop.

They're there.

The two turns of emergency rope, around the ribbon, tight, with the end
I cut swinging loose. I cut on the ribbon side, away from the knot, to
take the knot with him; and what was left were these two turns, here, hugging the
cable, with no knot at all, with the cut end. I spent three days at the Post knowing
they were here, and didn't think about them.

He marked the place, and I took the mark and left the place.

I put my glove over the two turns. They're firm. Six days outside and
they're firm. He knew how to pull tight.

"Hi," I say, to the rope.

---

I take the handheld reader off my thigh.

It's the size of a book, with one face that rests against the ribbon and a screen on the
other side. I rest it there, two hand-widths above the turns. I wait. The screen draws, in
bands of color, what's inside the ribbon at that point: the weave of the fibers,
the density, the tension, the stiffness, the modulus. Green, green, green. Normal.

I go down a hand-width. Rest it. Green.

Another hand-width. On top of the two turns, now, moving the rope aside with my other hand.
Green.

A hand-width below.

The modulus band turns a different green. Not yellow. A darker green,
heavier. I look at the number. Eight tenths of one percent below the ribbon's
value. Less than one percent.

Tolerance is one percent. Anything under one percent of
difference we call noise: the ribbon isn't the same everywhere, it never was,
it has stretches a little stiffer and stretches a little softer since it was
made, and the reader and the robots and the whole cable system were built to
ignore what's within tolerance, because otherwise we'd be stopping the
elevator every week over nothing.

Eight tenths of one percent is nothing.

I go down another hand-width. Eight tenths. Another. Seven. Another. Six. Four
hand-widths down, zero, normal green. I go back up. Eight tenths, eight, eight.
Forty centimeters of ribbon, just below the two turns of rope, where the ribbon
is eight tenths of one percent softer than it should be.

I do it again. Same.

It's nothing. It's within tolerance. No robot is going to flag it, no
filter is going to let it through, no report is going to tell anyone. It's
nothing.

And it's exactly here. In the forty centimeters under the knot.

I put my glove on it. I run it slowly over the face of the ribbon, and then over the edge, and
then the edge again, the way he did in my eighth month, and I close my eyes
inside the helmet, and try to feel.

On the edge, a hand-width below the turns, the glove catches. Faintly. As if the edge
of the ribbon, which should be smooth, were a little rough, with the finest fibers
working loose from the weave, standing up, like the hair on an arm.

I don't know if that's it. I don't know if it's the ribbon, or his lifeline, which ended up
frayed a hand's width from the carabiner, rubbing here. I don't know if it's the cause or the
consequence. I don't even know if it's anything.

I know it's here.

---

"Iara."

The voice in my ear isn't control's. It's Álvaro's, and it isn't the careful hospital
voice. It's another one, that I've heard only a few times in my life, once when a
rookie let go of his lifeline on the deck of the Anchor on a windy day.

"Iara, you're outside."

"I am."

"Alone."

"I am."

"It's four in the morning." His voice shakes. Not with anger. "You saw where he
is. You saw. And you go out alone, in the small hours, off-schedule, to the same place.
*Nobody falls alone*, kid. Did you forget?"

"There's something on the ribbon, Álvaro."

"Get back inside."

"Eight tenths of one percent on the modulus. Forty centimeters. Just below
where he tied himself. He marked it, Álvaro. He tied himself on the way you tie a mark.
With the turn. You know what the turn means. He taught you too."

Silence on the channel. A long silence. I hear Álvaro breathing, and I hear, behind
him, another voice, low, calm, that says something I can't make out.

"Eight tenths is within tolerance," Álvaro says, finally. His voice has
changed again. It's gone tired. "You know that."

"I know."

"The robots will reread the stretch. Today. All of it. I'll ask for it."

"The robots won't see it."

"If there's something there, they will."

"He saw it, Álvaro. And they didn't."

He doesn't answer. The other voice speaks again, and this time it's on the channel, direct, in
my ear, calm as if speaking across a kitchen table.

"Iara, this is Inês Garrido. You've left the station during an investigation, without
authorization, and you've disturbed the site where the body was found. I need you to
come back now, not to touch anything else, and to hand me the reader that's
in your hand without erasing any readings. All right?"

"All right."

"Thank you."

---

She's waiting for me in the airlock. She doesn't touch me. She holds out her hand, and I put the
reader in her hand, and she looks at the screen, the bands of color, the darker green, and
says nothing.

Álvaro is in the corridor, arms crossed, big, with his eyes red
again, or still.

"I'm going to have to suspend your access," Inês says. She says it as if apologizing,
without apologizing. "To the cable and the stations. Until the end of the investigation. It's protocol. You
altered an incident site. I'm not saying you meant harm. I'm saying
you did it."

"I didn't alter anything. The turns are there."

"You cut the rope."

I have nothing to say.

"Tomorrow you go down," Inês says. "With him. The maintenance car takes both of you.
I'll stay with Álvaro two more days, for the robots to reread the stretch. Down there,
I'll talk to you again."

I look at Álvaro. He looks at the floor.

"She's right, kid," he says. "Go down with him. Take him home."

And I understand, standing in the airlock with my suit still on and the condensation
running down the inside of the visor, that I've just lost the cable. Twelve years. One
night. Eight tenths of one percent.

And that he knew it would be like this. That whoever found that would have to choose between
tolerance and the extra turn.

He chose the turn. So did I.
