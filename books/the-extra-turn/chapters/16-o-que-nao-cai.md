The fine robots read the stretch every hour, and the number climbs.

The first reading comes in at two on Friday morning, and I'm awake in the meeting
module, held by my feet, with the notebook open. *Stretch 20,000 − 180 m. Modulus:
−0.9%.* One tenth more than I measured two months ago. Within tolerance.

At three: *−0.9%*.

At four: *−1.0%*.

At five, the number turns yellow on the screen for the first time in twenty years of the cable.
*−1.1%. Out of tolerance.* The system, which ignored eight tenths for four years,
now that someone has sent the robots to look up close, beeps. A short beep, polite,
like someone mentioning something they should have mentioned earlier.

I don't sleep. I redo Otávio's math with every reading, in the margin of page twenty-four,
under his crossings-out, with the pen from the kit. His curve had five points in four years. Mine has six points in six hours.

At eight in the morning: *−1.4%*.

---

Álvaro reaches the module at ten past eight, his eyes puffy, his gray hair
standing on end. He doesn't say good morning. He looks at the screen.

"Kid."

"I know."

"How long?"

I show him the margin of page twenty-four. His math and, underneath, mine, with
the night's points. The curve isn't a curve anymore. It's a wall.

"If nothing else goes through there," I say, "sixty hours. More or less. Could be
fifty. Could be seventy. There's no way to know better than that. He did the math with
four climbers a day and four months. The ribbon didn't wait the four months."

Álvaro keeps looking at the number. His shoulders, those broad shoulders, don't move.

"Two a day," he says, quietly.

"Two a day was the answer three months ago." I close the notebook. "Today,
anything that goes through there is what breaks it."

---

At eight thirty, Hugo comes into the module.

He isn't in his light shirt. He's in Station coveralls, gray, rumpled, his black
hair loose around his head. He hasn't slept either. He looks at the screen and stops, holding on to the
door frame.

"One point four."

"One point six," I say. The nine o'clock reading has just come in.

Hugo closes his eyes.

Behind him, in the corridor, comes the Station commander, a thin woman with a shaved
head whose name is Laís and whom I've seen twice in my life, and who has the face of someone
who was woken with bad news and hasn't yet decided whether to believe it. And Moisés comes, from the
yard. And, on everyone's wrist at the same time, Inês's calm voice, from down below, from
room 14 in hull two, a tenth of a second late.

"I'm seeing the numbers," Inês says. "Control passed them to me. What are you going to do?"

---

Nobody answers right away.

And then everybody answers at once, and I stay in the middle, held by my feet, with the
notebook closed against my chest, listening.

Laís says there's a passenger car at the Post. P-7, coming down from the Station to the
Anchor, with twelve people: ten end-of-shift technicians and the wife and daughter of one of them,
who had come up to see the Earth from above. It stopped at the Post last night, at the usual
stop. It was supposed to leave at six this morning. It didn't, because control held it when the
number turned yellow. It's there, docked at the Post's collar, a hundred and eighty meters
above the stretch. To get to the ground, it has to go through it.

Moisés says the Post has air for twelve people for four days.

Hugo says that if they stop the cable, they stop the cargo climbers, and the sunshade, and that
it's weeks and months and nobody knows how many, and that every day stopped counts.

Laís says that if the stretch breaks with P-7 above it, the lower part of the ribbon falls
along the Equator, and the upper part, with the Post and P-7 attached to it, rises into a
higher orbit, unbalanced, and nobody knows where to, and she doesn't want to find out.

And someone, a Station technician I don't know, who is behind Laís in the corridor,
says the thing I was waiting for someone to say.

"Release P-7."

Everybody looks at him.

"Release it from the ribbon," he says, quickly, breathless. "Before the stretch gives. It comes off the
collar, off the ribbon, stays in orbit, and then someone goes to get it. Takes its weight off the
ribbon. Takes away the risk. It stays up there, floating, waiting."

---

"No," I say.

It's the first thing I've said since ten past eight. It comes out louder than I meant.

The technician looks at me. Laís looks at me. I'm the woman in a technician's suit who
came out of a niche, suspended, with access to nothing, and everyone there knows it.

"At twenty thousand kilometers, a loose thing doesn't stay," I say. "The ribbon there moves at one
kilometer and nine hundred per second. To stay in orbit, it would need three
nine hundred. Twice as much. Released there, P-7 doesn't float. It falls. Slowly at first, and then not so
slowly, in a curve that ends inside the Earth. Before it gets there, it enters the air." I
hold the notebook tighter. "And burns. With the twelve people inside. And somebody on the deck of the Anchor
will look up and see a streak of light and think it's pretty."

Nobody speaks.

"Everything falls there," I say. "Just slowly. Don't you ever forget that."

The technician goes white. He doesn't say anything else. Álvaro, beside me, closes his big hand
around my arm, without force, and I don't know if it's to hold me up or to hold himself up.

---

"Then what?" Laís asks. "It stays at the Post and prays?"

"The clamp," Álvaro says.

Laís doesn't know what it is. Hugo doesn't know. Moisés, who is old, knows: I can see it on his
face.

I know. Otávio showed me once, in my second year, at the back of the Post's equipment
bay, under a tarp. A collar of metal and fiber the size of a
suitcase, in two halves, with a hinge on one side and a latch on the other, that hugs the ribbon from both
sides along two meters and tightens, and carries the load through itself,
across the weak stretch, like a splint on a broken arm. It was designed when the cable was
built, for a stretch that gave trouble before anyone could replace it. There's one
at every Post. It has never been used. In twenty years.

And it can't be installed by robot. The robots read. They don't tighten. The clamp is installed by hand, from
outside, in gloves, opening the two halves around the ribbon and closing the latch and
tightening the tensioners one by one, in the right order, with the one-kilo torque wrench, the
same one that fell slowly in my first year.

"With the clamp on the stretch, the ribbon will hold," Álvaro says. "Weeks. Until the replacement. P-7
goes over the clamp and down." He looks at Laís. "And the cable stops. Everything. No
climbers, no cargo, no extras, until the clamp is in place and the replacement starts."

"Who installs it?" Laís asks.

Álvaro doesn't answer right away.

"I know how to install it," he says, finally. "Otávio and I. We trained on it in 2089, at the temporary
Post, with a dummy one. Nobody trained on it after that. There was no need."

"And Otávio is dead," Hugo says, quietly.

"And I know how," I say.

Everybody looks at me again.

"He showed me. In my second year. With the real one, under the tarp. He opened it, closed it,
tightened the tensioners in order, so I could see. He said someone besides him had to know."
I look at Álvaro. "I saw it once."

"Once isn't knowing, kid."

"It's more than nobody."

---

The math is this, and I do it out loud, for everyone, in the round module, with the Earth the
size of a fist in the big window.

From the Station to the Post is fifteen thousand seven hundred and eighty-six kilometers. At
three hundred an hour, fifty-two hours and thirty-seven minutes. The stretch has
sixty, more or less. Could be fifty. If we leave now, we get there with seven hours
to spare, or with none.

And the maintenance car going down doesn't pass through the stretch. It stops at the Post, above it, at the
upper collar, beside P-7. We go out from the Post and climb down the last hundred and eighty
meters along the ribbon, hand over hand, the way I climbed down on the twelfth and on the sixteenth.

"And meanwhile," Hugo says, "the cable stopped. Fifty-two hours. And after that, the replacement."

"And after that, the replacement."

"Months."

"Months."

Hugo closes his eyes. When he opens them, he looks at the Earth, and I know what he's seeing: not
the Earth, the numbers. A tenth of a degree in one year. The still weeks that don't end.
Mrs. Celeste, whom he didn't know, multiplied by a whole coast.

"There has to be another way," he says.

"There isn't," I say.

"You don't know that."

"I don't." I look at him. "But I know that if the stretch breaks, there's no sunshade at all,
Hugo. Not two a day, not one. The ribbon falls on the Equator, and the sunshade stops for good, and nobody
replaces anything for years. Stopping now is losing months. Not stopping is losing everything."

---

The Station's emergency stop is in the main corridor, ten meters from the meeting
module, in a red box on the wall with a glass lid and a lever inside.
It has existed since construction, like the clamp. It's the only thing on the cable anyone can
pull, with or without access, with or without a schedule, with or without authorization, because it was made
for when there was no time to ask anyone. It's on page one of the manual. I'm
sure it's on page one.

Nobody has ever pulled it.

I take my feet out of the strap and push myself along the corridor. There's no floor. There's nothing to push against.
I go floating, slowly, with the notebook clipped to my wrist by its strap, with nothing holding
me and nothing making me fall, and behind me I hear Hugo say *Iara, wait*, and
Laís say something about protocol, and Moisés say nothing.

I reach the red box and stop with my hand on the wall, turning a little.

At the Station, nothing falls. And nothing holds. I'm thirty-five thousand kilometers from
any ground, weighing nothing at all, with access to nothing, suspended, alone in front of a
red box, and what keeps me here isn't anything I'm carrying. It's tension.
On one side, Hugo and the heat and Mrs. Celeste multiplied. On the other, Otávio and the
ruled box and twelve people in a car docked at an empty Post.

I lift the glass lid.

"Let her."

It's Álvaro's voice, behind me, in the corridor. Not loud. Firm. The same as when I
took Otávio to the cold room against protocol, and control hesitated, and he said
*let her*.

And, on my wrist, a tenth of a second late, Inês's calm voice, from down below:

"Do it."

I pull the lever.

---

There's no noise. Not here. But I know what's happening, because I've seen it my whole
life in training, on the screen, without its ever happening for real.

All along the ribbon, at the same time, a hundred thousand kilometers, the wheel rings of every
climber stop. The three cargo climbers that were going up in the morning convoy, at five, twelve
and twenty-eight thousand kilometers. C-114, in the transfer yard, where I got out, which was going to go down empty. The
arms in the transfer yard, which stop mid-movement with a column of disks
hanging. The belt that leads to the upper ribbon. At the tip, a hundred thousand kilometers out, the sunshade's
launcher, which for the first time in twenty years releases nothing toward the Sun.

And on the Anchor, on deck two, on the upper ring of the Well, the old men who watch the ribbon rise,
with the wet cloth on the backs of their necks, are going to see the ramp of the launch bay stop with three
climbers in a row, and not understand, and ask someone.

The cable stops singing.

Hugo is in the corridor, three meters from me, holding a strap. He doesn't say anything. He looks at the
pulled lever. Then he looks at me, and on his face there's no anger at all. There's something
else, which I recognize because it's the same thing I had in the cold room, sitting beside a
three-kilo man: the tension, without the weight.

"Go," he says. "Before I change my mind."

Álvaro already has his suit in his hand.
