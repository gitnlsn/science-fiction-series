I sleep fourteen hours and wake up weighing sixty-six kilos.

That isn't a figure of speech either. I feel every one of them. I feel them in my back, lying down, the
mattress pushing up with the exact force of sixty-six kilos. I feel them
in my neck, when I lift my head off the pillow, the weight of the head. I feel them in my
arms, when I push my body up to sit. Eleven days of almost nothing, and the body
has gone back to being work.

My apartment is in hull seven, on the second ring out from the Well, two
floors below the deck. It's one room and a kitchen and a bathroom, with a round
window that looks out on the sea and that's always fogged on the outside, because
the air outside is always more humid than the air in. The cooler on the
wall makes its usual noise, a tired hum, and keeps the room at twenty-nine
degrees. The quota for hull seven is twenty-nine. Whoever wants twenty-seven pays the
difference.

Outside, at seven in the morning, it's thirty-six.

---

I go out at eight. Along the corridor of hull seven, I climb the stairs to deck two,
counting the steps, because every step is work now. Twenty-two. On the
deck, the heat hits all at once, humid, smelling of salt and hot metal, and
under it the usual smell of the Anchor, which is seaweed and oil and coffee and
too many people in a small place.

I go to the Well. I don't know why. Because it's where I've gone every morning for twelve
years.

---

From the upper ring of the Well you can see the launch bay.

It's the north side of the Well, where the ribbon takes the cargo climbers. There are three of them,
lined up on the ramp, waiting their turn. They're bigger than the maintenance car,
long, gray, with no window at all, with the wheel ring around the ribbon
and the cargo body hanging underneath, and inside the cargo body, I know without
seeing, the disks are stacked.

I saw the disks once, up close, when I helped unload a climber
that had come back with a fault. They're the size of a dinner plate and thinner
than a soap bubble, transparent, with a pattern of very fine lines engraved
inside that changes color when the light hits it. They weigh almost nothing. They come stacked
in columns held by rings, like glass coins, and each climber carries thirteen
tons of them.

They go up the ribbon to the tip, a hundred thousand kilometers out, where whatever comes
loose from the ribbon goes away, and each stack goes with an ion tug that spends months
pushing it into place. A cloud nobody sees
from down here, between us and the Sun, bending a tiny bit of the light aside.

A tiny bit. One point two percent. That's what the sunshade takes off the Sun, today.

The first climber in line lets go of the ramp and rises. It makes a noise I know
the way I know my name: the rumble of the ring's wheels taking the ribbon, rising in pitch
as it accelerates, until it becomes a hum and then nothing. In a minute, it's a
dot. In five, it's gone.

Beside me, at the railing of the ring, an old man I know by sight, who sells
dried fish on deck three, is watching too, with a wet cloth on the back of his neck.

"Another one," he says.

"Another one."

"They say they go to where a thing goes around the Sun at the same pace as the Earth." He wrings
the cloth, and it drips on the deck. "Before them you couldn't go out in the daytime. We lived at night, like bats. I
remember. I already had a little grandson."

"I remember a little. I was fourteen."

"They say now it's going to get to one point eight percent," the old man says. "They say then you'll
have summer again. I won't see it. But my grandson will."

He says it looking at the ribbon, with something on his face that's almost devotion. It isn't
wonder. Nobody here wonders at the ribbon. It's something else. It's the way
the old people of the mainland, in the old images, looked at a dam, or at a
field that was going to get rain. The ribbon is what lets his grandson have summer.

Three climbers a day, some forty tons. Every day. For twenty years.
And the cloud loses about five percent a year: the ribbon barely keeps ahead of the wear. If the
ribbon stops, the sunshade doesn't just stop growing: it starts to shrink.

I've known that since I was a child, and I had never thought of it as something that could
end.

---

The tribute is at six in the evening, on the lower ring of the Well.

It isn't the burial. The burial will come later, when the agency releases the body. It's
what the Operator calls a *remembrance gathering*, and what the Anchor calls *the
circle*: everyone who wants to comes, stands around the Well, and whoever wants to speaks.
Álvaro isn't here: he stayed at the Post with Inês and the robots, rereading the stretch. The one who opens is
a man I know from a distance and had never seen speak without a microphone.

Hugo. The director of launches. The sunshade man.

He's about fifty and thin, tall, in a light long-sleeved shirt even
in the heat, his black hair combed back, wet with sweat at the temples. He isn't
from the Anchor. He came from the mainland eight years ago to run the program, and he never
lost the manner of someone who came from elsewhere: he stands a little too straight, speaks a little
too correctly. But when he starts to speak, at the edge of the Well, with the ribbon rising
behind him, the whole circle goes quiet.

"I used to quarrel with Otávio," he says. "Everyone here is aware of it. I do not intend to pretend."

A low laugh goes around the circle. Sad. Everyone knows.

"He believed that we launched too much. That three climbers a day were
too much for the ribbon. That it was tired. He used to say it like that: *the ribbon is
tired, Hugo*. As if it were a person." Hugo stops, looks at the ribbon behind
him, and comes back. "I'd say the heat doesn't wait. That every climber we
don't send is one more week of heat for these people here. For you. I'd
say that to him in front of everyone, in the deck three canteen, and he'd
say I wasn't from the cable, and I'd say he wasn't from the climate." The laugh
again, lower. "The last time was one month ago. He asked for a reduction to
two a day. I said no. He left my office without saying anything, which he never
did. He always said something."

Hugo goes silent. The ribbon rises behind him. The circle waits.

"What he was doing up there, we do not know," he says, finally. "But
I am certain that he went up because he believed the ribbon needed him. I would have liked to
ask him why. I did not ask. I was too occupied with being right."

He steps away from the edge of the Well. Other people speak. A woman from the canteen. Two
old technicians, whom I've known since I was a girl. An eighteen-year-old boy whom
Otávio trained last year, who can't finish his sentence and leaves crying.

I don't speak. I can't. I stay at the back of the circle, my back against the wall of the ring,
weighing sixty-six kilos, and I look at Hugo, who is on the other side of the
Well, alone, in his light shirt, looking at the ribbon. And I think: *a reduction to two
a day. A month ago. Denied.*

And I think: *the ribbon is tired, Hugo.*

Bia is on the other side of the circle too. I see her between the people. She sees me.
She looks at Hugo, and then at me, and then at the floor, and when the circle
ends she leaves before I can get near her.

---

Otávio's apartment is in hull nineteen, on the outer edge, where it's windy.

I've had the key since I was twenty-five. He gave it to me on a day of heavy rain,
when my hull flooded and I slept a week on his sofa, and afterward he never
asked for it back, and I never gave it back. I use it for the first time in nine years.

On the door, there's a seal. A thin strip, transparent, stuck across the seam of the door with
the agency's mark, and underneath, in small type: *inspected — 14th — do not
break*. The agency has been here. Seven days ago, while I was at the Post
sitting beside him in the cold room.

I break the seal.

It isn't a crime, I think. I have the key. But I break it knowing I'll have to explain.

---

Inside, it's the way he kept it.

One room and a kitchen, like mine, but emptier. A wooden table, a
chair, an old armchair turned toward the round window that looks out on the outer
edge, on the open sea, where it's windy. An iron shelf with paper books,
a few, old, and pieces of ribbon in frames, sample pieces with dates written
in pencil. On the wall, an old photo, on paper, of two young men in orange coveralls
in a temporary Post, laughing: him and Álvaro, in their early thirties, before the
beard.

The table is too tidy. I notice it right away. Otávio never left his table
tidy: he left paper, pencils, his mug, the notebook. Always the notebook. A
black-covered notebook, thick, real paper, in which he wrote down the measurements he
took by hand, with the handheld reader, on his exits. He had several, one for each year, on the
shelf, with the years written on the spine. The one for the current year was always on the table,
open, with the pencil inside.

On the shelf, the notebooks are there. Twenty-one. From 2089 to 2109, with the years on the
spine, in his handwriting.

The one for 2110 isn't there.

It isn't on the table. It isn't on the shelf. It isn't in the drawer, where I look, or
under the armchair, or in the bathroom, or in the kitchen, or at the back of the closet,
where I look knowing it won't be there. The notebook in which he wrote down the measurements
for this year is gone.

Or it was taken. By the agency, which was here and sealed the door. Or by him, who
took it up with him. Or by someone else, who was here before the agency, or after.

The table is too tidy.

---

In the table drawer, under some water bills and an old cooler
manual, there's an envelope.

A paper envelope, with the letterhead of the Operator's medical service, opened with
a finger, the way he opened everything, tearing the edge crooked. Inside, a single sheet.
Dated three weeks before his death.

I read it standing up, with sweat running down my back, in the apartment with no cooler
switched on.

*Dear Technician Otávio Reis. We wish to inform you that the effective dose accumulated over the course of
your career has reached the limit established by the Operator's radiation
protection regulations. In accordance with the regulations, your last authorized
ascent must take place no later than the 10th of next month, after which your duties
will be restricted to ground activities. We thank you for your thirty-one years of
service on the cable.*

Underneath, the signature of a doctor I don't know. And, in pencil, in the margin, in his
handwriting, slanted, with the dot on the i crooked, a single word:

*Still.*

I sit down in his chair. With the letter in my hand.

Thirty-one years on the cable. Three thousand nights at the Post. The crystal-strip
dosimeter, the old one, that didn't talk to control, keeping every dose in
layers, the way a tree trunk keeps the years, until the trunk got too thick. He'd known
for three weeks. That the last climb had a date. That after the tenth
he would never again go past a thousand kilometers, never again see the Earth the size
of a hand, never again feel the ribbon through his glove.

*Still.*

The leave. The week on the mainland, the first in two years, on the Thursday ship. It wasn't
leave. It was what you say when you don't want to say the other thing.

By the dosimeter curve, two and a half days of climbing before Thursday: he went up
on the fourth. Before the tenth. Within the deadline.

I stay sitting in his chair with the letter in my hand until the round window goes
dark, and the sea off the outer edge turns black, and the wind starts, and I don't know what
that word means: whether it was *I can still*, or *not done yet*, or *I still have
one thing to do up there*.

Or all three.
