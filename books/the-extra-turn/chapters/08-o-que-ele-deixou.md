The hearing is on a Tuesday, and Álvaro speaks for me without my asking.

It's in the big room in hull two, the one for the Operator's meetings, with a long table
and three people from legal sitting on one side, in light shirts, and me on the other,
alone, in the technician's coveralls I no longer have the right to wear and that I
put on anyway, because I have nothing else that's right for this. Inês is
in a corner, arms crossed, not speaking. It isn't her hearing.

Legal reads the charge. They don't call it a charge: they call it a *liability
inquiry*. They read the departure log, the line at twelve past one, my
code. They read the letter from the medical service. They read the ship's list. They read, in a voice that
neither rises nor falls, the sentence I've known by heart since room 14: that a technician
with emergency access allowed, by action or omission, a technician barred from
going up to go up.

And then the door opens, and Álvaro comes in.

He wasn't invited. I can see on the faces from legal that he wasn't. He comes in
slowly, the way he walks on the ground, as if the deck were asking permission, and pulls a
chair from the wall, and sits down on my side of the table. On my side. Not at the head, where
the bosses sit. On my side.

"May I speak?" he asks.

Nobody says no.

"Otávio knew her code because I told him to," Álvaro says. "Twenty years ago.
When the cable opened. I wrote the rule: every trainer knows the code of their trainees,
in case someone passes out outside. It's in the manual. Page forty-something.
I wrote it." He puts his two mechanic's hands on the table, one on top of the
other. "If anyone is responsible for Otávio having Iara's code, it's me. If
you want to inquire into something, inquire into the rule. Not the kid."

Legal takes notes. Someone says the rule will be reviewed. Someone says the
inquiry continues. Nobody looks at me.

On the way out, in the corridor, I want to thank him, and don't know how, and Álvaro notices, and
puts his hand on my shoulder, the big hand, heavy, at one hundred percent.

"No need," he says. "He'd have wanted me to do it. And I'd have wanted him to
do it for me."

---

On Friday, the heat starts.

Not the usual heat. The other one. The one the Anchor calls *the still week*, because
the air goes still, no wind at all, not even on the outer edge, and the humidity rises until
sweat has nowhere left to go. The old people know it by the smell, they say. I
know it by the cooler in hull seven, which at four in the afternoon starts making a
different noise, higher, like something working harder than it should.

My wrist shows, in red, a number I learned in school to respect: the
wet-bulb. Not the temperature. The temperature and the humidity together, the number that says
whether the body can still cool itself by sweating. On Friday afternoon it's at thirty-one.
On Friday night, thirty-two. The Operator sends the usual message to every
wrist: *stay inside, drink water, don't switch off your cooler*.

On Saturday morning, thirty-two and a half.

I spend the morning lying on the floor of my room, because the floor is cooler than the bed,
with a wet cloth on my forehead, doing nothing, because I have nothing to do.
I have no cable. I have no shift. I have no Bia, who doesn't answer. I have sixty-six
kilos stuck to the floor and a forty-one-second file that won't open.

---

Mrs. Celeste lives next door.

She's eighty-four and sold dried fish on deck three her whole life, until
her legs wouldn't let her anymore. She's a widow. She has a son on the mainland who calls on her
birthday. Every day, at five in the afternoon, she knocks on my wall with her
knuckles, three times, which is her way of saying good afternoon, and I knock back three
times, which is mine. It's been like that for eight years. Sometimes, when I'm at the Post, I
think about her knocking on the wall and nobody answering.

On Saturday, at five in the afternoon, she doesn't knock.

I wait. At ten past five, I knock. Three times. Nothing.

I get up off the floor. My legs complain. I go out to the corridor, which is at thirty-eight
degrees, with the air still, and knock on her door. I call. Nothing. The door isn't
locked; nobody locks their door in hull seven.

It's hot inside. Hotter than in the corridor. The cooler on the wall is
off, its little light dark, and on its small screen, when I touch it, the message
appears: *monthly quota used up*.

She's sitting in her armchair, near the round window, with a paper fan
fallen in her lap. Her eyes closed. She looks like she's sleeping.

She isn't sleeping.

---

The Operator's medics arrive in twelve minutes. I sit on the floor of the
corridor, outside her door, while they do what they do, and I don't
cry. I don't know why. I cried it all out at five thousand kilometers, maybe. Or maybe
it's the heat, which dries out even that.

One of the medics comes out and crouches beside me. He's young. He's sweaty down to his neck.

"Did you know her?"

"She was my neighbor."

"She switched off her cooler on Wednesday," he says. "To save. Her quota
had run out, and she didn't want to pay the difference." He wipes his forehead with his
arm. "That's the fifth this week, on the Anchor. All over seventy."

Five.

I think of the old man with the dried fish, on the ring of the Well, with the wet cloth on the back of his neck. *My
mother used to say that before the sunshade you couldn't go out in the daytime.* I think of the three
climbers a day going up the ribbon, full of disks the size of a dinner
plate. One point two percent. I think of Hugo at the edge of the Well: *every
climber we don't send is one more week of heat for these people
here.*

I think of Mrs. Celeste knocking on the wall three times, every day, at five.

And I think of the eight tenths of one percent, of the forty centimeters of ribbon, just
below where he tied himself. That nobody sees. That are within tolerance. And
that, if they are something, if they grow, if one day they're a reason to stop the
elevator, will also stop the climbers, and the disks, and the cloud nobody sees
between us and the Sun, which already loses a few percent a year and which nobody will replace.

I had never thought about what it costs to stop the cable. I never needed to. The cable never
stopped.

Now I know what it costs. It costs this. An eighty-four-year-old woman in an
armchair, with a paper fan in her lap and the monthly quota used up.

---

At night, I don't sleep. The cooler in hull seven screams on the wall. On the other side,
Mrs. Celeste's place is empty and dark, and nobody knocks.

I lie on the floor and think about Otávio.

I don't want to. I think. The way things come back when the body stops and has
nothing else to do.

I was twenty-two and had been on the cable for three weeks, and I still hadn't
gone up once. Rookies spend the first month on the ground, in the Well,
learning what can be learned without leaving the Anchor. I was hating it. I wanted
to go up. I wanted to see the Earth the size of a hand.

One night, in the small hours, Otávio took me down to the lower ring of the Well, where the ribbon comes out of the
anchor proper: an enormous metal ring, thick as a tree trunk, fixed to the
bottom of the central hull, where the ribbon enters and is held by a mechanism I
didn't understand yet. It was empty. Only the noise of the sea hitting the hulls, down
below, and the ribbon rising from the ring and vanishing into the dark.

"Put your hand here," he said. And he put his on the metal of the ring. I put mine.

The metal was warm. And it vibrated. Not much. Something fine, continuous, that I
felt more in the bones of my hand than in the skin, the way you feel an engine running on the
other side of a wall.

"What is that?"

"It's the cable singing."

I thought he was joking. He wasn't.

"Everything that's on the ribbon makes the ribbon vibrate," he said. "Every climber going up,
every one coming down, every car parked at the Post, every time the wind hits down
here, every pebble that hits up there. The whole ribbon is a string stretched a
hundred thousand kilometers, kid. Everything that touches it, it sings." He closed his
eyes. "Feel it now. There's a cargo one going up. Three, four thousand kilometers, more
or less. It's the low note, the one that seems to come and go. Feel it?"

I closed my eyes. I felt the fine vibration. And, underneath it, after a while,
if I paid very close attention, something slower, lower, that came and went,
came and went, like breathing.

"I'm feeling something."

"That's it." Otávio opened his eyes. "The cargo ones sing low, because they're
heavy and pull more. The maintenance ones, high. And each one has its own way of coming and
going, because each one pulls the ribbon sideways in its own way, because each one is
always a little behind the piece of ribbon that's above it." He
smiled. "*Whoever climbs slowly*, remember?"

"Gets there faster than the cable."

"Gets there late. Always. And pulls. And the ribbon sings the lag."

"Do the robots hear that?"

"The robots hear everything. They record everything. On every stretch." He took his hand off the ring.
"But they only warn about what someone told them to warn about. The rest, they keep. There are a hundred
years of the cable's song stored somewhere, and nobody listens."

"Why?"

"Because it's work, kid." He got up, his knees cracking. "Listening
is always work."

I wrote it down in my round-lettered notebook, that night: *the cable sings. The robots
keep the song. Nobody listens.* And I didn't think about it again for twelve years.

---

Now, lying on the floor of hull seven at one hundred percent, with the cooler screaming and
the place next door empty, I think about it. And I think about the other thing he said, at the
Station, at the big window, with the apple.

*An orbit is a fall that never ends.*

I thought it was about the Station. About the physics. About the satellites that fall and
miss the Earth forever.

It wasn't only that.

I've been falling since the twelfth. Since the dark smudge on the ribbon, a hundred and eighty
meters below the Post. Falling since the knot, since the cold room, since the nine
seconds of wind, since the line at twelve past one with my code, since Bia with her
back turned at belt three, since Mrs. Celeste in her armchair. Falling toward a
ground I thought would come, and would hurt, and would end this.

And the ground doesn't come. I keep falling.

And maybe that's it. Maybe it never comes. Maybe what he was trying to
tell me, at twenty-four, at the big window, is that you can fall forever and
go on. That the fall doesn't have to end for you to survive it. That the
Station has been falling for twenty years and it's there, whole, with people inside, because
it's moving sideways fast enough to miss the ground on every turn.

Move sideways. Don't stop. Fall and miss.

I sit up on the floor of my room, in the dark.

I don't know yet what moving sideways means, for me. But I know I'm not going to wait for the ground.

---

On Sunday morning, the wet-bulb drops to thirty. The wind comes back to the outer edge.
The still week is over.

I go down to deck three and buy dried fish from the old man from the ring of the Well, who is
at Mrs. Celeste's stall, which is his now, and who recognizes me and says nothing, and
gives me an extra fish, wrapped in paper.

Then I go to belt three on the cargo deck, where Bia isn't, because it's
Sunday, and I stand at the edge of the deck watching the climbers go up, with the
wrapped fish under my arm, and I think that nobody listens to the cable's song.

And that someone used to.
