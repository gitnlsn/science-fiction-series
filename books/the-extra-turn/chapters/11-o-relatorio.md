At ten thousand kilometers, I weigh nine point three kilos and Bia talks to me over the
telemetry channel.

It isn't a channel for talking. It's the data line the climber uses to tell the Anchor
what's happening to it: ring temperature, bearing pressure, load,
speed. Numbers. One line of numbers per second, which nobody reads, because the
robots read for everyone. Bia found out, in her third month working on the side,
that you can put text in among the numbers, in a remarks field left over at the
end of each line, and that the text arrives at the other end without anyone seeing, because
nobody looks at the ends of the lines.

The extras use it to arrange times. We use it for this.

The niche's screen shows the lines going by, and at the end of one of them, at eleven
on Sunday night, there appears:

*Found it.*

---

I write back with the niche's emergency keyboard, which is small and stiff and
made for gloves: *Found what.*

The answer takes a while. Three lines. Five. I picture Bia at the kitchen table in
hull eleven, with Mrs. Rute's soap on the other side of the door, typing with two
fingers on her wrist, deleting, typing again.

*The report. He did it. The 17th of the second month. "Condition report — stretch
20,000 minus 180 m." With all the measurements. With the math. With both numbers. It's
in the Operator's archive. I got in through the deck access, which can see the condition
file because we need to know what not to load.*

I read it three times.

He did it. I knew he had. Otávio believed in reports.

*Who got it*, I write.

Another wait. Longer.

*Nobody.*

---

I keep looking at the word at the end of the line of numbers, until the line moves
up and disappears.

*Nobody* isn't an answer. Every report goes to someone. It goes to the chief
technician, which was Otávio, and he wasn't going to send it to himself; so it goes up, to the
head of operations. To Álvaro. A condition report on a stretch of the cable
goes to Álvaro, and Álvaro reads it, and decides.

And Álvaro said, at the Post, at the kitchen window: *I don't know what he saw*.

I feel something cold rise through my belly, under the notebook. Something I don't
want to feel and feel. The big hand over the visor white with ice. The shoulders
shaking without a sound. *I believe he did something very stupid, kid.* The
code rule, which he wrote, and which he owned at the hearing, on my side of the
table. *Nothing that's on the schedule.* The hesitation.

*Álvaro*, I write. Just that. My finger shaking on the stiff keyboard.

The answer comes quickly, this time.

*No. Wait. It wasn't anybody. Literally. Let me show you.*

---

She sends the report's routing log. Not the report: the path it took
inside the Operator's archive, step by step, with the time of each step. It arrives in
pieces, at the ends of twenty lines of numbers in a row, and I put it together on the screen, and
when I put it together, it's this:

*17 — 23:02 — received. Origin: technician Reis, O. Type: condition report with
manual measurement.*

*17 — 23:02 — forwarded to automatic triage.*

*17 — 23:03 — triage: comparison with robotic reading of stretch. Robotic reading:
modulus within tolerance (−0.16%). Manual measurement: modulus −0.7%. Divergence
between manual and robotic. Robotic prevails per standard. Manual measurement classified
as instrument error or noise.*

*17 — 23:03 — archived without forwarding. Reason: no confirmed anomaly.*

One minute. Otávio's report, with six measurements in four years and a sum in
pencil redone in the small hours and two numbers in a ruled box, lived for one
minute inside the Operator. It went in at eleven oh-two. It went out at eleven oh-three. Nobody
opened it.

*It never got to Álvaro*, Bia writes. *It never got to anybody. Triage compared it
with the robot, the robot said it was fine, and triage archived it. It's like that with everything.
There must be a thousand reports in there archived the same way. Álvaro never saw it.*

---

Minus zero point one six percent.

I read the robot's number again. The routine robot's, the one that goes over the stretch every
week, not the fine reader Álvaro had them use afterward. Zero point one six.
I measured zero point eight with the handheld reader. Otávio measured zero point seven. The
fine robots, the ones Álvaro sent, measured eight tenths, same as me.

The routine robot measured five times less.

And I understand, in the niche, at nine kilos, with my hand cold on the notebook, before
Bia explains it to me, because it's the kind of thing you learn in your first year and
forget in your second.

The routine robots don't read point by point. They read two meters at a time. They go along the
ribbon and average each two-meter stretch, because if they kept every point
the whole ribbon wouldn't fit anywhere, a hundred thousand kilometers of points. Two
meters of ribbon, one number. The average.

And in a two-meter average, forty centimeters at eight tenths turn into nothing. They turn into
a sixth of a percent. Noise. They vanish into the rest of the stretch, which is fine.

The robot didn't lie. It measured right, the way it was told to measure. Triage didn't lie.
It compared right, with the rule it was told to use: *robotic prevails*. Nobody
hid anything. Nobody read anything. Otávio's report spent one minute in a place
where nobody was and was put away in a drawer where nobody looks, by a rule
someone wrote twenty years ago, probably with good intentions, so that the cable wouldn't
stop every week because of some tired technician with a badly calibrated handheld
reader.

Bia writes: *I'm sorry. I thought there'd be someone to blame.*

So did I.

---

*There's one more thing*, Bia writes, half an hour later, when I'd already switched
off the screen.

I switch it on again.

*Since I was in there. The extras. I went to look at that week. Not on the schedule, they're not
on it. In the other log, the program's, the billing one, the one for the man who pays us. It's
the only place they show up, because somebody has to bill somebody.*

The line goes by. Another.

*Tuesday, 4th. Supplementary C-X9. Left Well 06:40. Full load. Destination
tip.*

*Tuesday.* The fourth. Six forty in the morning.

Five and a half hours after car M-3, with my code, at twelve past one.

On the same ribbon. In the same direction. Behind. With distance, so there's no trouble.

*I don't know if it means anything*, Bia writes. *But it was there. I loaded that one.
On Monday night. That's what I was leaving for when Otávio knocked on my
door.*

---

I don't sleep.

I sit in the niche with my back against the wall, at fourteen percent, with the screen
off and the notebook on my knees, and the climber climbing, and the hum of the ring in the
bone of my skull.

There's no one to blame. I repeat it to myself like a safety rule, the way
I used to repeat the things Otávio said in my first year. *There's no one to blame.* A robot
that averages two meters. A triage that sides with the robot. A twenty-year-old
rule. A program running late that sends a fourth climber a day because the heat doesn't
wait and five old people died in the still week. A man who pays on the side. My
friend, who loads on the side so her mother won't die like Mrs. Celeste. A boss who
never saw the report. A director who denied a reduction in front of everyone,
because he was right about the heat.

Nobody did anything wrong. Or everybody did a little, within tolerance.

Like the ribbon.

*We write things down*, Otávio used to say, *so that, when it goes wrong, nobody can
say they didn't know.*

He wrote it down. And now everybody can say they didn't know, and they'll be telling the
truth.

I rest my forehead on the closed notebook. The black cover is warm from my body. Outside,
the ribbon rises, and somewhere above me, ten thousand kilometers from here, just
below an empty Post, there are forty centimeters of ribbon at eight tenths, and two
turns of rope with the end cut, and a month and a bit.
