Bia lives in hull eleven, on the outer edge, with her mother.

I know the way by heart and walk it at night, along deck two, with the afternoon rain
still dripping from the gutters. It's Sunday. Bia doesn't work Sundays. For eighteen days
she hasn't answered my messages, she's turned her back at belt three,
she's vanished when I come into a corridor. For eighteen days I've let her. Tonight, no.

The door in hull eleven is metal painted blue, peeling at the edges. I knock.

Her mother opens it. Mrs. Rute is seventy-nine, with hands twisted by
rheumatism, and she's always called me *sweetheart*, since the first time I
slept there, at twenty-five, after a climb when Bia and I got
stuck at the Post by a solar storm. She sees me and breaks into a smile that doesn't
match Bia's face, which appears behind her, at the end of the hallway, and stops.

"Sweetheart," Mrs. Rute says. "Come in, it's an oven out there."

It isn't an oven. The still week ended a week ago. But inside it's
cool, and I feel it on my skin, and I understand why before I see it: on the living room wall, in the
place where Mrs. Rute's old cooler used to be, the one that made the noise of a
boat engine, there's a new one. White, slim, silent, with a little green light and
a small number on the screen. Twenty-six degrees.

The quota for hull eleven is twenty-nine, like seven's.

"Pretty, isn't it?" Mrs. Rute says, following my eyes. "Bia bought it. The week
before the heat. If it weren't for that, I don't know."

I think of Mrs. Celeste in her armchair, with the paper fan in her lap.

Bia, at the end of the hallway, closes her eyes.

---

We talk in the kitchen, with the door shut, and Mrs. Rute in the living room watching
an old soap opera on her wrist with the sound up too loud.

Bia sits on one side of the table and I sit on the other, like in room 14. She doesn't look at
me. She looks at her own hands, which are on the table, one squeezing the other.

"Do you know what one of those coolers costs?" she asks.

"I know."

"Three months' pay. Mine." She laughs, without humor. "I didn't have it. Nobody on the
deck has it."

"Bia."

"I'm working on the side." She says it all at once, like pulling off a bandage.
"Since the start of the year. For the sunshade program. There's a man, a
contractor, who isn't from the Operator, who pays cash, off the
books. To load the extras."

"What extras?"

"The climbers that aren't on the schedule." She finally raises her eyes. "The
program is behind, Iara. Further behind than they say. Hugo has a target,
two percent, and a date, and he's not going to make the date with three climbers a day.
So they send a fourth. In the small hours, sometimes. Off-schedule. In the same
direction as the others, behind, with distance, so there's no trouble. And they don't log it.
And someone has to load it, off shift, without showing up in anybody's records." She
points at her own chest. "Me."

I just look at her.

*Nothing that's on the schedule*, Álvaro said, in the Post's kitchen. And hesitated.

"Does Álvaro know?" I ask.

"I don't know. I think he suspects. Everybody on the deck suspects. Nobody asks."
She squeezes her hands. "The week Otávio went up, I worked on the side
three nights. Monday, Tuesday and Thursday. On Thursday, until four in the morning."

Thursday. The sixth. The night his dosimeter reared up at ten oh-four.

"That's why you disappeared," I say.

"That's why I disappeared." Bia doesn't cry. Bia never cries, and I can see she's a
hair away from crying. "The agency was going to ask where everyone was. I had nowhere
to have been. I was loading a climber that doesn't exist, for a man who doesn't
exist, for money that doesn't exist. If I told, I'd lose my job. And my
mother would lose her cooler, because I'm still paying for it." She stops.
"And if I didn't tell, I was the person who was awake and at the Well the night
he died, without explaining why."

"You thought I'd suspect you?"

"I thought you'd ask me." She looks at me. "And that I'd have to lie to
you. And I didn't want to lie to you, Iara. So I turned my back."

---

I stay quiet a long time. In the living room, Mrs. Rute's old soap opera has a woman
crying and violin music underneath.

"There's one more thing," Bia says.

She gets up. She goes to the kitchen cabinet, the top one, the one with the pans nobody
uses, and takes from the very back, behind a stack of frying pans, a package. A package
wrapped in clear plastic, the thick plastic for packing parts, sealed
with gray tape, the same gray tape as the crooked patch on his shoulder.

She puts it on the table, in front of me.

I know what it is before I touch it. By the size. By the thickness. By the black cover
you can see through the plastic.

The 2110 notebook.

---

"He came here," Bia says. "On Monday. The third. Late at night. Eleven
forty, more or less. I was leaving for the side shift, and he was at the
door, with this in his hand."

Eleven forty. Twenty-six minutes after calling me.

"He was strange. Not strange-strange. He was too calm. You know how
he was when he was too calm."

I know. When he was too calm it was because he had already decided something.

"He gave me this and said: *if I'm not back by Monday, give it to Iara. Only to
Iara. Nobody else.* I asked back from where. He said he was going to check something
up there. I said he was on leave, that he was going to the mainland. He laughed."
Bia stops. "I asked why he didn't give it to you himself. You live ten
minutes from here."

"And him?"

"He said he'd called you and you hadn't answered." Bia looks at me, and
now there's something on her face that isn't guilt. It's pity, the same as Inês's. "And that
you'd come off a fourteen-hour shift. And that he wasn't going to wake you."

I don't say anything.

"He said it like this: *she's tired, let her sleep. Monday I'll give it to her myself.*"

---

I can't open the package.

I stay with my hands on the plastic, feeling the cover of the notebook underneath, and I
can't, and Bia doesn't say anything, and in the living room the soap ends and another
begins, and Mrs. Rute laughs at something.

He didn't wake me.

Since room 14 I've thought he called to ask for my code, and
that I didn't answer, and that that's why he went up with the code without asking, and that that's why I'm
the person who made all of it possible. It's what legal thinks. It's what I thought.

And it's true. And it isn't only that. He called, and I didn't answer, and he didn't insist. He didn't
call again. He didn't knock on my door, which was ten minutes away. He left nine
seconds of wind and my name, and went to Bia, and gave her the notebook, and said
*let her sleep*.

He didn't wake me because I was tired.

He went up with my code, without asking, on the climber of a man who isn't from the Operator,
who was paying my friend on the side, in a week when nobody knows who else was on the cable, and
he died up there, and before he died he tied himself on with an extra turn for me to find.
And, on the last night when I could have talked to him, he chose to let me
sleep.

I cry in Bia's kitchen the way I cried at five thousand kilometers, with my hands on
the plastic, and Bia comes around the table and hugs me from behind, the way
we used to hug at the Post when the solar storm wouldn't pass, and stays there.

---

"Why didn't you give it to me before?" I ask, later, when I can.

"Because he said *if I'm not back by Monday*." Bia sits down again. "And on
Monday, the tenth, he hadn't come back, and nobody knew where he was, and everybody
thought he was on the mainland. I thought he'd really gone.
That he'd changed his mind. I was going to give it to you. I swear I was. And then, on Wednesday, you
found him."

"And then you got scared."

"And then I got scared. Because if I gave it to you, I'd have to say I was
awake on Monday at eleven forty, leaving for a shift that doesn't exist. And
on Thursday, until four." She pushes the package a little closer to me.
"I'm a coward, Iara. I know I am. But I kept it. I didn't open it. I didn't read it. It's the
way he gave it to me."

I look at the gray tape. It's intact. Stuck on crooked, the way he stuck things.

"You're not a coward," I say. "You were scared of losing your mother."

Bia doesn't answer. In the living room, Mrs. Rute calls: *Bia, come see, it's that actress
who died*. Bia doesn't go.

---

"Are you going to tell the agency?" she asks. "About the extras?"

I think of Inês, in room 14, separating what I know from what I think. I think of
Álvaro hesitating: *nothing that's on the schedule*. I think of Hugo at the edge of the Well, *I
was too busy being right*, and the reduction to two a day that he denied. And
I think of something I don't want to think and think anyway: a climber that doesn't exist,
going up in the small hours on the same ribbon, in the same week, behind a maintenance
car with my code.

In the same direction. With distance. So there's no trouble.

"I don't know," I say. It's the truth. "First I need to read this."

Bia nods.

"And Iara." She hesitates. "You're suspended. You can't go up. You can't even go near the
Well at night. If you want to go and see what he saw..."

"I didn't say I want to."

"You don't need to say it." Bia almost smiles, for the first time in eighteen days.
"I load climbers that don't exist. I know how to put something inside one
without anyone seeing."

---

I carry the notebook to hull seven under my shirt, pressed against my belly, like
something stolen, along deck two, in the fine night rain.

At home, I sit on the floor of my room and cut the gray tape with the hook knife,
carefully, along the side, so as not to ruin the tape. The plastic opens. The notebook falls into my
lap.

Black cover. Thick. On the spine, in his handwriting: *2110*.

I open it to the first page. He wrote the date above each measurement, and the stretch,
and the time, and the numbers, and sometimes a short sentence beside them, in pencil, the dots on his i's crooked.

I don't read the measurements. Not yet. I turn the pages to the last one written.

It's from Monday, the third. There's no measurement. There's a single sentence, in the middle of the page,
in bigger letters than the others, like someone writing to be read by someone else.

*Iara. Start with the stretch just below the Post. A hundred and eighty meters. You'll
see.*

Underneath, the extra turn. Not the knot: a drawing of the knot, in pencil, small, with the turn
on the inside of the loop. Like a signature.
