Otávio weighed seventy kilos. Here, he weighs three.

With the suit, five and change. But I say three, because it's him I'm
carrying, not the suit, and because if I say five I'll have to think about the suit,
and I don't want to think about the suit yet.

Three kilos. A bag of rice. A small child. Less than my shift
backpack. I hold him by the waist, with my left arm, and with my right I pull the
lifeline, and we go down the ribbon together to the car, the two of us in white against
the dark, and he weighs nothing, and I can't bear it.

The car is three meters by two. He fits. I lay him on the back seat and climb the
two hundred meters to the Post, and at the place where he was the ring gives a jolt,
passing over the two turns of rope.

"Iara, control. What's your situation?"

"Going up to the Post. With him."

"The guidance is not to move the body until..."

"The guidance is not to leave anyone outside. It's in the manual. Page one."

I don't know if it is. It must be. Control goes quiet, and I know that behind
control someone is asking someone, and that someone is going to ask Álvaro,
and that Álvaro is going to say: let her.

"Let her," says Álvaro's voice on the channel, half a second later. "Iara. Take him to the Post's cold room. Don't take off the suit. Don't touch anything
you don't need to touch. I'm boarding in forty minutes. I'll be there in two
days and nineteen hours."

"I know how long it takes."

"I know you know."

He drops off the channel. Otávio, in his place, would have said one more thing.
When I complained that he was explaining what I already knew, he'd say: *I'm saying
it for me.*

---

Before going up, I had to untie him.

I didn't untie him. I stayed a while with my gloves over the knot, on the ring at his
waist, the gray rope going twice around the ribbon and coming back, and the extra turn
on the inside of the loop, the one that holds nothing. And I couldn't. Not because I didn't know how:
a bowline comes undone with one hand, that's what it's for. I couldn't
because undoing it was erasing it. He had made that knot with his own hands, inside
gloves, twenty thousand kilometers from anyone, and the knot was the last thing
he had done in the world that I knew for certain he had done.

So I cut the rope on both sides, close to the ribbon, away from the knot. With the
hook knife we carry on the thigh for cutting tangled line. The two turns stayed on the
ribbon. The knot stayed where it was, on the ring at his waist, whole, with the length
of rope that was left swinging, and I wound that length around the ring so it wouldn't
come free.

Whoever wants to undo it will have to come up here and undo it.

---

The Post's cold room is a room two meters by three, at the back of the module,
behind the pantry, where we keep ribbon samples and frozen food and
spare parts that don't like heat. Twenty below. The door is
heavy, with a lever latch, the old kind, and when I open it a white cloud comes out
that doesn't fall, because here nothing falls fast: it stays in the air, still, and then
sinks slowly, like the torque wrench in my first year.

I lay Otávio down on the floor of the room. I don't need to lay him down: he'd stay standing if I
let him, leaning against the wall, because he weighs three kilos and the wall holds three
kilos with a fingertip. But I lay him down. On his back, his arms at his
sides. I arrange the arms. I arrange the legs. I don't know why I do it, and I do it.

Then I sit on the floor, next to him, with my suit still on and my helmet in my
lap, and I stay there.

The air in the room is so cold it hurts my nose. I breathe it anyway. Otávio doesn't
breathe. His visor is white on the inside, crystallized at the edges, with a
thin layer of ice that was the last moisture of his body, and that left the body and
became this, a lace of ice on the glass, and stayed.

I want to see his face. I don't open the visor. Álvaro said not to touch anything, and
Álvaro is right, and besides I don't know what I'll see if I open it, and I don't want to
know yet.

"Hi," I say.

My voice in the cold room makes a small sound, muffled, that the ice on the walls
eats.

My wrist buzzes, inside the glove.

I take the glove off and look, by reflex, the way you look when someone calls. The
band on my wrist is soft glass, two fingers wide, and warm when it's working; in
here, idle, it had gone cold against the skin. Outside, with no network, it's just a
watch.

It's a message. The
sender is a line I've never seen written that way, because nobody writes it
that way: *Otávio Reis — suit 4471*. Below, in gray: *delivered late. File
damaged.*

Suits hold on to what they can't send. When the person is out of
range of the channel, the suit keeps the message and sends it the first time it finds a
network. His suit found one when I brought him inside, six days later.

I touch the file. The wrist thinks for a moment and says: *unable to open*.
I touch it again. *Unable to open.* It's forty-one seconds long. It won't open.

I keep looking at those words until they stop meaning anything, and then I close my hand
around my wrist, hard, as if you could hold on to a file by squeezing.

"You know I'm going to have to do the math," I tell him. "You know. You
taught me."

---

I do the math out loud. He liked it when I did it out loud.

At twenty thousand kilometers up I'm twenty-six thousand four hundred from the
center of the Earth. The ribbon turns with the Earth, once a day, and at that distance
from the center once a day is one kilometer and nine hundred meters per second.
It sounds like a lot. It isn't. To stay in orbit here, without a cable, without anything, a thing
would need three kilometers and nine hundred per second. Twice as much.

So if something comes loose from the ribbon here, it doesn't stay. It's moving
at half the speed it would need. The Earth pulls, and it falls. It doesn't fall
straight, it falls in a curve, an ellipse whose lowest point is inside the Earth, which
means it never reaches the lowest point: before that, it enters the air.
And it comes down faster and faster: a little over two hours after it's let go, when
it reaches the air, it's already moving at almost ten kilometers per second, and it
enters the air the way a stone enters a window, and burns.

Let go here, a body doesn't stay. Let go here, a body becomes that streak of light that
somebody on the deck of the Anchor will think is pretty.

"And you stayed," I say.

He stayed. Tied. With a rope that isn't the lifeline.

The lifeline. I look at the ring at his waist again, and now I see
what I hadn't seen outside: the lifeline's carabiner is there, clipped to the
loop on the right side, closed, with the screw lock tight, but with no
line. The line was cut, or it snapped, a hand's width from the carabiner. The end is
frayed. It isn't a knife cut, clean, like mine. It's something else. A ragged
end, with the fibers splayed out in a fan, as if it had been rubbed until it
gave, or pulled until it broke.

So he was held by the lifeline, like everyone. And the line ran out.
And he took the emergency rope from his leg, went twice around the ribbon and tied the knot.

With how much time?

---

The tear is on the left thigh.

I only find it because I'm looking. No one would find it without looking. It's small: two
centimeters, maybe less, on the outside of the thigh, where the outer fabric of the
suit, the white, is thickest. It looks like a scratch. I run my fingertip
over it and feel the edge.

The edge is turned inward.

It's the first thing Otávio taught me about tears in a suit, in my second month,
with an old suit hanging on the wall of the Anchor's workshop and a screwdriver
in his hand: *look at the edge. The edge tells you where it came from. If the suit burst from the inside
out, the edge opens outward, like a flower. If something went in, the edge
sinks.* And he pushed the screwdriver into the fabric so I could see. The edge sank.

This one sank. Something went in. From outside.

I don't know what. A grain of something, out of the millions that cross the cable every
day, pebbles, chips, the remains of satellites that broke apart a hundred years ago, that
we just call *dust* and that sometimes isn't dust. A tip of metal. A
corner. Something I don't know what it is. The edge doesn't say what. It only says where from.

And it says something else, which I understand sitting on the floor of the cold room with my knees
against my chest, doing another sum, the one Otávio never taught me because
he never needed to:

a big tear, and the person blacks out in ten seconds, fifteen. The air goes all
at once, the water in the mouth and eyes boils, the blood stops, and in a minute and a half
there's no coming back. With a big tear, nobody ties any knot.

A small tear is something else. A small tear is a leak. The suit feels the
pressure dropping and opens the reserve, and the reserve pushes air in as fast
as the air goes out, for a while, and the while depends on the size of the hole and the size
of the reserve. Minutes. Ten, fifteen, for a hole this size. But minutes.

Minutes is enough to tie a knot. Enough to tie a knot with an extra turn.

I open the reserve panel, on the side of his helmet, with my hands shaking,
and the needle is on zero. Used up.

He used the whole reserve. He stayed outside until the air ran out. And, at some
moment in those minutes, knowing, he stopped trying to get back to the Post, which was
a hundred and eighty meters away. Without a line, a hundred and eighty meters hand
over hand along the edge of the ribbon are a hundred and eighty meters to slip once,
and once, here, is the streak of light. He took the rope.

---

His dosimeter isn't like mine.

Mine is the new one, a window of green numbers that counts in real time and sends
everything to control. His is the old one, the crystal-strip kind, which sends nothing
to anyone: it keeps. It keeps the dose the way a tree trunk keeps the years, in layers, and
can only be read on the reader in the workshop. Otávio refused to change it. He said a
dosimeter that talks to control is a dosimeter control can silence.

That I never understood either.

The Post has a reader. In the workshop, next to the bench. I take the dosimeter off his
chest, unscrewing it carefully, and carry it, and put it in the reader, and the reader
takes four minutes to draw on the screen the curve, in gray, of the dose
Otávio received over the last fifteen days.

The curve is almost flat for days. Low. It's the dose of someone who's shielded: on the
ground, on the Anchor, or inside the car, behind the shielding. Then it rises a little,
gradually, for two and a half days, which is the climb, the car passing through the
belts with the shielding taking the worst of it. Then it goes flat again, higher,
for a few hours: the Post, which protects, but not as much.

And then it rears up. It climbs straight, steep, the way dose climbs when the
person leaves the shielding and stays outside, in the belt, with nothing between them and
the sky but the suit. And it doesn't stop. It climbs almost straight for six days, the same, without
changing, until the moment I took the dosimeter off his chest.

The dosimeter doesn't know whether the person is alive. It only knows they're outside.

I look at the date where the curve rears up. The reader marks it underneath, in small type.

Thursday. Twenty-two oh-four.

Thursday morning, Otávio was supposed to be on the ship. Everyone said so on the
channel. A week of leave on the mainland, the first in two years. The Thursday
ship sails at seven.

At twenty-two oh-four on Thursday, Otávio left the Post,
twenty thousand kilometers up, and didn't come back.

---

I go back to the cold room.

The white air comes out again when I open the door, and hangs still in the air.
Otávio is where I left him, on his back, his arms arranged, his visor white
with ice. The knot at his waist. The length of rope wound around the ring. The carabiner with no
line. The tear in the thigh, its edge turned in.

I sit on the floor beside him again.

*Everyone thinks it's a column*, he used to say, in my first week, on the deck, showing me
the ribbon that rose from the middle of the Anchor and vanished. *It isn't. The cable
doesn't push anything. It's pulled from both sides, and it stays in the middle.*

I'm sitting on the floor of a room at twenty below, next to a
man who weighs three kilos and whom I can't carry, and for the first time
I understand that it isn't the weight. Three kilos is nothing. What I can't bear is the other side. It's being stretched between two things that
pull: Otávio, here, dead, tied with the extra turn; and something I
don't know, that took him outside at ten at night on a Thursday when
he was supposed to be on a ship.

The part of my head that nobody switched off does one last sum, the simplest.

If he had come loose, no one would have found him. If someone had let him go,
no one would have found him. If the line had snapped and he had done
nothing, no one would have found him. Of all the ways that night could end, only one
left Otávio on the cable, a hundred and eighty meters below the Post, waiting.

He wanted to stay.

Or someone wanted him to stay. But who? And why a knot that only he tied?

"You wanted to," I tell him. "Didn't you?"

The white visor doesn't answer. The ice on the walls eats my voice.

"Iara, control." It's the channel, in my ear, low, careful, as if talking
to someone in a hospital. "Álvaro has boarded. He gets in Saturday night. The
agency investigator is coming with him. You need to rest."

"I am resting," I say.

And I stay sitting on the floor of the cold room, next to Otávio, until the cold comes through the
suit and I can't feel my hands anymore, and even so I don't leave, because someone has to
stay with him, and because, if I get up, I'll have to go and sleep in the module where he
slept three thousand nights, and I still can't.
