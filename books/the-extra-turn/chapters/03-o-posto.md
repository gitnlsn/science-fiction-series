I spend three days alone with him at the Post, and I don't sleep in his bunk once.

The Post has four bunks, two on each side of the corridor in the sleeping module,
each with a curtain of thick cloth and a small reading light. His is the
bottom one, on the left, the one closest to the hatch. It always was. Nobody slept there,
not even when he was down on the Anchor, months without coming up. It wasn't a rule. It was
something everyone knew without anyone having said it, the way you know which chair
your grandfather sits in.

I sleep in the top one, on the right. The farthest.

The first night, I open his curtain. I don't know why. To see. It's the
way he left it: the blanket folded in four at the foot of the mattress, with the fold
turned in; the reading light turned to the wall, because he said light in
your face wakes you up before it's time; a pair of wool socks rolled up in the corner, next to
the pillow. And, taped to the inside wall, at eye level
for someone lying down, a small piece of paper with something written in pencil that I had never
seen, because I had never opened that curtain.

*Tension, not weight.*

That's all. In his handwriting, slanted, with the dot on the i crooked.

I close the curtain. I don't open it again.

---

For three days, I work.

I have nothing to do and I work anyway, because it's what I know. I do the
panel check, which didn't need doing. I do the air reserve check, which didn't
need doing. I change a filter in the workshop that still had three months of life.
I put the tools on the straps in the equipment bay in order by
size, and then I undo it and put them in order by use, which is how he left them, and which is more
right. At the back of the bay, a tarp covers something the size of a suitcase. I go
to straighten the tarp and it won't lift: whatever is under it weighs more than
anything in there has a right to weigh. Otávio showed me once what it was, in my
second year. I smooth the tarp over it and leave it.

Twice a day, I open the cold room. The white air comes out, hangs, sinks. He's
there, on his back, his arms arranged. I don't go in. I stay at the door. I say
good morning, in the morning, and good night, at night, and close it. Near midnight
the Post goes into the Earth's shadow, and for an hour and fifty minutes the light
inside switches to battery, a shade more yellow. Near the equinoxes it's every night.
The rest of the year, never.

Control speaks to me every six hours, in the careful hospital voice. I
answer that I'm fine. Once, on the second day, it's Bia who speaks, on the service
channel, in the middle of the night, when she shouldn't be on shift.

"Iara."

"Hi, Bia."

"I heard."

"I know you heard."

She goes quiet. Bia doesn't know how to be quiet, and she is. I hear, behind her, the
noise of the Anchor's cargo deck, the belts, someone shouting a number.

"I wish I was there," she says.

"I know."

"Iara." A pause. "Did he tell you anything? Before?"

I don't understand the question. I don't understand the tone, which is strange, the tone of someone
asking one thing and wanting to know another.

"No," I say. "I didn't talk to him."

And it's true. I didn't. He called and I didn't answer.

Bia says she has to get back to the deck, and hangs up, and I sit looking at the empty
channel, with the feeling that there was something in that conversation that wasn't
about me.

---

Álvaro's car docks at the Post on Saturday, at nine at night.

I hear it before I see it: the rumble of the ring's wheels changing pitch on the ribbon, down
below, and then the dull thud, which you feel in your feet and not in your ears,
of the car locking onto the docking collar. The lower hatch turns. The air
hisses.

Álvaro comes up first.

He's big. He was always the widest of all of us, with the back of someone who carried
heavy parts his whole life before he had an office, and with a mechanic's
hands, thick, the nails cut short. He's fifty-eight, with gray
hair cut short, and on the decks of the Anchor he walks slowly, in a way
that makes it seem the deck is asking his permission to be stepped on. Here, at four
percent, he looks like something else. He comes through the hatch floating, a little clumsy,
like a bear someone let loose in water.

He sees me. He stops.

I don't know what to do with my arms. He does. He hugs me. A big hug, a
bear's, that up here weighs nothing and even so squeezes all of me, and I smell
him, bearing oil and cheap soap, which has been the smell of the Anchor's
workshop since I was twenty-two.

"Kid," he says, into my hair. He doesn't say anything else.

Behind him, through the hatch, comes a woman I don't know.

---

"Where is he?" Álvaro asks.

I show him. He goes alone. He doesn't ask me to go with him, and I don't go. I stay in the
corridor, and through the small window in the cold room door I watch him go into the
white air, and kneel on the floor, beside Otávio, with his hand on the
wall. And stay there.

I don't hear anything. The door is thick. I watch Álvaro put his open hand over the
visor white with ice, covering Otávio's whole face with his palm, the way you
put your hand on the forehead of someone with a fever. I watch his shoulders, those broad
shoulders, start to shake. Without a sound. Shaking, stopping, shaking.

Thirty years. They built this cable together, the two of them, when the ribbon was still
being lowered from the Station, stretch by stretch, and they were two young men in
orange coveralls who slept in bunks like these, in a temporary Post that
doesn't even exist anymore. Otávio told the stories. Álvaro never told them; he stood by,
listening, and sometimes corrected a date.

I turn my face away. It isn't for me to see.

---

The woman's name is Inês Garrido and she's from the safety agency.

She's about fifty, dark hair pulled back, and a calm that isn't the calm of
someone who is calm: it's the calm of someone who decided, at some point in her life, to show nothing
while she's working. She wears the agency's gray coveralls with the sleeves
rolled twice, always twice. While Álvaro is in the cold room, she
walks around the Post with a small device in her hand, the size of a pack of cards,
pointing it at things: the equipment bay, the workshop, the dosimeter
reader, the exit hatch. The device makes no sound. She doesn't ask
anything. She looks.

Then she sits at the Post's kitchen table, which is a table fixed to the floor with four
chairs fixed to the floor, and asks me to sit across from her.

"Iara," she says. She says my name like someone checking it's right. "I'm going to ask you
questions. You've answered some of them for control already. I'll ask them again. It isn't
distrust. It's method."

"All right."

She asks everything. The time I saw the object on the ribbon. The distance. Whether I touched
the body before reporting. Whether I touched the knot. I tell her I cut the rope on both
sides, close to the ribbon, away from the knot, and why, and she notes something on the device without changing her face. Whether I
took the dosimeter off his chest. I tell her yes, and that I read the curve, and I show her on
the workshop screen.

She looks at the curve for a long time.

"Twenty-two oh-four," she says.

"On Thursday."

"The ship sailed at seven."

"Without him."

She raises her eyes to me for the first time since she started.

"That you don't know."

"I know he was here at ten at night."

"You know his dosimeter was here." She says it without any
malice, like a teacher correcting a student's sum. "That's what I need to
separate, Iara. What you know, from what you think. Both things matter. But
they're not the same."

---

Álvaro comes back from the cold room with red eyes and sits in the third
chair. He puts his hands on the table, one on top of the other, and keeps looking at
them.

"The preliminary is accident," Inês says, to both of us. "I'll tell you why, and
you correct me where I'm wrong, because I'm not from the cable. He was
outside. His lifeline wore through on the edge of the ribbon and broke. A dust
impact, or something, opened a small hole in the suit. He ran
out of air. It's a sequence that happens. It's happened twice on this cable, in twenty
years."

"Three times," Álvaro says, quietly, without taking his eyes off his hands.

"Three."

"And the knot?" I ask.

"He tied himself on with the emergency rope so he wouldn't fall. Isn't that what you're taught?"

"It is. But he used a knot only he tied. With an extra turn."

"And what does that mean?"

I don't know. I open my mouth and I don't know. It's a letter I know and can't read.

"I don't know yet," I say.

Inês makes a note, without changing her face.

"And what was he doing here, at ten at night on a Thursday when he was supposed
to be on a ship?" I ask.

"That I want to know too," she says. "It's the part that isn't accident." And she turns
to Álvaro. "Who came up with him?"

Álvaro raises his head.

"No one."

"No one goes up alone, I was told. It's a rule."

"It's a rule." Álvaro tries to smile, and the smile comes out crooked. "*Up there,
nobody falls alone.* He was the one who said it. Every rookie heard it from Otávio on the
first day. He said it just like that, *nobody falls alone*, and everyone
thought it was about company." He stops. "And he went up alone."

---

Inês wants to know who else was on the cable that week. Álvaro explains, with
the patience of someone who has explained this to a lot of people who weren't from the cable.

"It's a single ribbon, ma'am. One meter wide. The climbers travel clamped to it.
In the same direction, they go one behind the other, with distance. In opposite
directions, there's no way for one to get past the other. They don't cross on the
ribbon. Only at the sidings: a collar every five thousand kilometers, the Post's two
collars, the Station. Whoever's coming down waits at the collar until the convoy going
up has passed. That's why there's the schedule. It's the most important thing
we do, the schedule. Everything that goes up and down is on it."

"And who was on the schedule?"

"Routine maintenance, which was Iara, going up Sunday night. The
sunshade's cargo climbers, which go up every day, two, three a day, in
convoy, in the same direction. And nothing else." He hesitates. "Nothing that's on the
schedule."

Inês notices the hesitation. I watch her notice. She doesn't say anything. She makes a note.

"Otávio had a riddle," Álvaro says, suddenly, to no one, looking
at the window, where the Earth is the size of an open hand. "*Whoever climbs
slowly gets there faster than the cable.* He asked every rookie. Nobody
got it."

"And what does it mean?" Inês asks.

Álvaro looks at me. I know the answer. He knows I know.

"It means the cable turns with the Earth," I say. "And that the higher up,
the faster it moves sideways, because the circle is bigger. Whoever's climbing is always
going slower than the piece of cable just above them. So the cable has
to keep pulling us sideways, pushing, so we keep up. We're
never at the speed of the place we're going to. We always arrive late."

"And why *faster*?"

"Because he said it wrong on purpose," Álvaro says. "To see who was thinking."

Inês looks at the two of us. Then she makes a note. I don't know what she writes.

---

Inês goes to sleep at two in the morning, in the top bunk on the left. She doesn't ask
whose the bottom one is. She doesn't need to.

Álvaro and I stay at the kitchen window. The Earth is almost all in darkness,
with its blue edge lit on one side, and the ribbon comes out from under the Post and goes down, and vanishes,
and somewhere down there, in the middle of a sea we can't see, nine thousand
people are sleeping on top of a city that floats.

"I owe you something," Álvaro says.

"You don't owe me anything."

"I do." He doesn't look at me. He looks at the ribbon. "He wanted you to have
his post. Chief technician. He told me so last year. He said: *when
I stop, it's her*. I said he was never going to stop. He laughed."

I don't say anything. I can't.

"The post is yours," Álvaro says. "Whenever you want. Not now. Whenever you
want."

He says it in good faith. I know it's in good faith, because Álvaro doesn't know how to say
anything any other way. And even so something in me closes when he says it,
the way a hand closes when someone offers it something that belongs to someone else.

"Álvaro. Do you believe it was an accident?"

He takes a while. He looks at the ribbon for a long time.

"I believe Inês is going to write accident," he says. "And that it's the most
likely. And that he went up alone, without being on the schedule, against the rule he
made up himself, in a week when he was supposed to be on a ship." He rubs his eyes
with the back of his hand, that mechanic's hand. "I believe he did something very
stupid, kid. And I don't know why. And that I'm never going to forgive you
for, Otávio."
