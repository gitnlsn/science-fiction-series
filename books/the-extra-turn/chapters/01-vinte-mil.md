At twenty thousand kilometers, I weigh two point nine kilos.

That isn't a figure of speech. The car shows it in the corner of the panel, in small type,
because someone at the Operator thought the technicians would like to know: *apparent
mass 2.9 kg*. I weigh sixty-six down below, on the Anchor, barefoot, after
breakfast. Up here the Earth still pulls at me, but the cable is turning with it, and
the turn it makes throws me outward almost as hard as the Earth calls me back.
This is what's left: four percent of me. Enough for me to know where the floor of the
car is. Not enough to hurt myself if I fall on it.

Otávio used to say it's the best height on the cable for thinking. Down below you weigh
too much to think straight, and at the Station you weigh nothing, and without weight
your head won't settle anywhere. Here, he said, we have only the weight we need.

I've been climbing for two days and nineteen hours. The maintenance car is a
box three meters by two, held to the ribbon inside a wheel ring that
grips the cable from both sides and climbs at three hundred kilometers an hour, which up here
doesn't feel like any speed at all, because nothing is going past the window. Only the
ribbon. A dark band, one meter wide, thinner than a sheet of
paper, that comes out from under the car and goes down, and down, and vanishes at a point very far away,
down where the Earth is.

The Earth, from here, is the size of an open hand at arm's length. A little
bigger. Twenty-eight degrees of sky. I did the math once, in my first year,
and showed it to Otávio, proud of myself, and he looked at the math and then looked out the
window and said: *sure, but look*. I look. Blue and white and a patch of brown at the
edge, where it's day. The rest is night, and in the night there are lights, a few, yellow,
scattered along the coast of a continent I've never visited.

Somewhere down there, on the sea, without a single light I could see from here,
is the Anchor, with its nine thousand residents and the coffee I drink barefoot.

---

The first time I came up this far, I was twenty-two and I threw up twice
before the first thousand kilometers.

It wasn't motion sickness. It was fear. I had spent my whole childhood on the
deck of the Anchor watching the ribbon vanish into the sky, and I had never thought about what it would be like
to be inside that dark line, climbing, with the sea getting smaller and smaller
below and then no longer being sea and turning into a color. Otávio was sitting
in the front seat of the car with his legs crossed, eating an apple. He handed me
a bag. He didn't say anything. He finished the apple.

At the Post, after three days, when I'd stopped throwing up and had started
to think I knew something, he took me to the equipment bay, which
is the biggest room in the Post, with the walls covered in tools held by
elastic straps. He took a torque wrench off the wall, a heavy wrench, a kilo,
and held it at shoulder height.

"Pay attention," he said. And let go.

The wrench didn't fall. I mean, it fell. But it fell like a thing that's thinking
about whether to fall. It stayed in the air a moment, still, and then it started down, so
slowly that I had time to crouch, to reach out my hand, to watch it
coming down, and to catch it in the air before it reached the floor, which was a meter
and a half below.

"Everything falls here," Otávio said. "Just slowly. Don't you ever forget that.
You'll think you're floating, because you don't weigh anything. You're not. The Earth keeps
calling you. If you let go of the cable, you don't stay here. You fall, slowly at first,
and then not so slowly, and down there you turn into a streak of light that somebody on the
deck will think is pretty."

I stood there with the wrench in my hand, not knowing what to say.

"And if I'm higher up?" I asked. "At the Station?"

"There you don't fall. There you stay." He took the wrench back and hooked it on the
strap. "And higher than that, past the Station, you fall outward. Away.
Forever. Every height on the cable has a different fall, Iara. The job is
knowing which one you're in."

I was twenty-two and I wrote that down in a notebook the way you write down a safety
rule. It took me twelve years to understand that he was also talking about
something else, and I still don't really know what.

---

"Control, Iara. Post at four hundred meters, docking speed."

"Iara, control. Copy. Welcome to twenty thousand."

The voice arrives with a delay you can't notice, because twenty thousand kilometers, for
light, is nothing: a tenth of a second, there and back. Otávio used to say that was
the strangest thing of all. That we're at a distance nobody
can imagine, and even so the voice of whoever's on the ground arrives as if they
were across the table.

The Post appears at the top of the window. It's a white cylinder, fat, fastened to the ribbon
like a thick knot on a string, with its panels open to the Sun on both
sides and its position lights blinking red and green. It's the only place between
the ground and the Station where anyone can really sleep. I've slept there some
two hundred nights in my life. Otávio, some three thousand.

I slow down. The wheels of the ring change their sound against the ribbon, from a
hum to a low rumble, and the car decelerates without my feeling it. I only know it has
decelerated because the number on the panel goes down.

And then the panel beeps.

It isn't the alarm. It's the other sound, the short one, the one for *object at edge*. The forward
reader, which sweeps the cable above the car, has found something that shouldn't be
there, fastened to the cable, a hundred and eighty meters below the Post, and has stopped
counting to show me.

I look at the image.

It's a dark smudge, the size of a person, at the edge of the ribbon, outside the
wheel track.

---

I stop the car at twenty meters.

I don't ask for authorization. I should. The protocol says an object on the ribbon gets reported, gets
photographed, and you wait for control to decide. I report. I photograph. But I don't
wait, because I already know, before I know, and the body knows before the head, and my
two-point-nine-kilo body is putting on the suit before I decide
to put it on.

The suit is white and gray, with the dosimeter on the chest, a small window of
green numbers that counts the radiation I've taken since I left the Anchor. At
twenty thousand kilometers, the ribbon passes through the edge of the outer belt, and the
dosimeter counts faster. Every exit has a limit. Today I have an hour
and ten before the number turns yellow.

I clip the lifeline's carabiner to the car's ring. I open the hatch.

There's no noise. There never is. The air in the chamber leaves in a breath that I feel on my
gloves and don't hear, and then it's the usual silence, the silence that isn't the silence
of a room, it's something else, it's the absence of anything that could carry a sound. I go out.

The cable is in front of me. One meter wide, dark, matte, with no shine
at all. Seen up close it doesn't look like a ribbon: it looks like a cut in the sky. I reach out my
hand and touch it with the glove, the way I touch it every time, because Otávio touched it.
He read the ribbon with his palm, and that's why we call an exit a glove. It's hard. It isn't rigid, but it's hard, stretched between two forces I can't
imagine, the Earth pulling down and the counterweight, a hundred thousand kilometers from here,
pulling out.

*The cable doesn't hold weight*, he used to say. *It holds tension.*

I go up the ribbon, hand over hand, pulling the lifeline. I don't need
strength. I weigh almost nothing. It's more swimming than climbing.

Twenty meters. Fifteen.

The dark smudge has arms.

---

It's a work suit. Like mine, white and gray, only older, with the
reflective strips peeling at the elbows, the way strips only get
on someone who wears the same suit for years and refuses to change it. It has its back to
me, against the ribbon, with its arms loose, open, swaying in a
wind that doesn't exist. It's still. Everything up here is still, and even so
that suit is stiller than everything.

I know those elbows.

I stop two meters away. The lifeline goes taut behind me. I don't want to get any closer,
and I get closer.

There's a patch on the left shoulder, a square of gray tape over the white
strip, that he put on himself three years ago, after scraping his shoulder on the edge of the
Post's hatch, and that I told him was crooked. *It's crooked*, I said. *It's
supposed to be crooked*, he said, *that way I know it's mine*.

It's crooked.

I reach out and turn the suit toward me, without effort, because it weighs nothing, and the suit
turns on the ribbon with a horrible lightness, as if it were empty.

It isn't empty.

Behind the visor, which is fogged on the inside, half white, half crystallized
at the edges, I see his face. The eyes closed. The gray beard he
never trimmed properly. The mouth a little open. The skin a gray that isn't a
person's color.

"Otávio," I say.

Inside my helmet, my voice is too loud.

---

"Iara, control. You're outside the car. Confirm?"

I don't answer.

"Iara. Control. Confirm your situation."

"Confirmed," I say. My voice comes from somewhere that isn't me. "The object on the ribbon is
a suit. With someone in it. It's Otávio."

Silence on the channel. A tenth of a second of light and then much more than that.

"Repeat."

"It's Otávio. Otávio Reis. He's dead."

I hear control breathe. I hear, behind it, another voice, and a chair scraping,
and someone saying *what do you mean, he was on leave, he went down to the mainland
on the Thursday ship*. And I think, with a part of my head that keeps
working on its own, like a machine nobody switched off: that's true. He said
he was going. He told everyone. A week of leave, the first in two years, on the
mainland, to see the sea from the other side. Nobody missed him because he
had told them he'd be gone.

"Iara. Don't touch anything. Go back to the car. We need to..."

"He's tied."

"What?"

I see it now. I hadn't seen it, because I was looking at his face. At the waist of the
suit, on the waist ring where we clip the lifeline, there's no
lifeline. I don't see a carabiner. There's a rope. A thin rope, a work rope, gray
fiber, the kind we carry coiled on the leg of the suit for emergencies. It
comes out of the ring at his waist, goes twice around the ribbon, over and under, and
comes back to the ring. And closes in a knot.

"He's tied to the ribbon," I say. "With rope. Not with the lifeline. With
rope."

"Iara, that doesn't..."

I stop listening.

---

I know that knot.

I don't know yet what it means. I know that I know it, the way you know a
letter before you know how to read. It's a bowline, the sailor's knot every technician
learns in the first month, because it doesn't slip and doesn't jam and comes undone with one hand.
But it has an extra turn. One turn on the inside of the loop, before it closes, that
nobody on the cable puts in, because you don't need it, because it's good for nothing.

Nobody. Only one.

*Tie it like you're coming back*, he used to say, in my first year, watching me get
the bowline wrong for the tenth time on the deck of the Anchor, with my hands burned by the
sun. And he'd put in the extra turn, slowly, so I could see. *That turn holds nothing.
It's so whoever comes to undo it knows it was you.*

I never really understood. I thought it was a quirk. Otávio had a lot of them.

I keep looking at the knot. A glove of an hour and ten, and I spend I don't know how many minutes
looking at a knot.

There's something else, and I only notice it because I'm a technician, and because the part of my head
that nobody switched off keeps doing math. A loose body doesn't stay up here. The
ribbon turns with the Earth, and at this height it turns too slowly to hold
a loose thing in orbit. If he had come loose, if he had slipped, if
someone had let him go, he would have gone. He would have fallen, slowly at first, then
faster and faster, toward that blue-and-white thing down there, and
at some point he would have entered the air and burned, and no one would ever have found anything.

He didn't go. He's here. Tied with a rope that isn't the lifeline,
with a knot that only he tied.

I don't know what that means. I know it means something.

---

The dosimeter on my chest goes from green to yellow. I didn't see the time pass.

"Iara." It's another voice now, on the channel. Deeper, older, one I've known
since I was twenty-two. It's Álvaro, the head of operations, who must have
come running into the control room, because he's out of breath. "Iara, it's Álvaro.
Listen to me. Go back to the car. Now. You're in the yellow."

"I can't leave him here."

"You won't. We'll come get him. I'll come up. But right now you go back.
Please."

I stay a moment longer. I put my hand, my glove, on top of his hand, his glove,
which is open, the fingers slightly curled, as if he had let go of something
not long ago. The two white gloves, one on top of the other, against the dark cable.

And then I remember.

I don't want to remember, and I remember, the way things come back when the body stops:
nine days ago, on an ordinary night, on the Anchor, my wrist buzzed with his name.
I was lying down. I'd come off a fourteen-hour shift on the cargo deck,
with my back aching and my head full of something else, and I saw his name on my wrist
and thought *tomorrow*. I thought: it's Otávio, it's something about the cable, or it's him wanting
to talk, and he'll talk for an hour, and I'm dead on my feet, I'll call tomorrow. And I turned
over and went to sleep.

The next day I forgot. The day after that, he went away on leave. That's what
everyone said.

I didn't call.

I take my hand off his glove. I pull on the lifeline. I go back down the ribbon to the
car, weighing nothing, with nothing holding me but a rope tied
to a box fastened to a ribbon fastened to a city that floats on a sea I can't
see from here.

Above me, a hundred and eighty meters below the Post, Otávio stays where he is.
Tied. Waiting for someone to come undo the knot and know it was him.
