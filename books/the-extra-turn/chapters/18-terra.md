On the Anchor, I weigh sixty-six kilos, and today I don't complain about any of them.

It's been a month since I came down. I came down with Álvaro in M-1, along the stopped ribbon, two days and
nineteen hours, with the panel saying *2.9 kg* at the start and *66 kg* at the end, and this time I
didn't cry at five thousand kilometers. I watched out the window as the sea became the sea, with the
clouds over it and the shadows of the clouds on the water, and the pale round spot in the middle of it,
with the thin line coming out of the center and rising toward me.

In the Well, when the hatch opened and the heat came in, I got up from the seat and stood.
My knees complained. I stood anyway.

---

The cable is still stopped.

Not all of it. The replacement of the stretch began two weeks after the clamp: a crew of eight, with
Álvaro at the Post and me in control, because control is what I'm allowed to do while
legal hasn't decided. The maintenance cars go up and down, one at a time, released by
hand, carrying new ribbon and tools and people. The cargo climbers, no. The ramp in the
launch bay has been empty for thirty-five days. C-114 is parked against the wall of the
cargo deck, with the lid of its niche open, because nobody has had the heart to seal it
again.

It's going to be four months. Maybe five. Forty meters of new ribbon, spliced above and
below the stretch, with the clamp holding everything in the meantime. After that, the climbers
come back. Two a day, at first. On the schedule. No extras. Hugo signed off on it.

And the sunshade waits.

Hugo did the math in public, at the circle three weeks ago, standing at the edge of the stopped
Well, in his light long-sleeved shirt, with the too-correct voice that I now know
is just his way of not shaking. Five months of the cable stopped. The shade up there, between
us and the Sun, losing what wears out, with nobody replacing it. Two percent less
cloud, more or less. Four hundredths of a degree, more or less, added to the world, in a year
when there shouldn't have been any. And, on the Anchor and along the whole coast of the Equator, more still
weeks than there would have been.

"People are going to die," Hugo said, at the circle. "I'm not going to pretend they won't. More than would
have died if the cable hadn't stopped. I know the numbers." He looked at me, from the other
side of the Well. "And I know the other number. The one for the ribbon on the ground along the Equator. That one is
bigger."

Nobody applauded. It isn't something you applaud. But the old man with the dried fish, who now has
two stalls, his and Mrs. Celeste's, went up to Hugo afterward and gave him a fish
wrapped in paper, and Hugo stood there holding the fish not knowing what to do, in his light
shirt, and I saw it, from the other side of the Well, and almost laughed.

---

Inês closes the case on a Thursday, in room 14.

She sends for me the way the agency does. A message, a time, no question. I go. She's
by the round window, in gray coveralls, with the sleeves rolled twice, and the
device the size of a pack of cards on the table. Through the window, the ribbon rises,
stopped, not singing.

"*Death from undetermined cause*," she says. She reads it from the device. "*During unauthorized activity,
at a hazardous location, with failure of personal protective equipment whose origin could not
be determined. Contributing factors: off-schedule exit; unlogged supplementary
traffic on the stretch; structural condition not detected by monitoring systems.*"
She turns off the device. "That's what I can write."

"That's what it is."

"That's what I know." She looks at me. "You taught me something, Iara. I thought separating
what we know from what we think was the end of the job. That after you separate them, what's left is
the truth." She almost smiles, and it's the first time I've seen her almost smile. "Sometimes
what's left is only the separation."

I don't say anything.

"The Operator's legal people have closed the inquiry into you," she says. "They said the
responsibility is *diffuse*." She says the word like someone holding something between two fingers.
"The *robotic prevails* rule was revoked last week. From now on, every manual measurement
that diverges from the robotic one goes to a person. A person reads it."

"Who?"

"The chief technician." Inês stands up. "Who I hear still hasn't accepted the post."

---

Álvaro offers me the post again that night, on the outer edge of the deck, where it's windy.

Not at the Post's kitchen window. Down here, at the edge of hull nineteen, outside
Otávio's apartment, where we went because neither of us wanted to go in yet. The
sea hitting the hulls. The wind in his gray hair. The ribbon rising from the middle of the Anchor,
back there, stopped, black against a sky that hasn't gone dark yet.

"The post is yours," he says. "Chief technician. I said so at the Post: whenever you want."

"I want it."

He turns to me, surprised.

"On two conditions," I say.

"Go on."

"The first: every manual measurement that diverges comes to me. Not to triage. To me. And I
read it. All of them. Even the wrong ones. Especially the wrong ones."

"The rule's already been revoked."

"I know. I'm saying it for me."

Álvaro almost laughs. He nods.

"The second: Bia comes back. To the technicians. Not to the deck. With me."

Álvaro goes quiet. Bia was fired the day she told Inês about the extras. The man who
paid on the side has vanished from the mainland. Mrs. Rute's cooler is still on the wall, paid off to the
end, and I didn't ask Bia where the money came from.

"She loaded the extras," Álvaro says, slowly.

"And she told. When nobody else was going to tell." I look at the ribbon. "He tied himself on for me to
find. Bia kept the notebook for me to find. Without her, I'd have had nothing."

Álvaro looks at the ribbon too. For a long time. The wind blows.

"All right," he says. "Both of them."

---

The burial is the next day, at sunset, on the outer edge.

On the Anchor there's no cemetery. There's no ground to put anyone in. We give people back to
the sea, the way the Anchor has done since it has existed: from the edge of the outermost hull, wrapped
in white cloth, tied to a long rope that goes down slowly from the edge to the water, and someone
lets go of the rope when the cloth touches the sea.

The agency released the body last week. He had been with the agency since the twentieth.
Three months. The white cloth is on a board at the edge of hull nineteen, and around
it, standing, with the wind blowing, is the circle.

Not the usual one. Bigger. Everyone Otávio trained in thirty-one years: the old
technicians I've known since I was a girl, the eighteen-year-old boy who couldn't finish his
sentence at the first circle, the ones from the cargo deck in orange coveralls. Álvaro, at the head of the
board, big, not moving. Bia, beside me, with Mrs. Rute on her arm. Hugo, a
little apart, in his light shirt, with the dried fish from three weeks ago still on his mind, I
think, because he looks at the old man from the stall and the old man waves. Inês, in gray coveralls,
behind everyone, with her sleeves rolled twice. Moisés, who came down from the Station on the
first car Laís released, for this. And the girl from P-7, eight years old, with her mother,
whom nobody invited and who came, and who stands looking at the white cloth with her forehead furrowed, the
way she looked at me through the car window.

Nobody speaks. It isn't a circle for speaking.

Álvaro looks at me.

---

The burial rope is an ordinary rope, thick, a deck rope. Someone has to tie it to the cloth,
at the waist, to lower it.

I take the gray rope off my wrist. The half-meter one. Flattened by two months of wheels, with
both ends cut by me on the twelfth, and the grip of his hands still pressed into the
turns he made around the ribbon, a hundred and eighty meters below the Post, at eleven-something on a
Thursday when he was supposed to be on a ship.

I don't know what time. I don't know from what. I'm not going to know.

I pass the gray rope under the cloth, at his waist, where the ring used to be. Two turns.
Tight. And I close it with a bowline, in my deck gloves, with my hands at one hundred percent,
which weigh what they weigh and shake a little.

And before I close the loop, I add the extra turn. On the inside. The one that holds nothing.

*Tie it like you're coming back. It's not for you. It's for whoever comes.*

Nobody is coming to undo this knot. The sea will undo it. But whoever is on the edge now, whoever
looks, will know two things: that it was Iara. And that it's here.

Then I tie the thick deck rope to the gray rope, with an ordinary knot, and I stand up.

---

Álvaro and I lower the rope together. Slowly. Hand over hand, like on the ribbon. The white
cloth goes down over the edge of hull nineteen, against the metal, and the wind makes it sway a
little, and the low sun, from the side, turns it orange for a moment. He weighs seventy kilos.
Here, he weighs seventy. My arms feel every one.

*The cable doesn't hold weight. It holds tension.*

I understand now, with the rope burning my hands, what I didn't understand in the cold room. It isn't
the weight you carry. Seventy kilos, three kilos, it's the same thing. It's what you hold
stretched between two things that pull. Hugo and Otávio. The heat and the ribbon. Álvaro and the
rule he wrote. Bia and her mother. Me and a call at twenty-three fourteen that
I didn't answer, and a forty-one-second voice saying *leave that with me*.

Nobody holds the weight. We hold the tension. And that's what holds.

The cloth touches the water.

Álvaro looks at me. I nod. He lets go of the rope.

---

Afterward, when the circle has gone and the sun has gone and the sea off the outer edge has turned black,
I go down to the lower ring of the Well, alone.

It's empty. Only the noise of the sea hitting the hulls, down below, and the ribbon rising from the anchor
ring and vanishing into the dark. The metal ring, thick as a tree trunk, fixed to the bottom of the
central hull.

I put my hand on it.

It's warm. And it vibrates. Not much. Something fine, continuous, that I feel more in the bones of my hand
than in the skin.

It's a maintenance car. Just one. The replacement car, going up with new ribbon to the Post, where
Álvaro will be tomorrow, released by hand by Laís, at three hundred an hour. I recognize the
song. High. Light. With the coming and going of something that climbs and is always a little behind
the piece of ribbon above it.

The cable sings again.

I close my eyes and keep my hand on the metal, listening, because someone has to listen.

I don't know how he died. I'm never going to know. Maybe he didn't know either, at the end.

What I know is what he wanted me to know.

And it's here, under my hand, climbing.
